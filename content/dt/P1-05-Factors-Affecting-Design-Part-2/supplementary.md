# Supplementary Materials

The read-along reference for this episode. Nothing here is spoken in the audio — it reinforces
the remaining six factors, obsolescence types, the life cycle analysis worked example and the
model answers.

### Listing 1 — Part 2 factors defined (affordable & safe / well made / sustainable)
| Factor | Definition | How to write about it |
|---|---|---|
| Finance | The money needed to take a product through every phase (R&D, materials, plant, manufacture, distribution, marketing) | Get the design right early (errors cost more later); funding via govt/entrepreneur/crowdfunding; **scarce funds limit quality & functionality** |
| Work health & safety (WHS) | The rights & responsibilities of employers and employees for a safe workplace | NSW **WHS Act 2011** (in force 1 Jan 2012); Safe Work Australia; designers are responsible for the safety of researchers, makers **and** users; partner of ergonomics |
| Quality | A measure of excellence, linked to **value and durability** | Tie to superior materials, workmanship, quality control, brand reputation; better quality usually costs more (links to finance) |
| Environmental consequences | The short- and long-term environmental impact of a design | **Short-term** = waste, energy use (lean production); **long-term** = greenhouse gases, microplastics, deforestation; **environmental vs financial values** tension |
| Obsolescence | When a product is replaced by something doing the same job | Three types — style, technological, planned (Listing 2) |
| Life cycle analysis | Evaluation of all resources/impacts across a product's whole life | Inputs & outputs, cradle-to-grave; the **frame for any environmental answer** (Listing 3) |

### Listing 2 — The three types of obsolescence
| Type | What it is | Memory hook | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Style / aesthetic | Still works, but a newer/more attractive version makes it feel dated | "works fine but feels old" | Last year's phone styling; fashion |
| Technological | An entire technology is superseded; product no longer supported | "can't get parts" | Cassette player after streaming |
| Planned / built-in | Deliberately designed to fail/become obsolete within a set time | "designed to die" | Non-replaceable battery, poor finishes, low-durability parts |

> **Technological = circumstance** (the world moved on). **Planned = intent** (designed in from
> the start). Don't blur them — that's the classic mark-loser. Planned obsolescence is the
> ethically loaded one ("discuss"/"evaluate" favourite).

### Listing 3 — Life cycle analysis (cradle-to-grave)
```text
Life cycle analysis = evaluate ALL inputs and outputs across a product's WHOLE life.

  INPUTS  (materials, resources, energy)
  OUTPUTS (pollution, waste)
  across stages:  Design -> Manufacture -> Distribution -> Use -> Disposal
                  ( cradle ----------------------------------------> grave )

Why: a product that looks clean in ONE stage may be costly in another. Judge the whole life.
Cradle-to-CRADLE = greener goal: design so end-of-life products are reused/recycled, not landfill.
```

### Listing 4 — Worked life cycle analysis: reusable steel bottle vs single-use plastic
| Stage | Reusable stainless-steel bottle | Single-use plastic bottle |
|---|---|---|
| Materials | Mined iron ore; energy-intensive — high upfront cost | Petroleum-based polymer; lower upfront energy, fossil-fuel input |
| Manufacture | Energy-intensive to form | Cheap, quick to mould |
| Distribution | Heavier → higher transport emissions per bottle | Light → low transport cost |
| Use | Used for years → displaces hundreds of single-use bottles | Used once |
| Disposal | Durable, recyclable | Landfill / microplastics lasting centuries |

> Verdict: steel = **higher short-term** impact to make, **far lower long-term** impact (reuse
> offsets it). Plastic = low impact per item, **catastrophic long-term** impact at scale. This
> stage-by-stage, short-vs-long comparison is exactly what the 2022 HSC drink-bottle question wanted.

### Listing 5 — Headline question: "Discuss the long-term environmental impacts of a design"
```text
WEAK answer (caps ~half marks):
  "It creates waste and pollution." -> vague, no stages, no timeframe, no judgement.

STRONG answer (band ~6):
  - FRAME with life cycle analysis (materials -> manufacture -> distribution -> use -> disposal).
  - SEPARATE short-term impacts from long-term ones.
  - NAME specific long-term harms (microplastics, greenhouse gases) + drivers (single-use,
    planned obsolescence).
  - JUDGE / weigh the impacts ("discuss" = both sides + a conclusion).
```

### Listing 6 — Model answers to the closing exam questions
```text
Q1 (2 marks) — Define life cycle analysis.
  Evaluation of all inputs (materials, energy) and outputs (pollution, waste) across a product's
  WHOLE life — design, manufacture, distribution, use, disposal (cradle to grave). Not one stage.

Q2 (3 marks) — Distinguish technological vs planned obsolescence.
  Technological = product outdated because the technology was superseded (cassette vs streaming).
  Planned = deliberately designed to fail/date within a period (non-replaceable battery) to force
  repeat sales. Key: circumstance vs intent.

Q3 (6 marks) — Discuss the long-term environmental impacts of a product.
  Frame with LCA. Single-use plastic: low per-item impact but severe long-term harm (landfill,
  microplastics centuries, fossil fuels). Reusable steel: high short-term (mining/energy) but low
  long-term (reuse displaces hundreds; recyclable). Weighed judgement: single-use design is the
  bigger long-term harm; durable/reusable reduces it at higher upfront cost.

Q4 (3 marks) — Tension between environmental and financial factors.
  Sustainable choices (renewable/recyclable materials, durable, lean production) usually cost
  more -> competing environmental-vs-financial values -> designer must balance the environmental
  benefit against cost and affordability. Design is a trade-off.
```
