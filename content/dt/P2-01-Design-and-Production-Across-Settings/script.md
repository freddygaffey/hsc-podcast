NARRATOR: Welcome to module two, Design Processes and Production. If module one was about how a designer thinks, this module is about how things actually get made, in the real world, at real scale. Let's reset where we just were, because today builds straight on it. We just finished module one with the review, where the big idea was that design is one connected system: a designer runs the design process while weighing the factors. The process is DRIPER, Define, Research, Ideate, Produce, Evaluate, Refine, and it's cyclic, you loop back from evaluation until the design meets its criteria. And there are eleven factors affecting design, tagged FFA EQ NEW LOAd. Now, in module one, episode three, when we taught the design process, I made one promise I'm cashing in today. I said the same process runs across different settings, domestic, community, industrial and commercial, and what changes is the scale, not the process. That throwaway line is now today's entire home topic.

NARRATOR: Today's topic is design and production across the four settings. The syllabus dot-point names them precisely: design and production processes in domestic, community, industrial and commercial settings. And the skill it wants is to compare and contrast the processes and technologies used in design projects with those used in industrial and commercial settings. So this is a comparison topic, and comparison questions are exactly where you earn or lose marks. By the end of today you'll be able to define all four settings, explain what varies between them and what stays the same, and write a strong comparison of any two. Let me give you the four settings, and the memory hook, first.

NARRATOR: The four settings, in order of increasing scale and formality, are Domestic, Community, Industrial and Commercial. The hook is simply the first letters in that order: DCIC. Picture it as a ladder, you climb from the smallest, most informal setting, someone making something at home, up to the largest, most formal, a company mass-producing for a market. DCIC. Domestic, Community, Industrial, Commercial. They're defined in Listing 1, and that ladder image, small and informal at the bottom, large and formal at the top, is the thing to hold, because the differences between settings are really just differences in where you sit on that ladder.

NARRATOR: First setting, domestic. A domestic setting is design and production based in a home or a small workshop, usually small-scale or one-off production, using simple tools and techniques to keep costs low, with products sold at local markets or made to specific client orders. The textbook gives a lovely real example: Faye Cahill, a cake designer, who began producing from home, working independently to meet client demand. Hold that example, because it also shows the ladder in action, as her sales grew, she had to expand and employ others, climbing from a purely domestic operation toward something larger. Domestic is the individual, the kitchen-table start-up, the maker in the garage. Small scale, simple tools, low cost.

NARRATOR: Second, community. A community setting is where a small community or interest group forms a company or cooperative, a co-op, for the common good of local people. And here's the defining feature that the exam loves, and that students miss: a community setting is not primarily intent on making a profit. Its aim is to provide a service or product for the community, like creating local jobs or growing food. The textbook's example is community gardens, where locals grow and harvest their own produce, partly to cut down on food miles, the carbon cost of transporting food from farm to consumer. So community is the co-op, the not-for-profit, the local-benefit setting. If you remember one thing about community settings, remember that profit is not the main goal, because that single feature distinguishes it from all three of the others.

NARRATOR: Third, industrial. An industrial setting is defined by large-scale production of goods. It involves a large number of people in the production process, a variety of manufacturing techniques, and large-scale machinery and tools. Because of the scale, products are more economically viable to manufacture, the cost per item drops, and industry has more funds to afford sophisticated manufacturing processes. Industrial is the factory: big, mechanised, mass-producing, capital-intensive. The making, at scale.

NARRATOR: Fourth, commercial. Commercial is closely tied to industrial, the syllabus often pairs them, but there's a distinction worth keeping. Where industrial emphasises the large-scale making of goods, commercial emphasises the business of selling them, design and production driven by a market, a target customer, and the goal of profit. A commercial setting designs products specifically to sell competitively in the marketplace, so market research and marketing loom large. In practice industrial and commercial overlap heavily, a company often does both, manufactures at industrial scale and operates commercially to sell, but in an answer it's worth signalling that you know industrial leans on the making and commercial leans on the selling. That distinction is in Listing 3.

NARRATOR: Now the single most important idea in this episode, the one that turns a list of four settings into a real understanding, and it's the promise from module one. The design process itself is the same in all four settings. Whether it's Faye Cahill icing a cake at home or a factory stamping out a thousand units an hour, both run DRIPER: define the need, research, ideate, produce, evaluate, refine. What changes between the settings is not the process, it's a set of variables. The textbook names them: the scale of production, the size of the company, the plant size, and the manufacturing techniques adopted. Add to those the level of formality, the division of labour, how many people are involved, and how much documentation and quality control there is. So the difference between a domestic and an industrial setting is the same design process run at wildly different scale, resources and formality. That contrast, same process, different variables, is in Listing 2, and it's the backbone of every answer in this topic.

QUESTION: Quick retrieval, because this is the exact thing the exam tests. What stays the same across all four settings, and what changes? Pause the player, answer both, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. What stays the same is the design process itself, all four settings define a need, research, generate ideas, produce, evaluate and refine. What changes is the scale of production, the size of the operation, the resources and machinery available, the division of labour, the formality, and the amount of documentation and quality control. If you said "the process is the same but the scale changes", that's the core mark right there. Don't fall for the trap of thinking a factory uses a fundamentally different process from a home maker, it doesn't, it uses the same process with far more resources and rigour.

NARRATOR: Let me make this concrete by taking one product and making it three ways, because seeing the same thing across settings is how the comparison clicks. The full version is in Listing 4. Take jam. In a domestic setting, one person makes a few jars in their kitchen, simple equipment, one-off batches, sold at a local market or to order, low volume, the maker does every step. In a community setting, a local co-op, maybe a community garden group, makes jam together to raise funds or use up local fruit, not chasing profit but a community benefit, modest scale, shared labour. In an industrial setting, a food factory mass-produces thousands of identical jars on an automated line, specialised machinery, division of labour where each worker or machine does one step, rigorous quality control and documentation, low cost per jar. Same product, same underlying process, define a recipe, source fruit, produce, test, refine, but utterly different scale, resources, labour and formality. That one example, jam made three ways, will answer almost any comparison question this topic throws at you.

NARRATOR: Now the headline skill, the comparison, because the dot-point is "compare and contrast". The question shape is: "Compare the design and production processes used in two different settings." A weak answer says "a factory is bigger than a home", which is true but earns almost nothing, it's one vague difference with no real contrast. A strong answer picks two named settings and contrasts them across several clear dimensions: scale of production, division of labour, equipment and technology, level of documentation and quality control, and the motive, with a concrete example for each setting. For instance, contrasting domestic and industrial: domestic is small-scale and one-off, one person doing every step with basic tools and informal checking, whereas industrial is large-scale and mass-produced, with a divided workforce, specialised machinery, and rigorous documented quality control. Same structure, several dimensions, named examples. The weak-versus-strong contrast is in Listing 5. Comparison questions reward structure, pick your dimensions and contrast both settings on each one.

Second, forward to the very next episode, P2-02, where we look at the scales of production, one-off, batch and mass, which map closely onto these settings, domestic tends to one-off, community to small batch, industrial to mass. Fourth, cross-link to project management, a topic coming in module three: the reason industrial settings need formal management of action, time and finance is exactly because of their scale, the bigger the setting, the more management it demands. And one more, the community setting's not-for-profit, common-good motive links forward to the social and ethical issues we'll hit later in this module, design isn't always about profit.

NARRATOR: One trap to flag. Students treat "industrial" and "commercial" as identical and use them interchangeably, or worse, forget the community setting's defining feature. Keep two things straight: community settings are distinguished by being for the common good, not primarily for profit, and while industrial and commercial overlap, industrial emphasises large-scale making and commercial emphasises selling in a market. Naming those distinctions, rather than blurring all four into "big versus small", is what separates a strong answer.

Three: the design process, DRIPER, is the same in all four, what changes is the scale, company size, resources, division of labour, formality and quality control. QUESTION: Question one. Identify the four settings in which design and production take place. Two marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The four settings are domestic, community, industrial and commercial. Two marks for naming all four correctly, DCIC. A quick way to be safe: pair each with a one-word tag, domestic the home, community the co-op, industrial the factory, commercial the market. That tagging protects you if a follow-up asks you to describe one.

QUESTION: Question two. Explain what distinguishes a community setting from the other three. Two marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A community setting is distinguished by its motive: it is run by a community group or cooperative for the common good of local people, such as creating jobs or providing fresh produce, rather than primarily to make a profit. For example, a community garden grows food locally to benefit residents and cut food miles. Two marks: identify the not-for-profit, common-good motive as the distinguishing feature, and support it with an example. The trap is describing community by its scale, the marker wants the motive, because that's what actually sets it apart from domestic, industrial and commercial.

QUESTION: Question three. Compare the design and production processes used in a domestic setting with those used in an industrial setting. Five marks. This is the headline comparison, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer, and notice it contrasts across several dimensions rather than making one vague point. Both settings follow the same design process, defining a need, researching, generating ideas, producing, evaluating and refining, but they differ markedly in execution. In scale, a domestic setting produces small quantities or one-off items, whereas an industrial setting mass-produces large quantities. In labour, a domestic maker typically performs every step themselves, whereas an industrial setting uses a divided workforce where each person or machine performs a specialised task. In equipment, domestic production uses simple, basic tools to keep costs low, whereas industrial production uses large-scale, sophisticated machinery. And in quality control and documentation, domestic checking is informal, whereas industrial production uses rigorous, documented quality control because a fault repeated across mass production is costly. For example, a person baking a few cakes at home versus a factory producing thousands of identical packaged cakes. Five marks: state that the process is shared, then contrast across scale, labour, equipment and quality control, with an example. The weak answer, "a factory is bigger", makes one point and caps low; the strong answer is structured across dimensions.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain why the design process remains the same across different settings while the scale of production differs. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The design process remains the same because every setting must still define a need, research, generate and develop ideas, produce a solution, and evaluate and refine it, this sequence is fundamental to creating any successful design regardless of where it happens. What differs is the scale and the resources, the size of the operation, the workforce, the machinery and the funds available, which determine how many units are produced and how formally. So the same process is simply executed with more people, equipment, documentation and rigour as the setting grows from domestic to industrial. Three marks: state that the process is fundamental and universal, that scale and resources are what differ, and explain that the setting changes the execution, not the process itself. This is the central insight of the whole topic.

QUESTION: Question five. Contrast the aims of a community setting with those of a commercial setting. Three marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer, and this one tests whether you really understand the motives. A commercial setting designs and produces goods primarily to make a profit by selling competitively in a market, so its decisions are driven by what will sell, market research, marketing and cost-effectiveness. A community setting, by contrast, is run by a community group or cooperative for the common good of local people, so its aim is to provide a benefit such as local jobs, fresh produce or a needed service, rather than to maximise profit. For example, a commercial bakery chain produces to sell at scale and return a profit, whereas a community garden produces food to benefit residents and reduce food miles. Three marks: state the commercial aim, profit through the market, state the community aim, common good not profit, and contrast them, ideally with an example each. The trap is treating all production as profit-seeking, the community setting is the deliberate exception, and saying so is the mark.
