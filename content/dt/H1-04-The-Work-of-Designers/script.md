NARRATOR: Today's home topic is the work of designers and emulating their practice. The syllabus dot-point is the work of designers, their design practice and the processes they use, and the skill is to emulate, where appropriate, the practices and processes used by designers to assist in the development of the major design project. That phrase, emulate where appropriate, is the whole episode. By the end you'll be able to study a designer's process and explain how you'd borrow it for your own project, which is exactly what H1.2 asks. Let me start with what you're studying.

NARRATOR: What you study is a designer's practice and process, how they actually work. The textbook's central idea, from module one, is that every designer runs a version of the design process, adapted to suit their needs. The example it features is Marc Newson, whose success, it says, lies in his flexibility and adaptability to different design situations, using the design process as a tool, and his courage to try new and unfamiliar materials. So Newson's practice isn't a particular product, it's a way of working, adaptable, willing to learn new skills, applying one process across furniture, products and aircraft interiors. That's the kind of thing you study: not what they made, but how and why they work as they do, in Listing 1.

NARRATOR: How do you actually study a designer? The textbook is practical, in Listing 2. You examine their process through case studies and in-depth interviews, you look for what makes their work unique and the problems they overcame, and, importantly, you study designers who work in a context similar to the one you're pursuing for your MDP, a furniture maker if you're making furniture. You can even contact a professional designer directly, by email or phone, to learn how they work. The point is to understand their process deeply enough to learn from it.

NARRATOR: Now the heart of H1.2: emulate, where appropriate. To emulate is to adopt their approach in your own work, and the crucial word is appropriate, you borrow the practices that genuinely help your project, not blindly copy everything. And this is the distinction that earns the marks, the same one from module one: you emulate their process, not their product. You don't copy what Newson built, you adopt how he works, his adaptability, his willingness to try unfamiliar materials, his disciplined use of the design process. If you studied James Dyson, you'd emulate his iterative prototyping, build, test, learn, refine, thousands of times, not build a vacuum cleaner. Emulate the method, not the artefact, that's in Listing 3, and it's the line between a band-six answer and a biography.

QUESTION: Retrieval. What does it mean to emulate a designer's practice, and what exactly do you emulate? Pause the player, answer both, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. To emulate a designer's practice means to adopt their way of working in your own project, where it's appropriate and helpful. What you emulate is their process and practices, how they research, prototype, iterate, test and make decisions, not their actual product. For example, you might emulate a designer's habit of building multiple prototypes and user-testing each one, applying that discipline to your own MDP, without copying anything they designed. If you said "adopt their process or method, not their product", that's the mark, and it's exactly what separates H1.2 from simply describing a designer.

NARRATOR: Let me make it concrete, in Listing 4, with a designer's process broken into stages you could borrow. Take James Dyson's practice, the one we met in module one. His process: identify a frustration with existing products, the vacuum that lost suction; research the underlying principle, cyclonic separation; then build prototype after prototype, famously thousands, testing and refining each against the problem, before finalising. Now emulate it in your MDP: you'd adopt his iterative prototyping, committing to build and test several versions of your solution rather than one, evaluating each against your criteria and refining, and you'd document each iteration in your folio as evidence. You haven't copied Dyson's vacuum, you've copied his disciplined, iterative method and applied it to your own need. That, written up with your own examples, is a model H1.2 answer.

NARRATOR: Now the headline question: "Relate the practices and processes of a designer you have studied to your major design project." Here's the move. A weak answer is biography, "Marc Newson is a famous Australian designer who designed the Lockheed Lounge and the Qantas Skybed", a list of who they are and what they made, which caps low because it never relates them to your project. A strong answer identifies a specific practice or process, explains it, and shows how you adopted it in your own MDP, with evidence: "Newson adapts the design process flexibly across unfamiliar materials; I emulated this by researching and testing a material I had never used, glass-reinforced plastic, rather than defaulting to timber, as documented in my folio." See the difference, the weak answer describes the designer, the strong answer relates their method to your work. The contrast is in Listing 5. Relate, don't recount.

That's description, and against the verb relate or emulate it caps low. The marks are in connecting the designer's process to your own project, what you learned from how they work and how you applied it. Always pivot from "they did X" to "so in my project I did X too, here's the evidence".

QUESTION: Question one. What does it mean to emulate the practices of a designer? Two marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. To emulate the practices of a designer means to adopt their methods and processes, the way they research, prototype, test and make decisions, in your own design work, where it is appropriate and beneficial, rather than copying the actual products they have made. Two marks: capture that you adopt their process or method, and that it's where appropriate, not a copy of their product. The trap is thinking emulate means copy their design.

QUESTION: Question two. Describe how you could study the practices and processes of a designer. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. You could study a designer by examining case studies and documentaries of their work to understand their process, conducting or reading in-depth interviews in which they describe how they work and the problems they overcame, and analysing their products to infer the practices behind them. Ideally you would study a designer working in a field similar to your own major design project, and you could even contact a professional designer directly to ask about their methods. Three marks: name case studies or documentaries, interviews, and studying a designer in a relevant field or contacting them. The marker wants real research methods, not "look them up".

QUESTION: Question three. Relate the practices and processes of a designer you have studied to your major design project. Five marks. This is the headline question, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer, using James Dyson and an example MDP. James Dyson's practice is defined by iterative prototyping: he identifies a flaw in existing products, researches the underlying principle, and then builds and tests many prototypes, refining each against the problem before finalising the design. I related this to my major design project by emulating his iterative process rather than committing to a single solution: I built three successive prototypes of my product, evaluating each against my success criteria and refining the design in response, for example moving from a screw fixing to a clip after testing showed it was faster to assemble, and I documented each iteration in my folio. By adopting Dyson's disciplined, iterative method, not his product, I improved my own design through evidence-based refinement. Five marks: identify the designer's specific practice, explain it, and relate it to your project with a concrete emulated example and evidence. Biography alone caps low; the relating is the skill.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain why studying the work of designers is valuable when developing a major design project. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Studying the work of designers is valuable because professional designers have developed effective, proven practices and processes that a student can learn from and emulate to improve their own project. By examining how designers research, prototype, test and adapt, for example Newson's flexibility with unfamiliar materials or Dyson's iterative prototyping, the student can adopt disciplined methods that lead to a better, more rigorously developed solution, rather than relying only on their own limited experience. It also exposes the student to materials, techniques and approaches they might not otherwise consider. Three marks: state that designers have proven practices worth emulating, that studying them improves the student's own process, and that it broadens their methods and ideas. The value is in the transferable method, not admiration of the designer.
