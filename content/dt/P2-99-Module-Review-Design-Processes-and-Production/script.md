NARRATOR: Let me re-surface the key terms topic by topic, fast, the way you'd need them, they're in Listing 3. Settings: the design process is the same in all four, what changes is scale, resources, formality and quality control, and community is distinguished by being for the common good, not profit. Scales: job is one-off and flexible with high cost per unit; batch is limited runs with jigs and templates, flexible between runs; mass is large, standardised and interchangeable, lowest cost per unit through economies of scale but high tooling cost. Materials: characteristics are what a material is like, properties are how it behaves, and you select by matching properties to function. Safety: a hazard is a potential source of harm, a risk is the likelihood and severity, and you control risk through the hierarchy with PPE last. Impact: assess across three levels, individual, society and environment, with sustainability meaning present needs without compromising the future and equity meaning fair access for all. Technology: five applications, with CAD linking through CAM to making, and simulation replacing physical prototypes.

NARRATOR: Now the connections between the topics, interleaving at full scale, because the integrated answers are where the marks are. Connection one: setting and scale are linked but distinct, domestic settings tend toward job production, industrial settings toward mass, but they're different questions, where and by whom versus how many. Connection two: material selection sits at the centre of everything, you choose a material for its function, but also for the scale, you injection-mould a polymer for mass production, hand-work timber for a one-off, for safety, some materials are hazardous, and for sustainability, the environmental criterion. So a material choice answers to scale, safety and impact all at once. Connection three: safety and impact overlap, the safety and health of workers and users is both a work-health-and-safety matter and one of the seven social issues. Connection four: technology threads through all of it, CAD models the design, simulation tests it safely, CAM makes it at scale. The whole module is one connected production system, and saying so is what lifts an answer.

QUESTION: A connection question, the kind the exam rewards. How does the choice of production scale influence the choice of material and manufacturing process? Pause the player, answer in a few sentences, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The production scale strongly influences material and process choice because a material and technique must suit the quantity being made. For mass production, a designer chooses materials and processes suited to high-volume, automated, standardised manufacture, such as a polymer that can be injection-moulded quickly and cheaply, achieving economies of scale. For one-off or job production, the designer can use materials and hand or rapid-prototyping techniques suited to a single unique item, such as timber worked by hand or a 3D-printed part, where flexibility matters more than per-unit cost. So scale, material and process are selected together, not independently. That linking of scale to material and process is exactly the integrated thinking the top band rewards.

NARRATOR: Let me walk the traps, because the review's most useful job is stopping you losing marks on the boundaries. They're in Listing 4. Trap one, the headline: confusing production setting with production scale. The setting is where and by whom, domestic, community, industrial, commercial; the scale is how many, job, batch, mass. They're related but independent axes, a domestic workshop usually does job production, but you can batch-produce in a domestic setting too. Don't merge them. Trap two: confusing batch and mass, batch is a limited, defined, flexible run, mass is large, continuous, standardised and costly to change. Trap three: hazard versus risk, the hazard is the thing, the risk is the likelihood and severity. Trap four: answering a safety question with PPE first, PPE is the last resort, work the hierarchy from the top, eliminate before you protect. Trap five: defining sustainability as vague "being green", it's meeting present needs without compromising the future. And trap six: blurring characteristics and properties, what a material is like versus how it behaves. Six traps, six boundaries the exam will probe.

QUESTION: Question one, the big integrated one. For a product you have studied, analyse how the choice of production setting, materials and processes affected its impact on society and the environment. Six marks. This fuses four topics. Pause the player and write a full answer before you play, and the worked integrated answer is in Listing 6.

NARRATOR: Model answer, using a mass-produced single-use plastic water bottle, and watch it thread the topics together. The bottle is made in an industrial setting using mass production, which allows millions of identical units to be produced cheaply through standardised, automated injection moulding, achieving economies of scale. This choice of setting and scale drove the material selection: a cheap, lightweight, mouldable polymer suited to high-volume manufacture. However, these choices have significant impacts. Socially, the low cost makes the product widely accessible, a benefit, but it encourages a throwaway culture. Environmentally, the scale multiplies the harm, billions of single-use polymer bottles create enormous waste, microplastic pollution and fossil-fuel use, which a life cycle analysis exposes as a severe long-term impact. So the industrial, mass-production, cheap-polymer choices that made the bottle affordable are the same choices that drive its environmental damage at scale. Six marks: the marks come from genuinely linking setting and scale to material choice, and then to social and environmental impact, with a judgement. An answer that treats each topic separately caps in the middle.

QUESTION: Question two. Compare design and production in a domestic setting with an industrial setting, referring to scale, resources and quality control. Five marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Both settings follow the same design process, but differ in execution. In scale, a domestic setting produces one-off or small quantities, whereas an industrial setting mass-produces large quantities. In resources, a domestic maker uses basic tools and limited funds and often performs every step personally, whereas an industrial setting uses large-scale machinery, a divided specialised workforce and substantial capital. In quality control, domestic checking is informal, whereas industrial production uses rigorous, documented quality control because a fault repeated across mass production is costly. For example, a person hand-making a few chairs at home versus a factory producing thousands of identical chairs. Five marks: state the process is shared, then contrast scale, resources and quality control with an example. The trap is one vague point like "factories are bigger".

QUESTION: Question three. Explain how a designer would select and safely use a material for a product, referring to selection criteria and the hierarchy of control. Six marks. This fuses materials and safety. Pause and write a full answer.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A designer selects a material by matching its properties to the product's function, then weighing the other criteria, summarised as CPA FACE, characteristics, properties, availability, function, appearance, cost and environmental impact, ideally supported by experimentation to test how the material behaves. For example, stainless steel might be chosen for a reusable bottle because its strength, durability and corrosion resistance suit the function, despite a higher cost. Using the material safely is then managed through risk assessment and the hierarchy of control: the designer first tries to eliminate hazards, for example buying material pre-cut, then substitutes safer options, applies engineering controls such as guards and extraction, administrative controls such as training and safe procedures, and only finally personal protective equipment. So selection is property-to-function plus criteria, and safe use works down the hierarchy with PPE last. Six marks: cover material selection by properties and criteria, then safe use through the hierarchy of control, ideally with an example. This links two whole topics, which is what the question rewards.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain why personal protective equipment is considered the least effective control in the hierarchy of control. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Personal protective equipment is the least effective control because it does not remove or reduce the hazard itself, it only reduces the harm to the individual wearing it, and only when it is worn correctly and consistently. Higher controls are more effective because they act on the hazard rather than the person: eliminating the hazard removes the risk for everyone, and substituting or engineering controls reduce it at the source regardless of individual behaviour. Therefore PPE is used as a last line of defence after higher controls have been applied. Three marks: state PPE only protects the wearer and doesn't remove the hazard, contrast with higher controls acting on the hazard for everyone, and conclude it's the last resort. The reasoning, acts on the person not the hazard, is the mark.

QUESTION: Question five. Discuss how computer-based technologies have changed the way designers work. Five marks. Pause and answer.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Computer-based technologies have transformed design across the five applications, modelling, research, simulation and graphics, communication and presentation. CAD modelling lets designers build and modify three-dimensional designs on screen and test them without time-consuming physical model-making, while simulation allows a design to be tested under real conditions virtually, identifying problems before any material is used and saving time, cost and waste. Communication technologies enable remote collaboration on shared models, overcoming barriers of distance, and CAD links to CAM and three-D printing so a digital model can directly drive manufacture. However, designers must discriminate, choosing the appropriate technology for each task rather than using computers indiscriminately. Overall, computer-based technologies have made design faster, more accurate, more collaborative and more easily tested. Five marks: cover several applications with their benefits, mention the CAD-to-CAM link and the need to discriminate, and reach an overall judgement. Naming specific capabilities, not just "computers help", is what scores.

QUESTION: Last one, question six, a quick trap-buster. Distinguish between a production setting and a production scale. Two marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A production setting refers to where and by whom a product is made, domestic, community, industrial or commercial, whereas a production scale refers to how many units are made, job or one-off, batch, or mass production. They are related but independent, for example a domestic setting usually involves job production but could also do small batch production. Two marks: define setting as where and by whom and scale as how many, and note they are distinct. This is the headline trap of the module, and the distinction, where versus how many, is the mark.

QUESTION: Question seven. Assess the environmental impact of choosing mass production for a product, referring to sustainability. Five marks. Pause the player and write a full answer before you play, and the model answer is in Listing 7.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Mass production has a mixed environmental impact that should be assessed against sustainability, meeting present needs without compromising the future. On one hand, mass production can be efficient per unit, and standardised processes can be optimised to reduce waste and energy use per item. On the other hand, the sheer scale multiplies environmental impact: producing millions of units consumes large quantities of raw materials and energy, and if the product is cheap and short-lived it drives a throwaway culture, generating enormous waste, as a life cycle analysis across materials, manufacture, distribution, use and disposal reveals. Whether mass production is sustainable therefore depends on the product's durability and end-of-life: durable, recyclable mass-produced goods can be reasonably sustainable, whereas cheap single-use ones are not. Five marks: assess both the per-unit efficiency and the scale-multiplied harm, frame it with sustainability and life cycle analysis, and reach a conditional judgement. The mark is in weighing both sides against the future-resources definition.

QUESTION: Question eight. A designer must produce two hundred identical wooden toys for a local market. Recommend a scale of production and justify your choice. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The most appropriate scale is batch production, because two hundred identical units is a limited, defined quantity, too many to make efficiently one at a time as job production, but not enough to justify the expensive tooling of mass production. Batch production suits this perfectly: jigs and templates allow the two hundred toys to be reproduced consistently and efficiently without new tooling for each one, keeping costs reasonable, and the design can still be varied for a future batch. Three marks: identify batch production, and justify it by the limited defined quantity and the use of jigs and templates, contrasting it with job being too slow and mass being unjustified at this volume. Matching the scale to the quantity, with a reason, is the skill.
