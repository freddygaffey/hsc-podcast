NARRATOR: Hazard versus risk. They sound similar and they're not the same, and the distinction is examinable. A hazard is anything with the potential to cause harm, a spinning saw blade, a toxic chemical, a hot soldering iron, loud machinery. A risk is the likelihood that the hazard actually causes harm, combined with how serious that harm would be. So the saw blade is the hazard; the risk is how likely you are to be cut and how badly. The point of the distinction is that you manage safety by first identifying hazards, then assessing the risk each one poses, then controlling it. Hazard is the thing; risk is the chance and severity of it hurting someone. They're defined in Listing 1.

NARRATOR: Now the legislative side, because the syllabus says legislative requirements, and naming the law earns marks. In New South Wales, workplace safety is governed by the Work Health and Safety Act of twenty eleven, which commenced on the first of January twenty twelve and replaced the older Occupational Health and Safety Act. The national regulator is Safe Work Australia. The law sets out rights and responsibilities for both employers and employees, and this two-sided structure is what the exam wants. Employers must provide a safe workplace, protective equipment, training, well-maintained machinery, and good lighting and ventilation. Employees have the right to that safe environment, and the responsibility to follow safety procedures, wear their protective equipment, and not use machinery they're untrained on. For you as a student, the textbook makes the point directly: the workshop is your workplace, and those rules exist to protect you. Rights and responsibilities, both sides, under the WHS Act. That's in Listing 2.

NARRATOR: So how do you actually keep a workplace safe? Through a process called risk assessment, and it has clear steps: identify the hazards, assess the risk of each, control the risk, and then review whether the control worked. The heart of it is that third step, control, and there's a specific tool for it that you must know, because it's the most examinable thing in this whole topic: the hierarchy of control. The hierarchy ranks ways of controlling a risk from most effective to least effective, and we'll remember it with the hook ES-EAP. E, Eliminate: remove the hazard entirely, the best option, if you can design out the dangerous step, do. S, Substitute: replace the hazard with something safer, swap a toxic solvent for a water-based one. E, Engineering controls: physically isolate or guard the hazard, a blade guard, a fume extraction system, a barrier. A, Administrative controls: change how people work to reduce exposure, training, signage, safe operating procedures, rotating tasks. And P, PPE, personal protective equipment: the gear worn on the body, safety glasses, gloves, ear muffs, dust masks. ES-EAP, and the order matters, because here's the single most important point.

NARRATOR: PPE is the last resort, not the first. Students reach for "wear safety glasses" as their whole safety answer, and that's exactly backwards. The hierarchy is ranked by effectiveness: eliminating a hazard protects everyone completely, whereas PPE only protects the one person wearing it correctly, and it fails the moment someone forgets it or wears it wrong. So PPE sits at the bottom, used only after you've eliminated, substituted, engineered and administered as much risk as you can. As the textbook puts it, PPE works best as part of a proper risk assessment, not relied on alone. If an exam asks how to control a hazard and you only say "wear goggles", you've shown you don't understand the hierarchy. The full hierarchy is in Listing 3, and PPE-is-the-last-resort is the marking point.

QUESTION: Retrieval, because this is the highest-value idea in the topic. What is the hierarchy of control, and why is PPE ranked last? Pause the player, answer both, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. The hierarchy of control ranks risk controls from most to least effective: eliminate the hazard, substitute it with something safer, use engineering controls like guards, use administrative controls like training and procedures, and finally personal protective equipment. PPE is ranked last because it only protects the individual wearing it and only if it's worn correctly, whereas higher controls like elimination remove the hazard for everyone, so PPE is the least reliable and used only after higher controls have been applied. If you got the five levels and "PPE protects only the wearer, elimination protects everyone", that's the full mark. ES-EAP, top to bottom.

NARRATOR: A few practical tools the syllabus and textbook expect you to know, all in Listing 4. Personal protective equipment, PPE, is anything worn to minimise risk to a worker's health and safety, common examples are safety glasses, gloves, ear muffs, hard hats, dust masks, overalls and covered footwear. For chemicals, there are material safety data sheets, MSDS, which suppliers must provide, giving the safe use, storage, disposal and first-aid information for a substance, reading the MSDS is part of safe practice. And workplaces use safe operating procedures, sometimes called safe work procedures, step-by-step instructions for using a machine or process safely. For your major design project, the textbook says your safety research and a safety plan should go in your folio, and if something is unsafe to make in a school workshop, you rethink the construction or outsource that part. Safety is documented and planned, not improvised.

NARRATOR: Now the designer's angle, because this is what lifts work health and safety from a workshop rule to a design principle. A designer has a duty of care on several levels, the safety of the people researching and manufacturing the product, and the safety of the end-user. And the principle that ties it together is that safety should be designed in, not bolted on. This connects straight back to ergonomics from module one, designing a product to fit the user so it doesn't strain or injure them is designing safety in. The best control is always elimination, and at the design stage you can sometimes eliminate a hazard from the product or its manufacture entirely, by choosing a safer material, a safer joining method, or a simpler form. So the strongest answer treats safety not as protective gear at the end, but as a consideration running through the whole design process. That's the principle underlying safe working practices.

NARRATOR: Let me make it concrete with a worked risk assessment, because applying the hierarchy to a real hazard is exactly what the exam tests. It's in Listing 5. Say you're cutting timber on a bandsaw in the workshop. Identify the hazard: the moving blade can cause serious cuts, and the process produces wood dust, a respiratory hazard. Assess the risk: contact with the blade is moderately likely without controls and the harm is severe, so it's a high risk. Now control it using the hierarchy, top down. Eliminate, can you avoid the cut, maybe buy the timber pre-cut to size, removing the hazard. If not, substitute, use a safer tool such as a hand saw or a guided machine for the task. Engineering controls, fit a blade guard and connect dust extraction. Administrative controls, train the user, display a safe operating procedure, supervise. And only then, PPE, safety glasses and a dust mask. Notice the answer worked down the hierarchy and reached PPE last. That structure, hazard, risk, then controls top-down, is a complete risk-assessment answer.

QUESTION: Question three. Explain the principles underlying safe working practices and environments. Five marks. This is the headline question, and the same wording appears in the HSC, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Safe working practices are based on several principles. First, the legal principle of duty of care under work health and safety legislation, such as the Work Health and Safety Act, which gives both employers and employees defined rights and responsibilities to maintain a safe environment. Second, the principle of risk management: hazards must be identified, the risks they pose assessed, and then controlled, following the hierarchy of control, which prioritises eliminating or substituting a hazard over merely protecting the worker with PPE. Third, the principle that safety should be designed in rather than added on, a designer should anticipate hazards in both the manufacture and the use of a product and reduce them at the design stage, for example by choosing safer materials or applying ergonomic principles. QUESTION: Question four. A student needs to use a hot soldering iron to assemble a circuit. Conduct a basic risk assessment for this task. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The hazards are the hot soldering iron tip, which can cause burns, and the fumes produced by molten solder, which can be inhaled.
