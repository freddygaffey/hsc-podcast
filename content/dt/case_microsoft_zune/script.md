NARRATOR: In 2006, the most powerful technology company on Earth launched a music player to take down the iPod. They had a dominant computing platform, billions of dollars, a track record of hardware hits, and even an endorsement from a rising young senator named Barack Obama. The product was called the Zune. Five years later it was dead, having never once climbed above about two percent of the market. This is the story of the Microsoft Zune — not a story about bad technology, but about what happens when a good-enough product is pushed through a broken design process into a market that's already moved on.

NARRATOR: You have to understand how strong Microsoft's hand looked. This was the era of Windows XP, which ran on almost every computer in the world. Microsoft had made hardware before and won — the Microsoft Mouse back in the nineties, and more recently the Xbox, which was a genuine hit. They'd launched MSN Music in 2004 to take on Apple's iTunes. So when they decided to build their own iPod competitor, nobody thought it was crazy. In November 2006, with a lot of fanfare, they released the first device — the Zune 30. Thirty gigabytes of storage, an FM radio, a three-inch screen, in black, brown, or white.

QUESTION: So with all of Microsoft's power behind it, why did almost nobody buy a Zune?

NARRATOR: Let's start with the cruel maths, because it sets the scene. The Zune never got past single digits — around two percent of the market. The iPod, meanwhile, had roughly three quarters of all music-player sales worldwide. Microsoft kept trying. In 2007 they brought out the Zune 80, plus a smaller Zune 4 and Zune 8 to go after the iPod nano. And in 2009 they made what was, by most accounts, genuinely their best device — the Zune HD, their first touchscreen model. It got good reviews. And it still didn't sell. By October 2011, Microsoft pulled the plug.

NARRATOR: Now here's the part that makes the Zune such a useful case study: the Zune HD was not a bad product. Some people actually preferred it to the iPod Touch. It had a distinctive, almost industrial look — a brushed-metal back panel pinned on with four prominent screws, deliberately less flashy than Apple's. It had a bold, original interface built around bright text instead of Apple's little illustrated icons, and the navigation was quick and slick, with an animated zoom whenever you tapped into something. It even had a clever wireless feature that let you share music with another Zune owner — although, thanks to copyright restrictions, a shared song could only be played three times over three days. The hardware and the design were, honestly, competitive.

NARRATOR: So if the product was good, the failure has to be somewhere else. And it was — in two places the syllabus cares about a lot. The first is the design process, specifically the marketing. The marketing team never got a clear picture of what the Zune actually was or who it was for. When a design process lacks clarity at the top, the people downstream can't do their jobs — and that's exactly what happened. The Zune's advertising was artsy and aimed at a very specific, narrow audience, and it completely missed the broad mass of ordinary music listeners who were the people actually buying iPods. They marketed a mainstream product as if it were a niche one.

NARRATOR: The second failure was about who they brought into the process — or didn't. To really succeed, Microsoft needed the music industry on side. They needed to convince record labels that having a real competitor to Apple was good for everyone, that it gave the labels more leverage. But Microsoft didn't build those relationships properly. They neglected the music industry — and as a result, a lot of features that could have been negotiated were simply blocked. A good design process means involving the right stakeholders early. Microsoft left a key one out.

QUESTION: And there's a third thing, bigger than either of those. Pause for a second — what was happening to music players in general by 2009?

NARRATOR: Smartphones. That's the one Microsoft couldn't out-design. The whole category of standalone music players was quietly dying, because people were starting to carry phones that played music, took photos, and did everything else too. To win, Microsoft didn't just need a good player — they needed something that made the iPod look old-fashioned, and the Zune never did that. For most buyers there was simply no reason to choose a Zune unless you were firmly in the anti-Apple camp. And on top of all of it, Apple's marketing power at that point was just an overwhelming wall to climb. There was even a famous embarrassment: in December 2008, a batch of the first Zune 30 players all froze on the same day — a software bug that couldn't handle the fact that 2008 was a leap year. Not the headline you want.

NARRATOR: So the Zune is a different kind of failure from the Edsel or the Newton. The technology was fine. The design was fine — arguably good. What killed it was a fuzzy design process that produced confused marketing aimed at the wrong audience, a failure to bring the right partners in, and the bad luck and bad timing of fighting for a market that smartphones were about to eat whole, against a competitor with unbeatable momentum.

NARRATOR: That's the lesson to carry out of this one. A product can be well made and still fail if the *process* around it is broken — if the marketing has no clear target, if the right stakeholders aren't involved, and if it's late to a market that's already shifting. Being good isn't enough; you have to be clearly better, clearly aimed, and on time. We'll tie that back to the design-process content, by name, in a later lesson.
