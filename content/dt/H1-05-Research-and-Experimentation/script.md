NARRATOR: Today's home topic is research and experimentation for the MDP. The syllabus dot-point is research and methods of experimentation to generate ideas, and the skills are to respond to the findings of experimentation and research, and to experiment with materials, tools and technologies when designing. Notice the verb respond, that's the whole point, research only matters if you act on it. By the end you'll be able to research and experiment to develop your project, and crucially, show how the findings changed your design. Let me start with research.

NARRATOR: Research for your MDP applies the full toolkit from module three, QISOTS, questionnaires, interviews, surveys, observation, tests and experiments, and statistical analysis, plus information research from print and electronic sources. The areas of investigation you listed in your proposal tell you what to research, materials, users, existing products, techniques. You use both primary research, gathered yourself, like surveying your target users, and secondary research, from existing sources, like material specifications. That's all in Listing 1, and it's module three's content turned on your own real project.

NARRATOR: Now experimentation, which the textbook calls a practical, primary research task, in Listing 2. Experimentation is hands-on testing of materials, tools and technologies, and it does two jobs, not one. It generates ideas, by experimenting with a material you discover what it can do, what shapes it takes, how it joins, sparking design possibilities you wouldn't have imagined on paper. And it tests ideas, you trial a joint, a finish, a mechanism, to see if it actually works. So experimentation isn't just confirming a decision you've already made, it's an engine for generating and evaluating ideas, the design process's research and ideate stages made physical. Experiment with materials, tools and technologies, and let the results suggest and test your design.

NARRATOR: And here's the skill that earns the marks, the verb the syllabus chose: respond to the findings. The textbook is explicit, you analyse and evaluate the results of your tests and apply them to your MDP, and you should show how you modified your design in response to the findings of research and testing. Research and experimentation have value only when they change a decision. If you test two materials and one performs better, you use it, and you document that you did, and why. This is the evaluate-and-refine loop of DRIPER: research and experiment, evaluate the findings, refine the design, go round again. A folio full of research that never changed the design scores poorly; research that visibly drove decisions scores well. Responding to findings is in Listing 3.

QUESTION: Retrieval. What two jobs does experimentation do, and what's the most important thing you must do with your findings? Pause the player, answer both, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. Experimentation does two jobs: it generates ideas, by revealing what materials, tools and technologies can do, and it tests ideas, by trialling whether something actually works. And the most important thing you must do with the findings is respond to them, analyse and evaluate the results and apply them by modifying your design, so the research actually shapes the project. If you said "generate and test ideas, then respond to and act on the findings", that's the mark. The trap is gathering research and never using it.

NARRATOR: Now the headline question: "Describe how research and experimentation informed the development of your major design project." Here's the move. A weak answer says "I researched materials online and did some experiments", vague, with no findings and no consequences, capping low. A strong answer names the specific research and experiments, states what they found, and shows how the design changed in response: "I experimented with three joining methods for the frame, testing each for strength; the mortise-and-tenon joint outperformed the others, so I selected it for the load-bearing joints and redesigned the corner detail accordingly, as documented in my folio." See the difference, the weak answer lists activity, the strong answer shows method, finding, and a design decision that responded to it. The contrast is in Listing 5. Method, finding, response, every time.

NARRATOR: Let me make it concrete with a model experiment, the kind of folio page that scores, in Listing 4. Suppose you're choosing a material for a chair seat. The aim: find the most suitable material for strength and comfort. The method, controlled like a real experiment: test three materials, plywood, solid timber, and a polymer, by loading each identically and recording deflection and any failure, keeping the test fair by using the same size and load. The result: the plywood held the load with least deflection and no cracking. The decision, the response: select plywood for the seat, and note the evidence. That's experimentation done as primary research, an aim, a fair method, a result, and a design decision that responds to it. Write your experiments that way and they're worth real marks, both in the folio and as an exam example.

NARRATOR: One trap to flag. Students treat research and experimentation as boxes to fill, pages of material printouts, a few tests, with no link to the design. That misses the verb respond. Every piece of research or experimentation in your folio should end with "and so I decided", a decision it drove. Research that doesn't change anything is decoration; research that changes a decision is design.

NARRATOR: Now the exam-style questions, real NESA format, get a pen. Four questions, model answers in Listing 6.

QUESTION: Question one. Explain how experimentation can be used to generate and test ideas. Three marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Experimentation generates ideas because hands-on trials with materials, tools and technologies reveal what they can do, their properties, how they form and join, suggesting design possibilities a designer would not discover on paper. It tests ideas because trialling a specific element, such as a joint, finish or mechanism, shows whether it actually performs as intended before it is committed to the final design. So experimentation both sparks new design directions and verifies them with real evidence. Three marks: explain the generate role, the test role, and that it provides real evidence. The marker wants both functions, not just "you test things".

QUESTION: Question two. Why is it important to respond to the findings of research and testing? Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. It is important to respond to findings because research and testing only add value to a design when they actually influence decisions. By analysing and evaluating the results and modifying the design accordingly, the designer ensures the solution is based on evidence rather than assumption, problems revealed by testing are corrected, and better options identified by research are adopted, this is the evaluate-and-refine loop that progressively improves the design. Research that is gathered but ignored does nothing to improve the project. Three marks: state that findings must influence decisions, that responding makes the design evidence-based and corrects problems, and that ignored research adds no value. The verb respond is the mark.

QUESTION: Question three. Describe how research and experimentation informed the development of a design project. Five marks. This is the headline question, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer, using an example MDP. To develop my project I used both research and experimentation. Secondary research into material specifications and primary research, surveying my target users, identified the requirements, and I then experimented to choose between options: for the load-bearing frame I tested three joining methods, loading identical samples and recording strength and deflection. The mortise-and-tenon joint performed best, so I responded to this finding by selecting it for all load-bearing joints and redesigning the corner detail to suit, documenting the experiment and decision in my folio. Similarly, user feedback that the first prototype was too low led me to raise the working height. In each case, the research and experimentation produced findings that I analysed and applied, directly shaping the design. Five marks: name specific research and experimentation, state the findings, and show the design decisions made in response, with evidence. Listing activity without findings or responses caps low.

QUESTION: Question four. Distinguish between primary and secondary research in the context of developing a major design project. Two marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Primary research is data the designer gathers first-hand for the project, such as surveying target users or conducting material experiments and tests, and it is current and specific to the project. Secondary research is data obtained from existing sources others have produced, such as material specifications, standards or published studies, and it is faster to access but more general. Both are used to develop the MDP, with experimentation being a key form of primary research. Two marks: define each with a project-relevant example, ideally noting experimentation as primary research. This links straight back to module three's research methods.
