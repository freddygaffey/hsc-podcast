NARRATOR: Today's home topic is evaluation. The syllabus lists three things: developing and refining ideas, criteria for evaluation, and methods of evaluation. And the skills are pointed: establish criteria for evaluation, conduct continual evaluation throughout design and production, and test and evaluate the appropriateness of design solutions. Notice that word continual, it's the key to the whole topic. Let me start with what you evaluate against.

NARRATOR: You evaluate a design against its criteria for success, the very criteria set in the brief, functional and aesthetic. Evaluation is the process of determining how successfully those criteria are met, as set out in Listing 1. The textbook gives a simple, powerful method: build a criteria checklist, a table with each criterion in the left column and how you'll measure success in the right column, then tick off each criterion as you check it. That table turns a vague "is it good?" into an objective "did it meet criterion one, two, three?" It's in Listing 2, and it's why measurable criteria from the brief matter so much, you can only evaluate against a criterion you can measure.

NARRATOR: Now the heart of the topic, and the thing students get wrong: evaluation is continual, conducted throughout design and production, not just at the end. The textbook says keep your eye on the basic criteria and amend the materials or methods as required, in other words, you evaluate as you go, and when something isn't meeting a criterion, you fix it then and there. Think of it in three phases, the hook is BMA, in Listing 3. Before: you establish the criteria, at the brief stage, so you know what success looks like. Mid: you evaluate continually during design and production, testing prototypes, checking against criteria, and refining, catching problems while they're still cheap to fix. After: you make a final judgement of the finished product against all the criteria. Before, mid, after, but the mid is the part that's easy to forget and worth the most marks. Continual evaluation is the Evaluate-and-Refine loop of DRIPER running over and over.

NARRATOR: The third syllabus point is the methods of evaluation, the ways you actually gather evidence, in Listing 4. Testing the product, does it physically perform, the load test, the leak test, links to last episode's testing. Checking against the criteria checklist. Getting feedback from the client, does it meet their need. And surveying consumers or users, do the people it's for find it works. The textbook adds a practical point worth a mark: keep evidence, if it took three test runs to get a joint right, keep samples of the failed runs as proof of your evaluation. Evidence of evaluation is exactly what a folio and a marker want to see.

NARRATOR: And evaluation isn't only about the product, it's also about developing and refining ideas, the first syllabus point. You evaluate your ideas during the ideate stage, weighing options against criteria, perhaps with a decision matrix, that's a cognitive organiser from the creativity episode, and you refine the chosen idea. So evaluation runs from judging early ideas, through monitoring production, to the final verdict. It's the quality-control thread through the whole process.

QUESTION: Retrieval. Why is evaluation described as continual, and what are the three phases? Pause the player, answer both, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. Evaluation is continual because it's conducted throughout design and production, not just at the end, you test and check against the criteria as you go and amend when something falls short. The three phases are: before, establishing the criteria at the brief stage; mid, evaluating continually during design and production, testing and refining; and after, the final judgement of the finished product against the criteria. Before, mid, after, BMA. If you got "continual means throughout, not just the end" and the three phases, that's the mark. The mid phase is the one that matters most and the one students forget.

NARRATOR: Now the headline question: "Explain why evaluation should be ongoing throughout a design project." Here's the move. A weak answer says "so you can check at the end if it worked", which actually contradicts the point and caps low. A strong answer explains the benefits of continual evaluation: it catches problems early, when they're cheap and easy to fix rather than after the product is built; it drives the refine loop, each evaluation feeds an improvement, so the design gets progressively better; it ensures the project stays aligned to the brief's criteria throughout; and it produces an evidence trail of testing and decisions that demonstrates a rigorous process, which markers reward. A top answer gives an example, a mid-project leak test failing and triggering a lid redesign. The weak-versus-strong contrast is in Listing 6. The whole answer hinges on "early and continual beats once at the end".

NARRATOR: Let me make it concrete, in Listing 5, a before-mid-after evaluation of a reusable drink bottle against three criteria: must not leak, must keep a drink cold for six hours, must cost under twenty-five dollars. Before: those measurable criteria are set in the brief. Mid: during production you leak-test the first prototype, it fails when inverted, so you evaluate, identify the lid as the problem, and refine to a sealed flip lid, you also cost the build mid-way and find it's tracking over budget, so you substitute a cheaper material. After: the finished bottle is tested against all three criteria, it holds, stays cold for seven hours, and costs twenty-two dollars, so you judge it a success on all three, with the evidence of your failed first lid test in the folio. Notice the mid-project evaluation changed two decisions, the lid and the material, before they became expensive mistakes. That's why continual evaluation matters.

NARRATOR: Now the exam-style questions, real NESA format, get a pen. Four questions, model answers in Listing 7.

QUESTION: Question one. What does a designer evaluate a design against, and how? Two marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A designer evaluates a design against the criteria for success established in the design brief, both functional and aesthetic, by checking how well each criterion has been met, often using a criteria checklist that lists each criterion alongside how its success will be measured, supported by testing, client feedback or consumer surveys. Two marks: state that you evaluate against the brief's criteria, and name how, the checklist and methods. The marker wants the link to the brief's criteria, not a vague "see if it's good".

QUESTION: Question two. Identify three methods a designer could use to evaluate a design. Two marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Any three of: testing the product against its functional criteria, for example a leak or load test; checking the design against a criteria checklist; obtaining feedback from the client; and surveying the consumers or users it is intended for. Two marks for three valid methods. Note the overlap with research methods, testing and surveys are research tools used here to evaluate.

QUESTION: Question three. Explain why evaluation should be conducted throughout a design project rather than only at the end. Five marks. This is the headline question, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Evaluation should be continual because it allows problems to be identified early, while they are still cheap and easy to correct, rather than after the product is built, when fixing them is costly or impossible. Continual evaluation drives the refine loop of the design process: each evaluation against the criteria feeds an improvement, so the design develops progressively toward success. It also keeps the project aligned with the criteria set in the brief throughout, ensuring the final solution actually meets the identified need, and it produces an evidence trail of tests and decisions that demonstrates a rigorous design process. For example, a mid-project leak test that fails can trigger a lid redesign before mass production, avoiding a costly fault. By contrast, evaluating only at the end can reveal failures too late to fix. Five marks: explain early problem-detection, the refine loop, alignment to the brief, and the evidence trail, ideally with an example. The contrast with end-only evaluation seals it.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain the relationship between the design brief and the evaluation of a project. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The design brief and the evaluation are directly linked, they are bookends of the project. The brief establishes the criteria for success at the start, defining what the finished design must achieve, and the evaluation, both during and at the end, judges the design against exactly those criteria. This is why criteria in the brief must be measurable: they are the standard the evaluation tests against. A project is judged successful if it meets the criteria its own brief set. Three marks: state that the brief sets the criteria and the evaluation judges against them, that they are linked start-to-end, and that measurable criteria enable objective evaluation. This bookend relationship is a high-value insight that ties the module together.
