NARRATOR: Let's reset. Last episode we covered the scales of production, JBM, Job, Batch and Mass, one, some, millions, and the trade-off between volume, cost per unit and flexibility. Before that, the four settings, DCIC, domestic, community, industrial and commercial, and the idea that the same design process runs at every scale. And back in module one, the eleven factors affecting design, FFA EQ NEW LOAd, and the design process DRIPER. Today we go a level deeper, into the workshop, and answer a question every one of those scales and settings depends on: what do you actually make the thing out of? Choosing materials, tools and techniques is where a design either physically works or physically fails, and it's where a lot of folio and exam marks live.

NARRATOR: Today's home topic is using materials, tools, techniques and other resources. The syllabus lists what it wants you to understand: the characteristics and properties of resources, their functions and uses, experimentation, the criteria for selection, and the consequences of use. And the skill, this is the important one, is to select appropriate materials and then justify and explain that selection. Notice the verb, justify. This topic isn't about knowing lots of materials, it's about choosing the right one for a reason and being able to defend that choice. By the end you'll be able to distinguish characteristics from properties, apply a set of selection criteria, and write a justification that earns marks. Let me start with a distinction students get fuzzy on.

NARRATOR: Characteristics versus properties. These two words get used loosely, but keeping them apart sharpens your answers. A material's characteristics are its descriptive qualities, what it is like, its appearance, colour, texture, the kind of material it is. A material's properties are how it behaves and performs, measurable things like strength, hardness, durability, flexibility, electrical and thermal conductivity, and workability, how easily it can be cut, shaped and joined. Characteristics describe; properties perform. They're set out in Listing 1, with the main property categories. And one property worth defining precisely, because the textbook does and the exam uses it: durability is the ability of a product to repeatedly perform its designed function for an acceptable period without failing. Durable means long-lasting under real use. When you describe a material, give its properties, not just "it's strong", say it has high tensile strength, or good workability, or it's an electrical insulator.

NARRATOR: Now the central principle of the whole topic, the one idea everything else hangs off: you select a material by matching its properties to the product's function. That's it. The function tells you which properties matter, and then you choose a material that has them. A product that must conduct electricity needs a material with the property of conductivity, so you choose a metal like copper, not a polymer. A product that must be light and waterproof, like a drink bottle, points you toward a polymer or aluminium, not timber. So before you can choose a material, you have to know what the product must do, which is exactly why function, one of our eleven factors, drives material selection. Properties to function. That mapping is in Listing 3, and it's the spine of every justification.

NARRATOR: Of course function isn't the only thing that matters, so the textbook gives a set of selection criteria, and we'll hold them with the hook CPA FACE: Characteristics, Properties, Availability, Function, Appearance, Cost, Environmental. Let me unpack them, they're in Listing 2. Characteristics and properties, we've covered, what the material is and how it behaves. Availability, can you actually get it, and what happens to your timeline if the supplier runs out. Function, does it suit what the product must do. Appearance, colour and texture, the aesthetic fit, linking to the aesthetics factor. Cost, the price of the material and the processes to work it, linking to finance. And environmental, the energy, waste, production impact and recyclability, linking to the environmental factors and life cycle analysis. Beyond CPA FACE, the textbook flags two more serious considerations: the ethical supply of resources, think conflict or blood diamonds, black-market materials, child labour in the supply chain, and broader supply-chain issues like country of origin and transport distance. So a thorough material selection weighs CPA FACE plus ethics and supply chain.

NARRATOR: How does a designer actually decide, rather than guess? Through experimentation, which is one of the syllabus words in this dot-point. The textbook lists research methods for selecting resources: studying existing designs, asking experts, accessing secondary research, studying characteristics and properties, and crucially, experimenting, manipulating materials and practising techniques to see how they behave. So you don't just read that a timber is workable, you cut and join a sample and find out. You test materials against the criteria, often recording the comparison in a table, before committing. This is the design process in action, research and ideate feed into producing a prototype, you evaluate the material's performance, and refine your choice, which is why prototyping with different materials is so valuable. Experimentation is how material selection becomes evidence-based instead of a guess, and saying so in an answer is worth marks.

NARRATOR: The last syllabus word here is consequences of use, and it's a forward-looking one. Every material choice has consequences beyond "does it work today". There's the environmental consequence, the waste, energy and recyclability across the product's life, straight back to last module's life cycle analysis. There's the safety consequence, some materials are hazardous to work or dispose of, which leads straight into next episode on work health and safety. And there's the practical consequence, a material that's hard to obtain or work with delays the whole project. A good designer chooses with the consequences in mind, not just the immediate fit.

NARRATOR: One thing to widen before we test you, because the dot-point says materials, tools, techniques and other resources, not just materials. Resources is the broad word, and it covers more than the stuff a product is made of. Tools and equipment are resources, and they're selected the same way, by what the job needs, you choose the right saw, machine or 3D printer for the technique and the scale. Techniques themselves are resources, the skills and processes for working a material, and the textbook notes you select techniques you can actually do, or learn, within your time. And other resources include your time, your finance, your own and others' expertise, and even information. So when an exam says "select and justify resources", it doesn't only mean materials, it means the whole toolkit, materials, tools, equipment, techniques, time, money and skills, each chosen and justified against the need. A common, easy mark is to acknowledge that the right material is useless if you don't have the tools or the skill to work it.

QUESTION: Retrieval. What's the difference between a material's characteristics and its properties, and what's the single most important thing a property must match? Pause the player, answer both, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. Characteristics are a material's descriptive qualities, what it's like, such as appearance and texture, while properties are how it behaves and performs, such as strength, durability, conductivity and workability. And the single most important thing a material's properties must match is the product's function, you choose the material whose properties suit what the product has to do. If you said "characteristics describe, properties perform, and properties must match the function", that's the heart of the topic. Most material-selection marks flow from that one idea.

NARRATOR: Let me make it concrete with a comparison, because the exam likes you to weigh materials against each other. Listing 4 is a properties table for three common material families, picking one product to design: say a simple chair. Timber, like pine, has moderate strength, good workability, an attractive natural appearance, low density so it's fairly light, around five hundred kilograms per cubic metre, and it's renewable, but it can warp and isn't waterproof. A polymer, like polypropylene, is light, around nine hundred kilograms per cubic metre, easily moulded so it suits mass production, weather-resistant and a good electrical insulator, but it's petroleum-based and not biodegradable. A metal, like mild steel, is very strong and durable, conducts heat and electricity, but it's heavy, around seven thousand eight hundred kilograms per cubic metre, can corrode, and is energy-intensive to produce. For a mass-produced outdoor chair you might choose the polymer, light, weatherproof, mouldable for mass production; for a premium indoor chair, timber, for its appearance and workability; for a heavy-duty industrial frame, steel, for strength. Same product category, three materials, and the choice follows the properties matched to the specific function and context. That's the reasoning a justification needs.

NARRATOR: Now the headline skill: "Justify the selection of materials for a design project you have studied." This is where folio and exam marks are won and lost, so here's the move. A weak answer says "I used pine because it was cheap and available", which gives one or two criteria with no real reasoning, and caps low. A strong answer justifies the choice against several criteria and, above all, links the material's properties to the product's function, with evidence from testing. For example: pine was selected because its properties, sufficient strength for the load and excellent workability, suited the function of a lightweight hand-assembled stool; this was confirmed by testing a sample joint; its renewable sourcing satisfied the environmental criterion; and its low cost suited the budget. See the difference, the weak answer states a choice, the strong answer defends it against criteria, leads with properties matched to function, and cites evidence. The contrast is in Listing 5. Justify means give reasons, ideally backed by testing, not just name the material.

First, back to module one's factors: material selection is where several factors become concrete decisions, function tells you which properties you need, finance constrains the cost, aesthetics shapes the appearance choice, and the environmental factors plus life cycle analysis judge the consequences of use. Selecting a material is the eleven factors applied to one decision. Second, forward to the very next episode, P2-04, on work health and safety, because the consequences of using a material include how safely it can be worked and disposed of. NARRATOR: One trap to flag. Students name a material and stop, or give only cost and availability. Cost and availability are real criteria, but a justification that never connects the material's properties to the product's function is missing the core mark. Always lead with properties matched to function, then add the other criteria. Naming is not justifying.

QUESTION: Question one. Distinguish between the characteristics and the properties of a material. Two marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A material's characteristics are its descriptive qualities, what it is like, such as its appearance, colour and texture, whereas its properties are how it behaves and performs, such as its strength, durability, electrical conductivity and workability. Two marks: define characteristics as descriptive qualities and properties as performance or behaviour, with an example of each. The trap is treating the two words as interchangeable, the marker wants the distinction, what it is like versus how it behaves.

QUESTION: Question two. Identify four criteria a designer should consider when selecting a material. Two marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Any four valid criteria, for example: the material's properties, such as strength and durability; its suitability for the product's function; its cost; its availability; its appearance; or its environmental impact and recyclability. Two marks for four sensible criteria. Use CPA FACE as your checklist so you never run dry, and including environmental impact shows breadth. A small upgrade: mention ethical supply or supply-chain issues to show you think beyond the obvious.

QUESTION: Question three. Justify the selection of a material for a product you have studied. Five marks. This is the headline question, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer, using a stainless-steel reusable drink bottle. Stainless steel was selected because its properties directly suit the bottle's function: it is strong and highly durable, so it withstands repeated daily use and impacts without failing; it is corrosion-resistant and food-safe, essential for holding drinks; and it can be formed into a double wall for insulation. These property-to-function matches were the primary reason for the choice. In addition, although stainless steel has a higher cost and a higher upfront environmental impact in manufacture than plastic, this is justified because its durability gives a long life that, on a life cycle analysis, displaces many single-use bottles, satisfying the environmental criterion. Its appearance also conveys a premium quality suited to the target market. Five marks: lead with properties matched to function, address several criteria including cost and environmental impact, and reach an overall justification. The weak answer, "steel because it's strong", names one property and stops, the strong answer defends the choice across criteria and ties it to function and life cycle.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain why experimentation is important when selecting materials. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Experimentation is important because it allows a designer to test how a material actually behaves under real conditions, rather than relying only on assumptions or secondary information. By manipulating materials and practising techniques, for example testing a joint's strength or how a material cuts and finishes, the designer gathers reliable evidence about the material's properties and suitability for the function, which informs and justifies the final selection and reduces the risk of failure later in production. Three marks: state that experimentation tests real behaviour, that it provides evidence about properties and suitability, and that it informs and justifies selection while reducing risk. This links material selection to the research and evaluate stages of the design process.

QUESTION: Question five. Explain how the consequences of using a material should influence its selection. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The consequences of using a material extend beyond whether it performs its function, and a responsible designer weighs them when selecting it. Environmental consequences, the energy used, waste produced, and whether the material can be recycled, affect the product's impact across its whole life, assessed through a life cycle analysis. Safety consequences matter because some materials are hazardous to work with or dispose of, creating work-health-and-safety risks. And practical consequences, such as a material that is difficult to source or to work, can delay or compromise the project. A designer should therefore select a material whose consequences of use are acceptable, not just one that fits the immediate function. Three marks: identify environmental, safety and practical consequences, and explain that selection should account for them, not only performance. This links directly to the environmental factors and to next episode's work health and safety.
