QUESTION: Question one, the big integrated one. Explain how identifying the need, applying the factors affecting design, and researching ideas shaped the early development of a design project. Six marks. This fuses three episodes. Pause the player and write a full answer before you play, and the worked answer is in Listing 5.

NARRATOR: Model answer, using an example MDP. Early development began by identifying and exploring a need through a needs analysis, confirming a genuine gap, which was formulated into a proposal stating the need, the areas of investigation, and measurable criteria for success. The factors affecting design then shaped the criteria and decisions: function, ergonomics and finance were translated into specific, measurable targets the design had to meet. Research and experimentation developed the project on evidence: secondary research and primary experiments, such as testing materials and joints, generated and tested ideas, and the findings were responded to by refining the design, for example selecting the strongest joint after testing. In this way the need defined the target, the factors set the standards, and research provided the evidence, together launching the project on a sound, justified basis. Six marks: link the need and proposal, the factors and criteria, and the research and evidence, showing they worked together. Describing one in isolation caps in the middle.

QUESTION: Question two. Explain the meta-skill of justifying rather than narrating, with an example. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Narrating describes what was done, for example "I chose aluminium and bent it into a frame", which provides no reasoning and earns little. Justifying explains why, linking the decision to a factor affecting design and supporting it with evidence, for example "aluminium was selected because its high strength-to-weight ratio suited the function of a lightweight portable frame, as confirmed by a load test in my folio". The pattern is decision, reason, factor, evidence, and it earns the higher bands in both the folio and the written exam because it demonstrates a reasoned, evidence-based process. Three marks: define narrate and justify, give a contrasting example, and name the decision-reason-factor-evidence pattern.

QUESTION: Question three. Explain the relationship between a project's success criteria and the factors affecting design. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The success criteria set in a project proposal are essentially the factors affecting design made specific and measurable for that particular project. For example, the general factor of function becomes a measurable criterion such as "must support one hundred kilograms", and the environmental factor becomes "must use recyclable materials". So the factors provide the categories a designer must consider, and the criteria turn the relevant ones into testable targets that the design is developed and later evaluated against. Three marks: state that criteria are the factors made specific and measurable, give an example, and note the criteria are what the design is evaluated against. This links the proposal to both the factors and the final evaluation.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain how studying the work of a designer can assist the development of a major design project. Three marks. Pause the player, answer, then play, the model answer is in Listing 6.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Studying a designer's work assists development because the student can examine the designer's proven practices and processes, how they research, prototype, test and adapt, and emulate those methods in their own project where appropriate. For example, adopting a designer's iterative prototyping or their flexibility with unfamiliar materials gives the student a disciplined, tested approach rather than relying on their own limited experience, and exposes them to techniques and materials they might not otherwise consider. The key is emulating the designer's method, not copying their product. Three marks: state that you learn and emulate proven processes, give an example practice, and note it improves and broadens the student's own method. The mark is in method, not biography.

QUESTION: Question five. Explain why responding to research and experimentation findings is essential during early development. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Responding to findings is essential because research and experimentation only add value when they actually change decisions. During early development, the designer analyses and evaluates the results, a material test, a user survey, and modifies the design accordingly, selecting a stronger material or adjusting a dimension, so the project is built on evidence rather than assumption. This is the evaluate-and-refine loop, and it also produces the documented evidence that justifies decisions in the folio. Research gathered but never acted on adds nothing and wastes time. Three marks: state that findings must change decisions, that responding makes the design evidence-based and corrects problems early, and that it produces justification evidence. The verb respond is the mark.
