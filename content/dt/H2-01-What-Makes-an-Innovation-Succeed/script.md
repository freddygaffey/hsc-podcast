NARRATOR: Welcome to module H2, Innovation and Emerging Technologies. If H1, the major design project, is most of your school-based mark, H2 is the written-exam powerhouse: Section one, multiple choice, and especially Section three, the extended response, lean heavily on innovation. So this module is where you build the essay. Let's reset the bridge from H1. Back in the success-and-failure episode, H1.1, you analysed designs using the eleven factors affecting design, FFA EQ NEW LOAd, and you met the case studies, the Flow Hive, the Edsel, the Newton, the Zune, Central Park. Today we meet a different, smaller set of factors, the six that decide whether an innovation succeeds, and right up front: do not confuse these two lists. The eleven are the design factors. Today's six are the innovation factors. Both get examined, often in the same paper.

NARRATOR: Today's home topic is the factors that influence the success of an innovation, outcome H3.1. The syllabus lists them: timing, available and emerging technologies, historical and cultural, political, economic and legal factors, and marketing strategies. And the skill is to differentiate between the factors that contribute to the success or failure of innovations. This is the spine of nearly every Section three answer, so by the end you'll have all six cold, with a worked example, and know how to use them to analyse success or failure. Let me give you the six and the hook.

NARRATOR: The six factors spell TEMPLE, and the line to remember is, an innovation succeeds inside a TEMPLE. They're in Listing 1. T, Timing: launching at the right moment, when society is ready. E, Emerging and available technologies: the technologies that make the innovation possible. M, Marketing strategies: how it's promoted and sold. P, Political factors: government support, regulation, the political climate. L, Legal and economic factors: patents and intellectual property, the cost and the economic conditions. And E, Era: the historical and cultural influences of the time. Timing, emerging tech, marketing, political, legal-economic, era. TEMPLE. Let me anchor each in one real innovation, because a worked example makes the six stick.

NARRATOR: The flagship success is the Flow Hive, a beehive that lets you harvest honey by turning a tap, without opening the hive or disturbing the bees, invented by Australians Cedar and Stuart Anderson. Run it through TEMPLE, the grid is in Listing 4. Timing: it launched in the middle of the save-the-bees movement, when public concern for bees was peaking, perfect timing. Emerging and available technologies: complex plastic honeycomb frames were only manufacturable thanks to three-D printing for prototyping and injection moulding for production. Marketing strategies: a brilliant crowdfunding campaign with a compelling video and a large social-media following turned the launch into a global event, raising over twelve million dollars, a crowdfunding record. Political: relatively little government support, which they worked around. Legal and economic: they filed a patent before crowdfunding, protecting the idea from copycats, a crucial legal move. And era: it tapped a cultural shift toward sustainability and home food production, against the centuries-old tradition of smoking bees. Six factors, all aligned, equals a runaway success.

NARRATOR: Now the skill, differentiating success and failure, in Listing 3. The power of TEMPLE is that the same six factors explain both: an innovation succeeds when the factors align in its favour, and fails when one or more go against it. The Flow Hive succeeded because timing, technology, marketing and legal protection all lined up. By contrast, an innovation can have a great idea but fail on timing, the Apple Newton was too early, the enabling technology wasn't ready, or fail on marketing and timing, the Microsoft Zune was a competent music player that arrived after the iPod had locked up the market. So when you analyse, you don't just say "it succeeded", you identify which TEMPLE factors drove the outcome. Same six factors, success when aligned, failure when not.

NARRATOR: And here is the headline trap, worth flagging hard because it's the most common error in the HSC, in Listing 2. The six innovation-success factors, TEMPLE, outcome H3.1, are not the eleven factors affecting design, FFA EQ NEW LOAd, outcome H1.1. The design factors, function, aesthetics, ergonomics and so on, are about whether the design itself is good. The innovation factors, TEMPLE, are about whether an innovation succeeds in the world, timing, marketing, the political and legal climate. Different lists, different questions, and a paper often asks both. Know which is which.

NARRATOR: Now the headline question: "Analyse the factors that contributed to the success or failure of an innovation you have studied." Here's the move. A weak answer says "the Flow Hive was a good idea and it sold really well", which asserts success without analysing why, capping low. A strong answer walks the relevant TEMPLE factors, weighs which mattered most, and supports each with real detail: "the Flow Hive succeeded due to several factors: its timing coincided with the save-the-bees movement; emerging technologies like injection moulding made its complex design manufacturable; and a crowdfunding marketing campaign raised over twelve million dollars; of these, its marketing and timing were decisive." A top answer adds a contrast with a failure on the same factor. The weak-versus-strong contrast is in Listing 5. Name the factors, weigh them, support with detail.

NARRATOR: One trap beyond the FFA-versus-TEMPLE one. Students list all six factors mechanically for every innovation. Don't, identify the two or three that actually decided the outcome and analyse those in depth. For the Flow Hive, lead with marketing and timing, for the Newton, lead with timing and technology. Focused, weighted analysis beats a six-item checklist, the verb analyse wants you to judge which factors mattered most.

QUESTION: Question one. Identify the factors that influence the success of an innovation. Three marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The factors are timing; available and emerging technologies; historical and cultural factors; political factors; economic and legal factors; and marketing strategies, the six remembered as TEMPLE. Three marks for naming them. Grouping them as TEMPLE, an innovation succeeds inside a TEMPLE, ensures you get all six and don't accidentally list the design factors instead.

QUESTION: Question two. Explain how timing can affect the success of an innovation, using an example. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Timing affects success because an innovation launched when society is ready for it succeeds, while one launched too early or too late often fails, even if the idea is sound. For example, the Flow Hive launched during the height of public concern for declining bee populations, so its timing amplified demand. By contrast, the Apple Newton, an early handheld computer, launched before the enabling technology was mature and before consumers were ready, so its poor timing contributed to its failure. Three marks: explain that timing is about launching when society and technology are ready, and give a success and ideally a failure example. The marker wants the principle plus a real example.

QUESTION: Question three. Analyse the factors that contributed to the success of an innovation you have studied. Six marks. This is the headline question, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer, using the Flow Hive. The Flow Hive succeeded due to several of the TEMPLE factors working together. Its timing was excellent, launching during the global save-the-bees movement when public interest in bees and sustainable living was peaking, which created ready demand. Emerging and available technologies were essential: its complex honeycomb-splitting mechanism could only be manufactured using three-D printing for prototyping and injection moulding for production. Its marketing strategy was outstanding, a compelling crowdfunding campaign with a viral video and a large social-media following raised over twelve million dollars, a record. And legally, the inventors filed a patent before crowdfunding, protecting their intellectual property from imitators. Of these, the marketing and timing were the decisive factors, converting a clever idea into a global commercial success. Six marks: analyse several named TEMPLE factors with real detail, and weigh which mattered most, reaching a judgement. Asserting "it was a good idea" without the factors caps low.

QUESTION: Question four. Distinguish between the factors affecting innovation success and the factors affecting design. Two marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The factors affecting innovation success are six factors, summarised as TEMPLE, that determine whether an innovation succeeds in the market, such as timing, marketing and the political and legal climate, relating to outcome H3.1. The factors affecting design are the eleven considerations a designer weighs when designing a product, such as function, aesthetics, ergonomics and finance, relating to outcome H1.1. The distinction is that the innovation factors concern the conditions surrounding an innovation's market success, whereas the design factors concern the qualities of the design itself. Two marks: identify the two as distinct sets with their focus. This is the headline trap, and naming it earns the mark.
