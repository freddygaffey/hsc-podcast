NARRATOR: Steve Jobs hated it. He'd wave his ten fingers in the air and say, "God gave us ten styluses — let's not invent another." And the moment he clawed his way back into control of Apple, he killed it stone dead. The product he was talking about was the Apple Newton, the world's first real handheld computer. It was a flop. It was mocked relentlessly. And yet, hidden inside that failure is the chip that's almost certainly in the phone in your pocket right now. This is the story of a design that was simply born too early — and of why "too early" and "wrong" are not the same thing.

NARRATOR: The Newton was, fittingly, conceived in the air. In early 1991, an Apple manager named Michael Tchao pitched the idea to Apple's chief executive, John Sculley, on an aeroplane. The vision was huge: not just a new gadget, but an entirely new category of computing. A computer that could slip into your pocket and come out into the world with you. Apple even coined a term for it — the PDA, the Personal Digital Assistant. The first one, the MessagePad, shipped in August 1993.

NARRATOR: Think about what they were promising in 1993. This thing could take notes, store your contacts, manage your calendar, even send a fax. It had a stylus, and — this was the headline act — it could read your handwriting and turn it into typed text. At a time when handheld computers were basically science fiction, this was wildly ambitious. The team obsessed over the physical design too. Battery life was so constrained it forced them to invent a whole new internal architecture. And Sculley added one hard rule: it had to fit in *his* pocket. So they focused relentlessly on the width — narrow enough that you could wrap your fingers around one side and your thumb around the other and feel like you wouldn't drop it.

QUESTION: So it could do all that, in 1993. Why is it remembered as a failure?

NARRATOR: One word. Handwriting. The single feature that was supposed to sell the Newton — reading your handwriting — barely worked when it shipped. As one of the lead engineers put it, "We were just way ahead of the technology. We barely got it functioning by '93 when we started shipping it." People would write a word, and the Newton would confidently turn it into something completely different. It got mocked everywhere — there was even a Doonesbury comic strip making fun of it, and a Simpsons gag. The killer feature became the feature that killed it. And here's the cruel twist: the team went back, kept working, and eventually got handwriting recognition absolutely flawless. But by then it was too late. The reputation was set. As another engineer said, the one stumbling block had finally become a joy to use — but nobody gave it a second look.

NARRATOR: It didn't help that Steve Jobs despised the thing. Partly that was personal — the Newton was Sculley's baby, and Sculley was the man who'd pushed Jobs out of Apple years earlier. But Jobs also genuinely thought the stylus was wrong. When he returned to a struggling Apple in the late 1990s, he shut the Newton down. In his own words, he thought there was some really good technology in there, but it had been, his phrase, "fucked up by mismanagement." By killing it, he freed up a group of good engineers — engineers who would go on to work on new mobile devices. Apple, he said, eventually got it right when they moved on to the iPhone and the iPad.

NARRATOR: There were hard manufacturing realities behind the scenes too. In the early 90s it was extremely difficult to get component makers to build custom parts, which meant nearly everything on the Newton's circuit board had to sit at right angles. That made it a nightmare to fit into the sleek, dark, sculpted shape the designers wanted — they called it the "Batman concept." It was also Apple's first real break from the clean white "Snow White" look that had defined the company through the 1980s. So even the look of the thing was a fight against the limits of what could actually be built.

QUESTION: Now, before the ending — was the Newton actually a waste? Pause, and have a think about what it might have left behind.

NARRATOR: Here's the payoff, and it's a genuinely great one. To get decent battery life, the Newton team went hunting for a processor that could do a lot of computing for very little power. The best option they found was a small British chip design called ARM. Apple owned about a third of the company at the time, and Apple directed the development of the specific ARM processor that went into the Newton. And without the Newton driving that work, that chip technology might quietly have died. Instead, ARM went on to become the processor architecture at the heart of the entire mobile industry. Almost every smartphone on Earth runs on an ARM-based chip. The failed product seeded the most important hardware platform of the next thirty years.

NARRATOR: And the idea itself didn't die either. The Newton's real contribution was the thought that the computer didn't have to live on a desk — that it could come with you everywhere. We don't use a stylus and we don't write on the screen anymore. But the smartphone is, in a sense, exactly what the Newton was reaching for: a PDA, a camera, a music player and a phone, all bundled into one thing in your pocket. The Newton even pioneered the idea of an assistant that understands plain language — you could write "I have a lunch meeting with John tomorrow at noon" and it would create the calendar entry. Sound familiar? That's Siri. That's Google Assistant. Newton did it first, decades early.

NARRATOR: So that's the Newton. The right idea, executed before the technology — the handwriting recognition, the components, the battery — was good enough to carry it. That's the lesson the exam wants from this one: timing isn't only about the market mood, it's about whether the *enabling technology* has arrived yet. A brilliant innovation that needs technology that doesn't quite exist will fail in the market — and may still change the world through what it leaves behind.
