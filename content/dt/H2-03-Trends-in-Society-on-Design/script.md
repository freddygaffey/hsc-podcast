NARRATOR: The forces that drive trends spell SPEEG, in Listing 1. S, Social: changes in society, demographics, multiculturalism, social conscience, values. P, Political: government policy, regulation, the political climate. E, Economic: economic conditions, wealth, socio-economic status, cost pressures. E, Environmental: concern for sustainability, climate, resources. And G, Global: globalisation, international markets, global supply chains, world events. Social, political, economic, environmental, global, SPEEG. The textbook lists exactly these as the influences that, directly and indirectly, affect trends and so impact design and production. Note one thing: historical and cultural influences are so important they get their own episode next, the era factor, so today we focus on these five contemporary drivers.

NARRATOR: How does each driver shift design? In Listing 2. A social trend toward inclusion drives universal and accessible design. A political trend, like government incentives for renewables, drives clean-energy products. An economic trend, like a downturn, drives cheaper, value-focused design. An environmental trend, the big one, drives sustainable, recyclable, low-footprint design. And a global trend, like globalised manufacturing, drives outsourced production and worldwide products. So when you see a trend, ask which SPEEG driver is behind it, that classification is the first half of a strong answer.

NARRATOR: But the syllabus skill isn't just to name trends, it's to discuss the issues arising from them, in Listing 3. Trends create problems as well as opportunities. The trend toward mass production and automation raises issues for the workplace, fewer jobs, deskilling, the social cost we covered in module two. The trend toward sustainability raises the issue of cost, environmentally friendly choices are often more expensive, the environmental-versus-financial tension. And the trend toward globalised production raises ethical issues, outsourcing to low-wage countries can mean poor labour conditions. So a strong answer names the trend, classifies its driver, and then discusses the issues, both the benefits and the problems, it creates.

QUESTION: Question two. Explain how an environmental trend has influenced design and production. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The environmental trend, driven by growing concern for sustainability and climate, has influenced design and production by pushing designers toward recyclable and renewable materials, energy-efficient products, and life-cycle thinking that minimises waste and carbon footprint. For example, many products are now designed for recyclability and repair rather than disposal. However, this trend raises the issue of cost, since sustainable materials and processes are often more expensive, creating tension between environmental and financial considerations. Three marks: identify the environmental driver, explain how it shifts design with an example, and discuss an issue it raises. The marker wants the influence plus an issue, not just "people care about the environment".

QUESTION: Question three. Discuss the issues arising from the trend towards automation in design and production. Five marks. This is the headline question, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The trend towards automation, driven by economic and global forces, is reshaping design and production, and it raises significant issues. On the positive side, automated and computer-controlled production increases efficiency, consistency and output, and lowers cost per unit, making products more affordable, and it enables more complex, precise designs. However, it raises serious workplace issues: it reduces the number of workers needed, contributing to job losses, and it deskills some traditional roles, replacing craft work with repetitive or machine-minding tasks, although it also creates new, more technical roles in programming and maintaining machinery. There are also social implications for communities dependent on manufacturing employment. On balance, automation delivers clear efficiency and cost benefits but carries real social costs that designers and society must weigh. Five marks: classify the driver, discuss both benefits and problems, especially the workplace and social issues, and reach a balanced judgement. Naming the trend without discussing its issues caps low.

QUESTION: Question four. Why is it important for designers to monitor trends in society? Two marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. It is important for designers to monitor trends because trends shape what consumers need and want, so a designer who keeps up with social, economic, environmental and other trends can create products that are relevant, timely and commercially successful, while a designer who ignores trends risks producing something outdated or mistimed, like an innovation launched as the market moves the other way. Two marks: state that trends shape consumer needs and that monitoring them keeps designs relevant and timely, ideally noting the risk of ignoring them. The timing link to innovation success is a nice touch.
