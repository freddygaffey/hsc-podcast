NARRATOR: Today's home topic is manufacturing and production processes, and at its heart is a simple, powerful idea: how many of something you make changes everything about how you make it. The syllabus dot-point is "selection of processes appropriate to a need" and "development of appropriate skills and techniques", and the skill it wants is to account for the practices undertaken in industrial and commercial settings. So this is about the scales of production and choosing the right one. By the end you'll be able to name and explain the three scales of production, choose the right scale for a given need, and tackle the exam favourite about how mass production affects the workplace. Let me give you the three scales and the hook first.

NARRATOR: There are three scales of production, and we'll remember them as JBM: Job, Batch, Mass. The one-word hook for the whole thing is "one, some, millions". Job production makes one. Batch production makes some. Mass production makes millions. They're defined in Listing 1. Let me take each properly, because you need to explain the features of each, not just name it.

NARRATOR: First, job production, also called one-off or custom production. This is where a special order is placed and the production processes are tailored to meet the individual needs of one client, producing an individual, unique product. The textbook makes a useful point: this is often what prototyping is, the designer spends time developing the first one to get it right. Think a tailor-made suit, a one-off piece of furniture, a custom prosthetic limb, or, at the large end, a building that engineering and architectural firms tender for. Three-D printing is great for one-off small items. Job production is highly flexible, you can make exactly what the client wants, but the cost per item is high, because all the time and skill go into a single unit. One, made to order.

NARRATOR: Second, batch production. This is making a limited quantity of identical or near-identical products, with small variations possible between batches. The textbook gives the key technique: batch production uses jigs and templates that let you reproduce the same task repeatedly without the expense of brand-new tools and moulds each time. It allows more specialised use of labour, some automation, and lower costs than one-off, and it often uses standard sub-assembled parts, standard nuts, bolts and fittings shared across designs. Think a bakery making two hundred loaves, a small furniture maker producing a run of fifty identical chairs, a limited release of a product. Batch sits in the middle: more efficient and cheaper per unit than job production, but more flexible than mass production, because between batches you can change the design. Some, in runs.

NARRATOR: Third, mass production. This is the production of large quantities of similar goods with only minor variations. The textbook ties it to history: the concepts of mechanisation and specialisation were developed in England during the Industrial Revolution, and the final crucial step was standardisation, making all parts for a function identical, so they're interchangeable. Mass production needs expensive tooling-up, setting up the machinery and moulds costs a lot upfront, but once running, the more units you produce from the same line, the lower the cost per unit. That's the famous idea of economies of scale. Think drink bottles, smartphones, cars on an assembly line. Mass production gives the lowest cost per item and the highest volume, but the lowest flexibility, changing a mass-produced design means re-tooling, which is slow and expensive. Millions, identical and interchangeable.

NARRATOR: So the real skill, and the dot-point, is selecting the process appropriate to the need. That's a trade-off between three things: volume, cost per unit, and flexibility, all laid out in Listing 2. Job production: low volume, high cost per unit, maximum flexibility. Mass production: high volume, low cost per unit, minimum flexibility, plus a big upfront tooling cost. Batch: in between on all counts. So you choose by asking how many you need and how much variation you want. Need one unique item for one client? Job. Need a few hundred with the option to vary between runs? Batch. Need a million identical units as cheaply as possible? Mass. Notice this connects straight to two of the factors from module one: finance, the tooling and unit costs, and quality, the consistency that standardisation gives you. Scale is where the factors meet the workshop.

NARRATOR: A quick word on the actual processes used to shape materials, because the dot-point also mentions developing appropriate skills and techniques, and it's in Listing 3. Production techniques fall into a few families: forming, where you shape material, often with heat, like casting and moulding metal or blow-moulding plastic; cutting and shaping, like machining or turning on a lathe; joining, like welding, bolting or adhesives; and finishing, like sanding, painting or coating. You don't need every technique by name, but you should know that a designer selects the technique to suit the material, the scale and the need, injection moulding suits mass-producing plastic parts, while hand tools or 3D printing suit a one-off. And quality production skills, doing each technique accurately and safely, are what turn a design into a quality product.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. Job, or one-off production: a custom prosthetic limb or a tailor-made suit, one unique item. Batch production: a run of fifty identical chairs from a small furniture maker, a limited quantity. Mass production: plastic drink bottles or smartphones, millions of identical units. JBM, one, some, millions. If you matched all three with sensible examples, you're ready. The thing the marker wants is that the choice of scale follows the need, you wouldn't mass-produce a custom prosthetic, and you couldn't affordably hand-make a billion bottles.

NARRATOR: Now the exam favourite, straight from the recent paper: "Explain how the trend towards mass production may affect workplace practices." This is worth real marks and students answer it shallowly, so here's how to do it. A weak answer says "machines replace people", which is one blunt point and caps low. A strong answer recognises that mass production reshapes work in several specific ways, and those effects are collected in Listing 4. It brings division of labour and specialisation, each worker or machine performs one narrow, repeated task rather than making the whole product. That can mean deskilling, the broad craft skills of making a whole item give way to narrow repetitive ones, though it also creates new skilled roles maintaining and programming the machines, that's reskilling. It brings increased automation and mechanisation, which raises output and consistency but can reduce the number of workers needed, a real social cost in job losses. And it raises work-health-and-safety considerations, repetitive tasks and heavy machinery introduce their own hazards. A strong answer names these effects and reaches a balanced judgement: mass production increases efficiency and consistency but changes the nature of work, often reducing craft skill and employment while demanding new technical skills. The weak-versus-strong contrast is in Listing 6, and notice the trap, this question is really about people, not machines.

NARRATOR: Let me make the scale-selection concrete with a worked example, because matching products to scales is exactly what the exam tests. It's in Listing 5. Take three products. A custom prosthetic limb: it must fit one individual's body exactly, so it's job production, one-off, tailored, possibly 3D printed from a scan of the patient, high cost per unit justified by the need for a perfect individual fit. A boutique run of designer chairs, say fifty of them: that's batch production, jigs and templates let the maker reproduce the design efficiently, the limited quantity keeps it exclusive, and between runs the design can be tweaked. A plastic drink bottle sold in supermarkets: that's mass production, millions of identical units, injection-moulded on automated lines, standardised and interchangeable, with the lowest possible cost per unit thanks to economies of scale. Same question every time: how many, how unique, how cheap, and the answer picks the scale.

NARRATOR: Now the connections. First, straight back to last episode: the scales map onto the settings. Domestic production tends to be job or small batch, community is often batch, and industrial settings are where mass production lives. Settings and scales are two views of the same thing, where it's made, and how many. Second, forward to Year 12, outcome H6.1: you'll have to justify the production techniques and processes in your own major design project by relating them to industrial and commercial practice, so understanding scale now is direct MDP preparation. Third, and this is a big one, that mass-production-and-workplace question is really a trend question, which is outcome H2.1 in Year 12, the influence of trends in society on design and production. Mass production, and now automation, is one of the biggest such trends, so hold this for the HSC. Fourth, a sharp link to the case studies coming later: scale doesn't rescue a bad design. The Ford Edsel and the Microsoft Zune were both mass-produced competently, and both flopped, because mass-producing a product the market doesn't want just means a warehouse full of unsold units. Production scale answers "how many", but the factors from module one, especially needs, decide whether you should make any at all.

NARRATOR: One trap to flag. Students confuse batch and mass production. The clean distinction: batch makes a limited, defined quantity in runs, and you can vary the design between batches, it's flexible. Mass production runs continuously to make very large quantities of standardised, interchangeable items, and changing the design is slow and costly because of the tooling. Limited and flexible versus large and standardised. If you can state that, you've got the distinction the marker is checking.

QUESTION: Question one. Distinguish between batch production and mass production. Three marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Batch production manufactures a limited, defined quantity of identical products in runs, often using jigs and templates, and the design can be varied between batches, making it relatively flexible. Mass production manufactures very large quantities of standardised, interchangeable products continuously, usually with expensive tooling and high automation, and changing the design is slow and costly. The key distinction is that batch is limited and flexible, whereas mass is large-scale, standardised and inflexible but achieves the lowest cost per unit through economies of scale. Three marks: define batch, define mass, and state the distinction, limited and flexible versus large and standardised. The trap is treating them as the same just because both make multiples.

QUESTION: Question two. A designer needs to produce a single custom wheelchair fitted to one user. Identify the most appropriate scale of production and justify your choice. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The most appropriate scale is job, or one-off, production, because the wheelchair must be tailored to the individual measurements and needs of a single user, producing one unique product. Job production allows the design to be fully customised, for example using the user's own body measurements, and although the cost per unit is high, this is justified because only one unit is needed and a precise individual fit is essential. Three marks: identify job production, justify it by the need for a single customised unit, and acknowledge the high per-unit cost is acceptable given the requirement. The marker wants the scale matched to the need, with a justification, not just a label.

QUESTION: Question three. Explain how the trend towards mass production may affect workplace practices. Five marks. This is the headline question, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The trend towards mass production significantly changes the nature of work. It introduces division of labour and specialisation, where each worker or machine performs a single narrow, repeated task rather than crafting a whole product, which can lead to deskilling as broad craft skills are replaced by repetitive ones. At the same time, increased automation and mechanisation raise output and product consistency but reduce the number of workers required, contributing to job losses, while creating new, more technical roles in maintaining and programming machinery, a form of reskilling. Mass production also raises work-health-and-safety considerations, since repetitive tasks and heavy automated machinery introduce specific hazards. On balance, while mass production increases efficiency and consistency and lowers costs, it changes work by reducing traditional craft skill and overall employment while demanding new technical skills. Five marks: identify division of labour and specialisation, deskilling and reskilling, automation and job losses, and WHS, and reach a balanced judgement. The shallow answer, just "machines take jobs", caps low; the strong answer addresses several specific effects on people and weighs them.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain why mass production achieves a lower cost per unit than job production. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Mass production achieves a lower cost per unit because it produces very large quantities using standardised, interchangeable parts and automated machinery, so although there is a high upfront cost in tooling up the production line, that cost is spread across a huge number of units. This is the principle of economies of scale, the more units produced from the same line, the lower the average cost of each one. Job production, by contrast, concentrates the full cost of time, skill and setup into a single unique item, making each one expensive. Three marks: explain standardisation and high volume, name economies of scale and the spreading of tooling cost, and contrast with job production's high per-unit cost. The key term the marker wants is economies of scale.
