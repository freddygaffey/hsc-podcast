NARRATOR: Now the core skill, and it's a comparison: you study what an industrial or commercial setting would do to make your product, and relate it to what you actually did, to justify your choices. The key insight, in Listing 2, is that the principle is often the same even when the scale is wildly different. An industrial setting mass-produces with CNC machines for precise, repeatable parts; you, working as a one-off in a domestic setting, use a jig or template to get the same repeatability by hand. A factory has formal quality control sampling; you check each part against your criteria. So you don't copy the factory's scale, you adapt the principle of its practice to your project, and Listing 3 lines up several such practices against their MDP adaptations. That's the move that earns H6.1 marks: relate the professional practice to yours, and justify your method by reference to it.

QUESTION: Question two. Explain how studying industrial and commercial practices can help justify the processes used in a major design project. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Studying industrial and commercial practices helps justify a student's processes because it provides a professional benchmark to compare against and learn from. By examining how industry selects production techniques, controls quality and ensures safety, the student can show that their own choices follow sound, proven principles, even if implemented at a smaller scale, for example using a jig to achieve the repeatability that industry achieves with automated machinery. This demonstrates that the processes were chosen deliberately and appropriately, rather than arbitrarily, which is what justification requires. Three marks: state that industry is a benchmark, that the student relates their processes to proven principles, and that this justifies the choices as deliberate and appropriate. The marker wants the justification link, not just a comparison.

QUESTION: Question three. Relate a production technique used in industrial settings to your major design project. Five marks. This is the headline question, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer, using jigs. In industrial furniture manufacturing, jigs and computer-controlled machinery are used to ensure that every component is produced identically and accurately, and that assembly is fast and consistent, the principle being repeatability and precision at high volume. In my major design project, working as a one-off in a school setting, I related this practice by designing and making a simple drilling jig for my chair legs. Although my scale was far smaller and the jig was hand-made rather than computer-controlled, it applied the same principle: it ensured all four legs were drilled identically, giving consistent, accurate assembly that I could not have achieved reliably by hand. I therefore justified my process by reference to the industrial practice, adapting its principle of repeatability to my scale and resources. Five marks: name the industrial technique, explain its principle, relate it to a specific adaptation in your project, and justify the choice. A surface scale-contrast caps low.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain why the design process is essentially the same in a student's project as in an industrial setting, even though the scale differs. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The design process is essentially the same because both a student and an industrial producer must define a need, research, generate and develop ideas, produce a solution, and evaluate it, this sequence is fundamental to creating any successful design. What differs is the scale and resources: an industrial setting uses large machinery, a divided workforce, formal quality control and mass production, whereas a student works on a one-off with limited resources and informal checking. So the same process and principles, such as repeatability and quality control, are applied, just at a different scale and level of formality. Three marks: state the shared process, identify scale and resources as what differs, and note the principles are the same. This is the settings-and-scales insight from module two applied to H6.1.
