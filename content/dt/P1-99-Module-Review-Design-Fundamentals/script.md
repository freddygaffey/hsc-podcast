NARRATOR: Let me re-surface the key terms topic by topic, fast, the way you'd need them in an answer. They're collected in Listing 3. From episode one: design is the purposeful creation of a product, system or environment to meet an identified need or opportunity. A need is a problem you respond to; an opportunity is a gap you reach for. Design differs from art because design answers to the user and the problem, whereas art answers to the maker and self-expression. And design theory, the knowledge that informs design, and design practice, the doing of it, are interdependent, theory disciplines practice, practice tests and refines theory.

NARRATOR: From episode two: a designer is a collaborative problem-solver who combines creativity and technology, shaped by influences like culture and personal experience. Professions interact, working together on one project, and overlap, crossing boundaries because the design process is adaptable. We had Marc Newson, the Australian industrial designer whose adaptability lets him use the design process across furniture, products and the Qantas Skybed; P.E Nation, founded from the designers' own need and built on collaboration; and James Dyson, whose thousands of prototypes are the theory-practice loop in action.

NARRATOR: From episode three: the design process is DRIPER, and it's cyclic and iterative, the same process scaling across domestic, community, industrial and commercial settings, what changes is the scale and formality, not the process. QUESTION: Here's a connection question, the kind the exam loves. How do the factors affecting design relate to the design process? Pause the player, answer in two or three sentences, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The factors affecting design are considered throughout the design process, not at a single point. During the define stage they're translated into criteria for success; during research they're investigated, such as material properties or ergonomic data; and during evaluation the design is tested against those factor-based criteria, with failures looping the designer back to refine. So the design process is the sequence of actions, and the factors are the considerations weighed at every stage of it. That answer, linking process and factors explicitly, is exactly the integrated thinking that separates the top band from the middle, because it shows you understand the module as a system, not a list.

NARRATOR: Now let me walk the traps, because a module review's most useful job is stopping you losing marks on the boundaries between topics. They're in Listing 4. Trap one, the big one: confusing function with appropriateness. Function is whether the product performs its task, does it work. Appropriateness is whether it's the right solution for that need and that user, is it suitable. A product can function perfectly and still be inappropriate, a powerful petrol chainsaw functions, but it's an inappropriate solution for a child's gardening toy. Keep them separate: function is does it work, appropriateness is is it the right, suitable solution for this user.

NARRATOR: Trap two: defining design theory and practice but never explaining the relationship. If the question says "explain the relationship", the marks are in the two-way loop, theory disciplines practice, practice refines theory, not in the two definitions alone. Trap three: describing the design process as a straight line. It's cyclic, evaluation is continual and you loop back, if you draw it as a one-way checklist you miss the iterative nature, which is the whole analytical point. Trap four: confusing the three obsolescence types, especially technological and planned. Technological obsolescence happens to a product because the world's technology moved on; planned obsolescence is done to a product on purpose, designed in from the start. Circumstance versus intent. And trap five: defining ergonomics as just "comfort". It's the fit between the user and the product, measured with real anthropometric data, and getting it wrong causes injury, not just discomfort. Five traps, and every one of them is a boundary between two ideas the exam will deliberately blur.

QUESTION: Question one, the big integrated one. Analyse how a designer you have studied applied the design process and the factors affecting design to develop a successful product. Six marks. This question deliberately fuses three topics, designers, the process, and the factors. Pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer, using Marc Newson and the Qantas Skybed, and watch how it weaves all three topics together. Marc Newson, an Australian industrial designer known for adapting the design process across diverse fields, applied that process to design the Qantas Skybed business-class seat. He defined the need, a comfortable lie-flat seat within the tight constraints of an aircraft cabin, and set criteria for success. Through research and ideation he generated and developed concepts, then produced and tested prototypes, evaluating and refining them iteratively. Throughout, he balanced the factors affecting design: ergonomics was critical, the seat had to fit a wide range of human bodies comfortably for long flights, drawing on anthropometric data; function demanded it convert reliably into a flat bed; aesthetics had to convey premium quality to the target market; and finance and quality shaped the materials within the airline's budget. The product succeeded because the design process was applied rigorously and the competing factors were balanced for the user and context. Six marks, and the marks are awarded for genuinely integrating the three: naming the designer and their process, walking the DRIPER stages applied to a real product, and weaving in specific named factors. An answer that only describes the designer, or only lists the factors, without connecting them through the process, caps in the middle. The full integrated answer is in Listing 6.

QUESTION: Question two. Explain the relationship between design theory and design practice, and illustrate it using the work of a designer. Four marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Design theory is the knowledge and principles that inform design decisions, while design practice is the application of that knowledge to create real solutions, and the two are interdependent: theory disciplines practice so decisions are informed rather than arbitrary, while practice tests and refines theory because real outcomes reveal which principles hold. James Dyson illustrates this: his theoretical understanding of cyclonic separation guided each prototype he built, that's theory informing practice, and each of his thousands of failed prototypes fed back to refine his understanding, that's practice refining theory, until the vacuum worked. Four marks: define both, explain the two-way relationship, and ground it in a real designer whose iterative practice demonstrates the loop. Linking the abstract relationship to a concrete designer is what lifts this answer.

QUESTION: Question three. A company designs a smartphone with a sleek, non-repairable glass body and releases a new model every year. Using the factors affecting design, evaluate this design approach. Six marks. This is a stimulus question, exactly the HSC format. Pause and write a full answer.

NARRATOR: Model answer, and notice "evaluate" demands a judgement weighing both sides. The approach can be assessed against several factors. Aesthetically it's strong, a sleek glass body has high visual appeal to the target market, supporting sales. In terms of function and quality the premium materials perform well. However, the approach raises serious concerns under other factors. The non-repairable body and annual release cycle are a form of planned obsolescence, deliberately designed to shorten the product's useful life and drive repeat purchases, which has negative short and long-term environmental consequences, more electronic waste, more resource extraction, a poor result on a life cycle analysis. There's also an ethical and financial cost to the consumer, who must repurchase frequently. On balance, while the approach succeeds commercially and aesthetically, it performs poorly against environmental and ethical factors, making it difficult to justify as responsible design. Six marks: identify the factors the approach satisfies, identify the factors it violates, name planned obsolescence and life cycle analysis explicitly, and reach a balanced judgement. The trap is only praising it or only condemning it, "evaluate" needs both sides and a verdict.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain why the design process is described as iterative, and why this matters for producing a quality solution. Four marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The design process is iterative because the designer does not move through the stages once in a fixed order; evaluation is carried out continually, and when a design fails to meet its criteria the designer loops back to an earlier stage, regenerating ideas or revising research, and repeats the cycle. This matters for quality because each loop allows problems to be identified early and the design to be progressively improved against its criteria, rather than committing to a single untested guess, the repeated cycle of evaluation and refinement is precisely what drives the design toward a quality solution. Four marks: explain the non-linear, looping nature with continual evaluation, and connect that looping to the progressive improvement of quality. This links episode three's process directly to episode five's quality factor, which is exactly the integrated thinking the review is training.

QUESTION: Last one, question five, quick but it catches people. Distinguish between function and appropriateness as factors affecting design, using an example. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Function is the ability of a product to perform the task for which it was designed, whereas appropriateness is whether the design solution is suitable for the specific end-user and the environment in which it's used. The two are different: a product can function well yet be inappropriate for its intended user. For example, a heavy, powerful industrial drill functions effectively, but it would be an inappropriate design solution for young children, for whom a lightweight, safe, simple tool is required. Three marks: define both factors, state that function is about performance while appropriateness is about suitability for the user, and give an example where something functions but is inappropriate. This is trap one from earlier, and the distinction, does it work versus is it the right solution for this user, is the mark.

QUESTION: Question six, spanning the process and settings. Outline the design process and explain how it would be used differently in a domestic setting compared with an industrial setting. Five marks. Pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The design process consists of defining the need and brief, researching, generating ideas, producing and prototyping, evaluating against the criteria, and refining, and it is cyclic, the designer loops back from evaluation to improve the design. This same process is used in both settings, but the scale and rigour differ. In a domestic setting, an individual runs the process on a small scale with limited resources, informal planning and little documentation, for example a person designing a piece of furniture at home. In an industrial setting, teams run the same process with large budgets, formal management of action, time and finance, specialised equipment and rigorous documented evaluation, because the product will be mass-produced and errors are costly. Five marks: outline the DRIPER stages, state that the process itself is the same in both, and contrast the scale, resources, formality and documentation with an example of each. The key insight is that the process doesn't change between settings, only its scale and formality do, and that idea opens straight into the next module.

QUESTION: Question seven, interleaving two factors. Explain how the factors of ergonomics and work health and safety work together to protect the user of a product. Four marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Ergonomics is the design of a product to fit the human user, using anthropometric data so that the product is comfortable and safe to use and does not cause strain or injury, for example sizing a chair or a handle to suit the range of users. Work health and safety concerns the rights and responsibilities that ensure people are protected when making and using the product, governed by legislation such as the Work Health and Safety Act. The two work together because ergonomics designs the safety into the product itself, while work health and safety governs safe practice around it, so a designer who applies both produces a product that is safe by design and safe in use. Four marks: define both factors, and explain that ergonomics builds safety into the product while WHS governs safe practice, the two complementing each other to protect the user. This shows you can link two factors rather than treat each as a silo, which is exactly what the higher bands reward.

QUESTION: Question eight, on designers and careers. Describe the nature of a designer's work and identify the career opportunities available in design and production. Five marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The nature of a designer's work is to solve problems by combining creativity and technology, applying and adapting the design process to turn an identified need into a real solution. Designers are influenced by culture, history, environment and personal experience, and they usually work collaboratively, interacting with other professions and sometimes overlapping into them because the design process is adaptable across fields. The career opportunities span traditional pathways such as industrial and product design, graphic and multimedia design, fashion and textile design, architecture and interior design, and engineering, as well as emerging fields created by new technology such as user-experience design, sustainability consulting and digital fabrication, in a field that requires lifelong learning. Five marks: describe the nature of the work, creativity plus technology, influences, collaboration, process-driven, and identify a range of careers across both traditional and emerging fields. Including emerging fields shows you understand the profession is evolving, which lifts the answer.
