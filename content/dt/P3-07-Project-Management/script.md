NARRATOR: Let's reset. Last episode, communication and presentation, the process SMMR, sender, message, medium, receiver, the forms V-WGVA, and the criteria CAE, clarity, appropriateness, ease of interpretation. Before that, research methods, QISOTS, and market research. Today's topic is the one that holds a whole project together: management. You can have great ideas, solid research and clear communication, but if you don't manage your time, tasks and money, the project never gets finished. And this is the topic that most directly feeds your Year 12 major design project, where you'll be marked on managing action, time and finance, so it's worth getting right now.

NARRATOR: Today's home topic is project management. The syllabus names two things: management techniques and tools, including those used in industrial and commercial settings, and the factors affecting management. And the skill is to plan, implement and evaluate a sequence of operations to complete a design project. By the end you'll be able to explain what management is, use the key management tools, especially the Gantt chart, and list the factors affecting management. Let me start with what management actually is.

NARRATOR: Management is the process of directing resources to achieve goals. The resources are time, money, materials and people, and the goal is the completed project meeting the brief. Managing means planning what needs to happen, organising the resources to do it, then monitoring progress and adjusting when things slip. The textbook frames it as combining tasks, resources, skills and a timeline so each task is done at the right time and in the right sequence, and that definition is in Listing 1. That phrase, the right time and the right sequence, is the heart of it.

NARRATOR: Now the management tools, and the three big ones map onto three kinds of plan that you'll meet again, formally, in Year 12 as action, time and finance plans, so I'll tag them ATF. They're in Listing 2. First, the action plan, the action part: this is the first step, you brainstorm and list every task needed to complete the project, often as a mind map, so nothing is forgotten. Second, the time plan, and the key tool here is the Gantt chart, the time part: a Gantt chart is a grid that lists every task down the side and time across the top, drawing each task as a bar showing its start date, duration and end date. It shows at a glance what's happening when, which tasks overlap, and whether you're on schedule. Third, the finance plan, the finance part: a detailed budget of all the costs, materials, tools, services, that ensures the project stays within budget, because, as the textbook warns, if costs exceed the budget the project's success is compromised. Plus a fourth tool, the journal or diary, an ongoing record of ideas, sketches, decisions and meeting notes, which doubles as your ongoing evaluation. Action, time, finance, plus a journal.

NARRATOR: The Gantt chart deserves a closer look because it's the most examinable management tool, and it's in Listing 3. The power of a Gantt chart is that it handles dependencies, the fact that some tasks must be finished before others can start, while others can run at the same time. You can't paint the model before you've built it, that's a dependent task, but you might research materials and sketch ideas simultaneously. The longest chain of dependent tasks, the sequence that determines the minimum time the whole project needs, is called the critical path, and any delay on the critical path delays the whole project. So a Gantt chart lets you sequence tasks correctly, spot what can be done in parallel to save time, and identify the critical tasks you can't afford to let slip. And crucially, you monitor against it, comparing actual progress to the plan and adjusting, that's the implement-and-evaluate part of the skill.

NARRATOR: The second syllabus point is the factors affecting management, the things a manager must consider, and the syllabus lists eight. We'll hold them with the tag RV GS-CDT D, in Listing 4, but the easier way is the logical flow: first work out your available Resources and clarify your Values, what matters for this project; then set your Goals and the Standards the work must meet; then evaluate the Costs and benefits of each alternative and make and implement Decisions; and finally describe and sequence the Tasks and keep Documentation of your plans. Resources, values, goals, standards, costs and benefits, decisions, tasks and sequencing, documentation. Eight factors, and that order, from resources and values through to documented sequenced tasks, is a logical way to remember them rather than memorising a bare list.

QUESTION: Retrieval. What is a Gantt chart, and what does the critical path tell you? Pause the player, answer both, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. A Gantt chart is a project-management grid that lists each task with its start date, duration and end date as a bar against a timeline, showing what happens when, which tasks overlap, and whether the project is on schedule. The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks, the sequence that determines the minimum time the whole project will take, so any delay to a task on the critical path delays the entire project. If you got "Gantt schedules tasks against time" and "critical path is the chain that sets the minimum duration", that's the mark. The critical path is the part students miss, and it's a top-band detail.

NARRATOR: Now the headline question: "Explain how management tools are used to plan and monitor a design project." Here's the move. A weak answer says "I made a timeline so I knew what to do", which names a tool and stops, capping low. A strong answer explains the tools doing real work: an action plan lists every task so none is forgotten; a Gantt chart sequences those tasks against a timeline, showing dependencies, parallel tasks and the critical path, and is used to monitor actual progress against the plan and re-plan when a task slips; and a finance plan tracks costs against the budget to keep the project viable. The key word is monitor, management isn't just planning at the start, it's continually checking progress against the plan and adjusting. The weak-versus-strong contrast is in Listing 6.

NARRATOR: Let me make it concrete, in Listing 5. Imagine a four-week project to design and build a small product. Week one, research and sketch ideas, two tasks you can run simultaneously. Week two, finalise the design and order materials, the design must finish before you order, a dependency. Week three, build the prototype, which can't start until materials arrive. Week four, test, refine and present. On a Gantt chart those become bars across four weeks, with build depending on materials depending on design, that dependent chain is the critical path. Alongside it, a finance plan lists the materials at, say, forty dollars, tools hire at twenty, and printing at ten, a seventy-dollar budget, and you track actual spending against it. If the materials are delayed, the Gantt chart immediately shows the build and everything after it slipping, so you re-plan. That's planning and monitoring in action.

First, back to module two, episode one, we said larger settings, industrial and commercial, need more formal management precisely because of their scale, this is why. And fifth, management connects to the evaluation topic next, because monitoring progress against the Gantt chart is a form of continual evaluation.

NARRATOR: One trap to flag. Students describe management as a one-off plan made at the start. It isn't, the skill is to plan, implement and evaluate, the monitoring and re-planning is half the marks. Always say that management tools are used not just to plan but to track actual progress against the plan and adjust when things change. A Gantt chart you draw once and never look at again isn't management.

NARRATOR: Now the exam-style questions, real NESA format, get a pen. Four questions, model answers in Listing 7.

QUESTION: Question one. Identify three project-management tools and state the purpose of each. Three marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. An action plan lists all the tasks needed to complete the project so none is forgotten. A Gantt chart schedules those tasks against a timeline, showing their start and end dates, durations and sequence, and lets you monitor progress. A finance plan budgets all the costs and tracks spending to keep the project within budget. A fourth valid tool is a journal or diary for ongoing recording and evaluation. Three marks, one per tool with its purpose. Pairing each tool with what it actually does, not just naming it, is what scores.

QUESTION: Question two. Explain the importance of a Gantt chart in managing a design project. Four marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A Gantt chart is important because it schedules every task against a timeline, showing each task's start date, duration and end date, so the designer can see what must happen when. It reveals dependencies, tasks that must be completed before others can begin, and tasks that can run simultaneously, allowing the work to be sequenced efficiently. It also identifies the critical path, the chain of dependent tasks that determines the project's minimum duration, so the designer knows which delays would set back the whole project. Finally, it is used throughout to monitor actual progress against the plan and to re-plan when tasks slip, keeping the project on schedule. Four marks: explain scheduling against time, dependencies and parallel tasks, the critical path, and monitoring/re-planning. The monitoring point and the critical path lift this to full marks.

QUESTION: Question three. Identify four factors that affect the management of a design project. Two marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Any four of: identifying available resources; clarifying values; setting goals; setting standards; evaluating the costs and benefits of alternatives; making and implementing decisions; describing and sequencing tasks; and documenting plans. Two marks for four valid factors. The tag RV GS-CDT D, or the flow resources and values, goals and standards, costs and decisions, tasks and documentation, keeps you from blanking.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain why management is described as an ongoing process rather than a single planning step. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Management is ongoing because a plan made at the start rarely survives contact with reality, tasks take longer than expected, materials are delayed, costs change. The skill is to plan, implement and evaluate continually: the designer monitors actual progress against the Gantt chart and actual spending against the finance plan, and when something slips, re-sequences tasks or adjusts the budget to keep the project on track to meet the deadline and the brief. Management that stops after the initial plan fails to respond to these changes. Three marks: state that plans change as reality intervenes, that management means continual monitoring against the plan, and that the manager adjusts in response. The insight is plan, implement, evaluate, monitoring is the ongoing part.
