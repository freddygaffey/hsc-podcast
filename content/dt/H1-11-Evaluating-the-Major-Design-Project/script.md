The textbook stresses evaluation is ongoing and occurs throughout, so when an exam asks about evaluation, show it happening across the project, not just at the end. Before, mid, after.

NARRATOR: Now the second half, specific to H4.3, the impact of the major design project, in Listing 3. Impact across individual, society and environment, costs and benefits, that's the H4.3 impact assessment.

NARRATOR: Now the exam-style questions, real NESA format, get a pen. Four questions, model answers in Listing 6.

QUESTION: Question one. What does a designer evaluate a finished major design project against? Two marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A designer evaluates the finished project against the criteria for success established in the project proposal, both the functional criteria, what it must do, and the aesthetic criteria, how it must look, judging objectively whether each measurable criterion was met. Two marks: state that you evaluate against the proposal's criteria, and name functional and aesthetic. The link back to the proposal's criteria is the mark.

QUESTION: Question two. Explain why evaluation of a major design project should be ongoing. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Evaluation should be ongoing because evaluating throughout design and production, not just at the end, allows problems to be identified and corrected early, when it is still cheap and possible to fix them, and lets the design be progressively refined against its criteria. Ongoing evaluation, before by setting criteria, during by testing and refining, and after by a final judgement, keeps the project aligned with its success criteria the whole way through, rather than discovering failures too late. Three marks: state that ongoing evaluation catches problems early and drives refinement, describe the before-during-after pattern, and contrast with end-only evaluation. The BMA structure secures it.

QUESTION: Question three. Evaluate a major design project against its criteria for success and assess its impact on the individual, society and the environment. Six marks. This is the headline question, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer, using a sample accessible storage unit. Against its criteria for success, the project largely succeeded: it met the functional criterion of supporting one hundred kilograms, confirmed by load testing, and the criterion of tool-free assembly within thirty minutes, achieved in twenty-five; it partly met the cost criterion, coming in slightly over budget at one hundred and sixty dollars. Assessing its impact, for the individual it provides safe, accessible storage that improves daily independence, at the cost of the purchase price; for society it promotes inclusive design and could benefit many users with reduced mobility, though its production consumed resources; and for the environment, the timber and fixings have a footprint in extraction and eventual disposal, partly offset by selecting renewable, recyclable materials. Overall the project met most of its criteria and delivers a net positive impact, with a modest environmental cost. Six marks: evaluate against each named criterion with evidence, and assess impact at all three levels with costs and benefits, reaching an overall judgement. Doing only one half, or omitting the costs, caps the answer.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain why a designer must consider both the costs and the benefits when assessing a project's impact. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A designer must weigh both costs and benefits because almost every design has positive and negative impacts, and an honest, balanced evaluation, which the verb evaluate demands, must account for both. Considering only the benefits overstates the project's value and ignores real social and environmental costs, such as resource consumption or waste, while considering only the costs misses the genuine good the project does. Weighing both at each level, individual, society and environment, produces a fair, reasoned judgement of the project's true impact. Three marks: state that designs have both positive and negative impacts, that a balanced evaluation needs both, and that this produces a fair judgement. The insight is that "evaluate" requires both sides.
