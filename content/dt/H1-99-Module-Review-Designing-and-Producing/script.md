QUESTION: A connection question. How do the project proposal and the final evaluation relate, and why does it matter that the criteria are measurable? Pause the player, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The proposal and the final evaluation are bookends of the major design project. The proposal, at the start, sets the criteria for success the project must achieve, and the evaluation, at the end, judges the finished project against exactly those criteria. It matters that the criteria are measurable because only a measurable criterion, such as "must support one hundred kilograms", can be objectively tested at evaluation, an unmeasurable criterion like "must be good quality" leaves the judgement subjective. So measurable criteria set in the proposal make the final evaluation objective and reliable. That bookend, with measurable criteria as the hinge, is a high-value point that ties the whole module together.

NARRATOR: Now the traps, in Listing 5. Trap one, the headline for the whole HSC: narrating instead of justifying. Describing what you did caps low, justify with decision, reason, factor, evidence. Trap two: confusing the eleven factors affecting design, H1.1, FFA EQ NEW LOAd, with the six factors affecting innovation success, H3.1, TEMPLE, you'll meet TEMPLE in module H2, keep them separate. Trap three: treating evaluation as a single step at the end, it's continual, before, mid and after. Trap four: answering a safety question with PPE first, PPE is the last resort in the hierarchy of control. Four traps, each a place the exam catches students.

QUESTION: Question one. Outline the components of a major design project proposal. Three marks. Pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A proposal outlines the identification and exploration of the need, supported by a needs analysis; the areas of investigation to be researched; and the measurable criteria to evaluate the project's success, both functional and aesthetic. Three marks, one per component, NICe. Noting the need is analysed and the criteria are measurable strengthens it.

QUESTION: Question two, the big integrated one. Explain how a designer manages, researches and evaluates a major design project to ensure a quality outcome. Six marks. This fuses three outcomes. Pause the player and write a full answer before you play, the worked answer is in Listing 7.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A designer ensures a quality outcome by integrating management, research and evaluation throughout the project. Management plans, action, time and finance, sequence the work on a Gantt chart, track the budget, and are monitored and revised when tasks slip, keeping the project on time and on budget. Research and experimentation gather evidence, primary and secondary, which is analysed and applied to justify design and resource decisions rather than guessing. And continual evaluation against the proposal's measurable criteria, before, during and after, catches problems early, drives refinement, and produces a final judgement of the project and its impact. Together, management controls the process, research grounds the decisions in evidence, and evaluation keeps the project aligned to its criteria, so the outcome is a quality solution that meets the identified need. Six marks: link management, research and evaluation, showing how each contributes and how they work together. Describing one in isolation caps in the middle.

QUESTION: Question three. Explain how studying a designer can assist a major design project. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Studying a designer assists the project because the student can examine the designer's proven practices and processes, how they research, prototype, test and adapt, and emulate those methods in their own work where appropriate. For example, adopting a designer's iterative prototyping gives the student a disciplined, evidence-based approach, and studying their flexibility with materials may suggest techniques the student would not otherwise try. The key is emulating the designer's process, not copying their product. Three marks: state that you learn and emulate proven processes, give an example, and stress method over product.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain the principles underlying safe working practices in the major design project. Five marks. Pause and write a full answer.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Safe working practices rest on several principles. There is a legal duty of care under work health and safety legislation, the designer is responsible for the safety of themselves and others. Hazards are managed through risk assessment, identifying hazards, assessing the risks, and controlling them using the hierarchy of control, which prioritises eliminating a hazard, then substituting it, then engineering controls, then administrative controls, and only finally personal protective equipment, because PPE protects only the wearer while elimination removes the hazard for everyone. Safety should also be designed in, anticipated and reduced at the design and resource-selection stage, and documented, through material safety data sheets and safe procedures. Five marks: name the duty of care, risk assessment, the hierarchy of control with PPE last, and designing safety in. This same answer scores in H4.2 and H6.1.

QUESTION: Question five, a trap-buster. Distinguish between the factors affecting design and the factors affecting the success of an innovation. Two marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The factors affecting design are the eleven considerations a designer weighs in any project, function, needs, aesthetics, ergonomics, finance, environmental and so on, relating to outcome H1.1. The factors affecting the success of an innovation are a separate set of six, timing, emerging technologies, marketing, political, legal-economic and historical-cultural factors, relating to outcome H3.1 in module H2. The distinction is that the design factors concern the design itself, while the innovation factors concern the conditions for an innovation's market success. Two marks: identify them as two distinct sets with their focus. This guards against the headline trap.

QUESTION: Question six. Explain how a designer selects and uses resources responsibly in a major design project. Four marks. Pause the player and write a full answer before you play, more model answers are in Listing 8.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A designer selects resources responsibly by weighing safety, ethical and environmental factors, SEE, on top of the material's suitability for the function, and by justifying the choice from the results of research and experimentation rather than assumption. For example, a material might be chosen because testing confirmed its strength, it is non-toxic and safe to work, ethically sourced, and recyclable. The designer then uses the resource safely by applying the hierarchy of control, eliminating or engineering out hazards before relying on personal protective equipment. In this way the resource choice is responsible, evidence-based and safe. Four marks: weigh the SEE factors, justify from research, and address safe use via the hierarchy. The verb is select and justify, so the justification is the mark.

QUESTION: Question seven. Assess the impact of a major design project on the individual, society and the environment. Five marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The impact of a major design project should be assessed across three levels, weighing costs and benefits at each. For the individual, the project may provide a useful, accessible product that improves daily life, at the cost of its purchase price. For society, it may offer a benefit such as promoting inclusive design or meeting a community need, while its production consumes resources and may have social costs. For the environment, both local and global, the materials, manufacture and eventual disposal create a footprint, which can be reduced by choosing renewable, recyclable materials and weighed against any environmental benefit the product delivers. A balanced assessment concludes with an overall judgement of the net impact. Five marks: assess all three levels, individual, society and environment, with both costs and benefits, and reach a balanced judgement. A one-sided or single-level answer caps low. This is the same impact skill that scales up to H2.2 for innovations.
