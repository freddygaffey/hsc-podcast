QUESTION: A connection question, the kind the exam rewards. How do the design brief and the final evaluation relate? Pause the player, answer in two or three sentences, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The brief and the evaluation are bookends of a project. The brief, at the start, establishes the measurable criteria for success that the design must achieve, and the evaluation, conducted continually and finally, judges the design against exactly those criteria. This is why the criteria must be measurable, they are the standard the evaluation tests against, so a project is judged successful if it meets the criteria its own brief set. That brief-to-evaluation link is a high-value point that ties the module together.

NARRATOR: Now the traps, in Listing 4. Trap one, the headline for this module: confusing market research with design or user research. Market research asks "will it sell, and to whom", design research asks "what does the user need and how do they use it", both are research, different questions. Trap two, primary versus secondary research, the test is who originally collected the data, you or someone else. Trap three, creative versus critical thinking, generate versus judge. Trap four, parameter versus criterion, an inherited limit versus a set success measure. Trap five, treating evaluation as a single step at the end, it's continual, before, mid and after. And trap six, answering collaboration questions with effort words like "everyone tried hard" instead of structural factors like defined roles and agreed communication. Six traps, each a boundary the exam probes.

QUESTION: Question one, the big integrated one. For a design project you have studied, analyse how research, management and evaluation worked together to improve the outcome. Six marks. This fuses three topics. Pause the player and write a full answer before you play, and the worked answer is in Listing 6.

NARRATOR: Model answer, using a reusable drink-bottle project. Research, management and evaluation worked together throughout. Market and user research, primary surveys plus secondary data, identified the need, a leak-proof reusable bottle, and informed the brief's measurable criteria. Project management then sequenced the work on a Gantt chart and tracked costs on a finance plan, so tasks like prototyping happened in the right order and within budget. Continual evaluation against the criteria caught a leaking lid during a mid-project test, which fed back to refine the design, and the management plan absorbed the extra time. By the final evaluation the bottle met all criteria. The three worked as a system: research set the targets, management kept the work on track, and evaluation checked progress and triggered refinements, together producing a better, on-time, on-budget outcome. Six marks: the marks come from linking the three, research informing the brief, management organising the work, evaluation driving refinement, not describing each in isolation.

QUESTION: Question two. Explain how a designer generates and then selects ideas during a design project. Four marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A designer generates ideas using creative thinking and structured techniques such as SCAMPER, which applies seven prompts, substitute, combine, adapt and so on, to an existing design, along with brainstorming and cognitive organisers like mind maps, to produce many ideas and break free of the obvious first one. The designer then switches to critical thinking to select among them, evaluating the ideas against the brief's criteria, often using a cognitive organiser such as a decision matrix to compare options objectively. So generation is divergent and creative, selection is convergent and critical. Four marks: name a generation technique like SCAMPER, name an evaluation or selection tool like a decision matrix, and explain the shift from generating to judging. The generate-then-judge structure is the insight.

QUESTION: Question three. Explain why both qualitative and quantitative research are valuable in designing a product. Four marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Quantitative research provides numerical data that can be counted and statistically analysed, such as how many people would buy a product or how much weight a component can bear, which proves trends and gives objective, measurable evidence. Qualitative research provides non-numerical data about people's feelings, opinions and reasons, such as why users find a product uncomfortable, which explains the numbers and reveals needs that statistics alone miss. Used together, the quantitative data establishes what is happening and the qualitative data explains why, giving the designer both a reliable measure and the understanding to act on it. Four marks: define each with its value, and explain that together they give both the measure and the reason. The marker wants the complementary relationship, not just two definitions.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain how project-management tools help a designer complete a project on time and on budget. Five marks. Pause and write a full answer.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Project-management tools coordinate the resources, time and money a project needs. An action plan lists every task so none is forgotten. A Gantt chart schedules those tasks against a timeline, showing their durations, which tasks depend on others, which can run in parallel, and the critical path, the chain of dependent tasks that sets the minimum project duration, so the designer sequences work efficiently and sees immediately if a delay threatens the deadline. A finance plan budgets all costs and tracks spending so the project stays viable. Crucially, these tools are used not just to plan but to monitor actual progress against the plan throughout, allowing the designer to re-sequence tasks or adjust the budget when something slips, keeping the project on time and on budget. Five marks: name the action plan, Gantt chart with the critical path, and finance plan, and stress the ongoing monitoring and re-planning. The monitoring point and the critical path lift it to full marks.

QUESTION: Question five, a trap-buster. Distinguish between market research and design (user) research. Two marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Market research investigates the marketplace, whether a product will sell, who the target market is, and what competitors offer, so it has a commercial focus. Design or user research investigates the users themselves, what they need, how they behave and use a product, so the design suits them. Both are research, but market research asks "will it sell and to whom", while user research asks "what does the user need". Two marks: define each by its focus and state the distinction. This is the module's headline trap, commercial viability versus user need.

QUESTION: Question six. Explain how a designer selects and justifies a communication technique for presenting to a client. Four marks. Pause the player and write a full answer before you play, and more model answers are in Listing 7.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A designer selects a communication technique by matching it to the audience, the message and the purpose, then justifies the choice against the criteria of clarity, appropriateness and ease of interpretation. For a non-technical client approving the look of a product, an orthographic drawing would be inappropriate, it is clear and precise for a manufacturer but hard for a layperson to interpret, so the designer instead selects a realistic rendered image or a three-dimensional model or virtual walkthrough, which communicates the finished appearance clearly and is easy for the client to understand. The choice is justified because it best satisfies all three criteria for that particular receiver. Four marks: explain selection by audience, message and purpose, name the CAE criteria, and justify a specific choice against them with a less-appropriate contrast. Naming the audience and the criteria is what lifts it.

QUESTION: Question seven. Explain why evaluation should be continual and how it links to the design brief. Four marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Evaluation should be continual because evaluating throughout design and production catches problems early, when they are cheap to fix, drives the refine loop so the design improves progressively, and keeps the project aligned with its targets, rather than discovering failures too late at the end. It links directly to the design brief because the brief, at the start, sets the measurable criteria for success, and the evaluation, both during and finally, judges the design against exactly those criteria, the two are bookends. This is why criteria must be measurable, they are the standard the continual evaluation tests against. Four marks: explain the benefits of continual evaluation, early detection, refinement, alignment, and the brief-to-evaluation bookend relationship with measurable criteria. Linking evaluation back to the brief is the high-band move that ties the module together.
