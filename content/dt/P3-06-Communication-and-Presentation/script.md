NARRATOR: Let's reset. Last episode, research methods, qualitative versus quantitative, the toolkit QISOTS, and research ethics, CVA. Before that, market research, then design briefs, then teams and creativity. Today's topic is what you do once you've researched and designed something, you have to communicate it, to clients, to your team, to a marker. The textbook says communication is at the core of a good design practice, and it's both a skill you use throughout the process and the final presentation stage of DRIPER. Get the design right but communicate it badly and the idea dies anyway. So today is how designers communicate and present ideas.

NARRATOR: Today's home topic is communication and presentation. The syllabus packs a lot in: forms of communication, the elements of the communication process, criteria for evaluating communication, visualising solutions, the purpose of prototypes and models, and presentation techniques. And the skills are to experiment with techniques and forms to visualise and communicate, and to justify the selection of communication techniques. By the end you'll be able to model the communication process, name the forms and the criteria, and explain how designers visualise and present ideas, and how technology overcomes communication barriers. Let me start with the model.

NARRATOR: The communication process has four core elements, and we'll hold them with the hook SMMR, in Listing 1: Sender, Message, Medium, Receiver. A Sender has a Message, which travels through a Medium, a drawing, an email, a spoken pitch, to a Receiver. And the whole point, the question the textbook says a designer should always ask, is "has my message been received and understood?" That introduces two more ideas: feedback, the receiver responding so the sender knows it landed, and noise, anything that interferes, a barrier. A communication barrier is interference anywhere in that chain, a mumbled pitch, a drawing too technical for the client, a language gap. So SMMR plus feedback, and a barrier is whatever stops the message getting through cleanly. That model is the spine of the topic.

NARRATOR: Next, the forms of communication, what medium the message takes, and we'll hold them with V-WGVA, in Listing 2: Verbal, spoken; Written, text; Graphical, charts and diagrams; Visual, drawings, images and models; and Audio, sound. Designers usually combine several. The skill the syllabus names is to experiment with a range of forms to find the one that communicates best for the message and the audience.

NARRATOR: How do you judge whether communication is good? The syllabus gives three criteria, and we'll hold them with CAE, in Listing 3: Clarity, is the message clear and unambiguous; Appropriateness, is the method suited to the audience and purpose; and Ease of interpretation, can the receiver understand it without effort. These criteria are how you evaluate or justify a communication choice, a technical orthographic drawing scores high on clarity for an engineer but low on ease of interpretation for a layperson client, so it's not appropriate for them. Clarity, appropriateness, ease, that's your evaluation framework.

NARRATOR: Now visualising solutions, the heart of design communication, because designers think and communicate largely through images, in Listing 4. The main visual forms, in rough order from rough to polished: a sketch, quick freehand to capture and explore an idea, fast and loose, perfect early on; an orthographic drawing, a formal technical drawing showing views and exact dimensions, precise, for manufacture; a rendered drawing, a realistic shaded image showing how it'll really look, for selling the look; and a model or prototype, a three-dimensional physical or digital representation. Each suits a different stage and audience: sketches for early ideation, orthographic drawings for the maker, renders and models for the client. And the purpose of prototypes and models is double, they communicate the design in three dimensions and they let you test it, which links straight back to last module's modelling and to testing in the research topic.

NARRATOR: Finally, presentation, choosing the technique that suits the audience. You present differently to a manufacturer who needs technical drawings than to a client who needs a persuasive render or walkthrough. The syllabus skill is to justify your choice, which is just applying the CAE criteria, this method is clear, appropriate to this audience, and easy for them to interpret.

QUESTION: Retrieval. Name the four elements of the communication process, and what a communication barrier is. Pause the player, answer both, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. The four elements are the sender, the message, the medium, and the receiver, SMMR, with feedback closing the loop. A communication barrier is anything that interferes with the message getting through clearly, such as a drawing too technical for the audience, a poorly worded message, or a physical or language obstacle, it's noise anywhere in the chain. If you got SMMR and "a barrier is interference that stops the message being received and understood", that's the mark.

NARRATOR: Now the headline question, which appeared in the recent exam: "Describe how technology can assist designers in overcoming communication barriers." We met this in module two from the technology side; here's the communication-model angle that completes it. Locate the barrier in the SMMR chain, then choose the form or technology that removes it. A barrier of distance between sender and receiver is overcome by a medium like videoconferencing or shared cloud files. A barrier where the receiver can't interpret the message, a client who can't read an orthographic drawing, is overcome by a clearer medium, a three-dimensional render or virtual walkthrough, raising ease of interpretation. A language barrier is reduced by visual rather than written forms. So you name the barrier, name the technology or form that overcomes it, and judge it against the CAE criteria. The weak-versus-strong contrast is in Listing 6, and the trap is "they just use computers", name the specific barrier and the specific fix.

NARRATOR: Let me make it concrete with the most useful worked example in this topic, one idea communicated four ways, in Listing 5. Say you've designed a chair. A sketch communicates the rough concept quickly to yourself and your team early on, fast but imprecise. An orthographic drawing communicates exact dimensions to the manufacturer, precise but unappealing and hard for a layperson to read. A rendered image communicates how the finished chair will look to the client, persuasive and easy to interpret but not buildable from. And a physical or digital model communicates the form in three dimensions and lets people test it. Same chair, four communication methods, each chosen for its audience and purpose, that's the experiment-with-forms skill, and judging each by clarity, appropriateness and ease of interpretation is the justify skill.

NARRATOR: One trap to flag. Students treat "communication" as just talking or writing and forget that for designers it's overwhelmingly visual, sketches, drawings, renders and models. And when asked to justify a communication choice, they say "it's clear" without naming the audience. Always justify against all three criteria, clarity, appropriateness to the specific audience, and ease of interpretation, an orthographic drawing is appropriate for an engineer but not for a child.

QUESTION: Question one. Identify the four elements of the communication process. Two marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The four elements are the sender, the message, the medium and the receiver, often shown with feedback from the receiver back to the sender. Two marks for the four elements, SMMR. Adding feedback and noise, the barrier, shows fuller understanding and protects you if a follow-up asks about barriers.

QUESTION: Question two. Identify three forms of communication a designer might use and give an example of each. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Any three of: visual, such as a sketch or render of a product; written, such as a design specification or report; verbal, such as a spoken presentation to a client; graphical, such as a chart of survey data; and audio. Three marks, one per form with a valid example. V-WGVA is your checklist, and for designers the visual forms, drawings and models, are the most important to mention.

QUESTION: Question three. Explain how a designer would select and justify an appropriate communication technique for a client. Five marks. This is the headline question, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A designer selects a communication technique by matching it to the audience, the message and the purpose, then justifies it against the criteria of clarity, appropriateness and ease of interpretation. For a client who is not technically trained and needs to approve the look of a product, an orthographic drawing would be inappropriate, because although it is clear and precise for a manufacturer, it is difficult for a layperson to interpret. Instead the designer would select a realistic rendered image or a three-dimensional model or virtual walkthrough, which communicates how the finished product will look clearly and is easy for the client to interpret, and is therefore appropriate for that audience and purpose. The choice is justified because it best satisfies all three criteria for that receiver. Five marks: explain selection by audience, message and purpose, name the three criteria, and justify a specific choice for the client against them, contrasting it with a less appropriate option. Naming the audience and the criteria is what lifts this.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain the purpose of a model or prototype in communicating a design. Two marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A model or prototype communicates a design in three dimensions, allowing the designer, client or team to see and understand the form, scale and function in a way a flat drawing cannot, and it also allows the design to be physically tested and evaluated. Two marks: state that it communicates the design three-dimensionally for better understanding, and that it doubles as a means of testing. The dual purpose, communicate and test, is the mark.

QUESTION: Question five. Explain why a designer might use several different forms of communication during one project. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A designer uses several forms because a single project involves different messages, audiences and stages, and no one form suits them all. Early on, quick sketches communicate rough ideas to the design team; for manufacture, precise orthographic drawings communicate exact dimensions to the maker; for client approval, realistic renders and models communicate the look and form persuasively; and a written specification records the technical requirements. By experimenting with and combining forms, the designer ensures each message is communicated with clarity, appropriateness and ease of interpretation for its particular audience. Three marks: explain that different stages, audiences and messages need different forms, give examples of forms matched to purposes, and link the choice to the communication criteria. The insight is that form is matched to audience and purpose, which is exactly the "experiment with a range of forms" skill the syllabus names.
