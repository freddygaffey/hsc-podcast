If you master one thing in Year 12, make it this.

NARRATOR: Today's home topic is the factors affecting design and the analysis of success and failure. The syllabus dot-points are the eleven factors, again, and "examples of success and failure in design", and the skills are to apply the factors to the development of your major design project, and to critically analyse examples of success and failure in design solutions. By the end you'll be able to apply the factors to your own project and, crucially, analyse why any design succeeded or failed through the lens of the factors. Let me start with the factors applied to your MDP.

NARRATOR: You already know the eleven factors, so a quick recap with the hook, FFA EQ NEW LOAd, in Listing 1: a design must do its job, function, needs, appropriateness; fit the user, aesthetics, ergonomics; be affordable and safe, finance, work health and safety; be well made, quality; and not wreck the planet, environmental consequences, obsolescence, life cycle analysis. In your MDP, these aren't abstract, every significant decision is weighed against them, and your success criteria from the proposal are really the factors made specific to your project. When you justify a decision in your folio, decision, reason, factor, evidence, the factor you name is one of these eleven. So the factors are the vocabulary of justification.

NARRATOR: Now the headline skill, H1.1: critically analyse examples of success and failure in design. Here's the core idea. A design succeeds when it satisfies the factors that matter for its need, especially the need itself, and fails when it falls short on one or more critical factors. So to analyse why a design succeeded or failed, you don't just say "it was good" or "it was bad", you identify the specific factor or factors that drove the outcome and judge the design against them. The Edsel didn't just "fail", it failed on the needs factor, it was the wrong car for the wrong market. That shift, from a verdict to a factor-based reason, is the entire skill, and it's in Listing 2.

NARRATOR: And this is where the case studies pay off, because they're your ready-made evidence, and the HSC loves real examples. We have five, told as full story episodes, and each illustrates a factor, in Listing 3. The Flow Hive, a beekeeping innovation, is the success story: it nailed the need, the timing and the marketing, the one triumph to anchor your answers. Then four instructive failures. The Ford Edsel, Ford's nineteen-fifties "sure thing", failed on the needs factor, the textbook notes call it the wrong car for the wrong market at the wrong time, it misread what buyers wanted and sold just sixty-four thousand units in its first year. The Apple Newton, an early handheld, is the noble failure: it failed on timing and technological obsolescence, the technology, especially its handwriting recognition, wasn't ready, it was simply too early. The Microsoft Zune failed on timing and marketing, a competent music player that arrived after the iPod had already locked up the market. And Central Park in Sydney, an award-winning green building, is the flawed case: it markets itself as sustainable but, on a real life cycle analysis, under-delivers, the gap between green image and measured environmental performance. Five stories, a factor for each, and a success to balance the failures.

NARRATOR: Notice the pattern: each failure is pinned to a factor. Edsel, needs. Newton, obsolescence and timing. Zune, timing and marketing. Central Park, environmental consequences and life cycle analysis. That's how you turn a story into an H1.1 answer, name the factor the design failed on. And the Flow Hive shows the flip side, success when the factors align. Keep one success and a flop for each major factor ready, and you can answer any success-and-failure question the exam asks.

QUESTION: Retrieval. What does it mean to critically analyse success or failure in design, and what's the difference from just describing a design as good or bad? Pause the player, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. To critically analyse success or failure means to identify the specific factors affecting design that caused the outcome and judge the design against them, reaching a reasoned verdict, for example, the Edsel failed because it did not meet the needs of its target market, the wrong car for the wrong market. Just calling a design "good" or "bad" asserts a verdict without the reason, and earns little. The difference is naming the factor and explaining how the design met or failed it. If you said "analysis names the factor and explains, description just judges", that's the mark, and it's the heart of the most examined topic in the course.

NARRATOR: Now the headline question: "Critically analyse an example of success and an example of failure in design." Here's the move. A weak answer says "the iPhone was a success because it was good, and the Edsel was a failure because it was bad", pure assertion, caps low. A strong answer analyses each against named factors: the Flow Hive succeeded because it met a genuine need with strong function and excellent marketing and timing; the Ford Edsel failed because it misread its market, failing the needs factor, and was mistimed against a shift to economical cars, reaching a clear verdict for each, supported by the real detail. A top answer contrasts them, the same factor, needs, that the Flow Hive nailed is the one the Edsel missed. The weak-versus-strong contrast is in Listing 5. Name the factor, judge against it, use the real example.

NARRATOR: Let me make it concrete with a factor-by-factor comparison, in Listing 4, of the Flow Hive against the Ford Edsel. Needs: the Flow Hive met a real need, harvesting honey without disturbing bees, the Edsel misread its market entirely. Function: the Flow Hive worked elegantly, the Edsel functioned but offered nothing the market wanted. Marketing and timing: the Flow Hive's crowdfunding campaign and timing were superb, the Edsel was over-hyped then mistimed against changing tastes. Finance: the Flow Hive's crowdfunding raised millions before production, the Edsel cost Ford a fortune for sixty-four thousand sales. Read down those factors and the verdict writes itself: the Flow Hive succeeded by satisfying the factors that mattered, the Edsel failed by missing the most important one, the need. That table is exactly the structure of a top-band answer.

NARRATOR: Let me weave the connections, including one trap-flag that's worth real marks. First, back to module one, episodes four and five, these are the same eleven factors, now used analytically. Second, and this is the headline trap of the whole HSC: do not confuse the eleven factors affecting design, H1.1, FFA EQ NEW LOAd, with the six factors affecting the success of an innovation, H3.1, which you'll meet in module H2 as TEMPLE, timing, emerging technology, marketing, political, legal-economic, era. They're different lists for different questions, and the exam often asks both in one paper, so keep them separate. Third, the case studies cash in across both, the Flow Hive is success for both the design factors here and the innovation factors in H2. And fourth, success and failure analysis links to evaluation, episode eleven, judging your own MDP's success against its criteria is the same skill turned on your own work.

NARRATOR: One trap beyond the FFA-versus-TEMPLE one. Students analysing success or failure list every factor mechanically, "it had good function, good aesthetics, good finance...". Don't. Identify the one or two factors that actually drove the outcome and analyse those deeply. The Edsel's story is the needs factor, lead with it, don't bury it under ten others. Focused, factor-driven analysis beats a checklist.

NARRATOR: Now the exam-style questions, real NESA format, get a pen. Four questions, model answers in Listing 6.

QUESTION: Question one. What does it mean to critically analyse a design solution? One mark. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. To critically analyse a design solution means to examine it against the factors affecting design, judging how well it meets each relevant factor and reaching a reasoned verdict on its success or failure, rather than simply describing or asserting that it is good or bad. One mark for capturing that it judges against the factors to reach a reasoned conclusion. The trap is treating "analyse" as "describe".

QUESTION: Question two. Identify a design that failed and explain which factor caused its failure. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The Ford Edsel failed primarily on the factor of needs. Despite extensive marketing and some genuine innovations, Ford misread the market and launched a medium-priced car just as buyers were shifting toward smaller, economical cars, making it the wrong car for the wrong market at the wrong time, and it sold only around sixty-four thousand units in its first year. Because it did not meet the genuine needs of its target market, the design failed commercially. Three marks: name a real failed design, identify the specific factor, here needs, and explain how the design failed that factor with real detail. The mark is in pinning the failure to a named factor, not "it just wasn't popular".

QUESTION: Question three. Critically analyse one example of success and one example of failure in design. Six marks. This is the headline question, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The Flow Hive is an example of success. It met a genuine, previously unsolved need, harvesting honey without disturbing the bees, with strong function, and its timing and marketing, a record-breaking crowdfunding campaign during heightened interest in bees, were excellent, so it satisfied the factors that mattered most and succeeded commercially and as a design. The Ford Edsel is an example of failure. Although well financed and innovative, it failed on the needs factor: Ford misread its market and launched a medium-priced car as tastes shifted to economical cars, the wrong car for the wrong market at the wrong time, selling only about sixty-four thousand units in its first year. Comparing the two, the very factor the Flow Hive satisfied, meeting a genuine market need, is the one the Edsel failed, which shows that correctly identifying and meeting the need is the single most decisive factor in a design's success or failure. Six marks: analyse a success and a failure each against named factors with real detail, reach a verdict for each, and ideally contrast them on a shared factor. Pure "good versus bad" caps around half.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain the difference between the factors affecting design and the factors affecting the success of an innovation. Two marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The factors affecting design are the eleven considerations a designer weighs when designing any product, such as function, needs, aesthetics, ergonomics, finance and environmental consequences, and they relate to outcome H1.1. The factors affecting the success of an innovation are a separate set of six, timing, emerging technologies, marketing, political, legal-economic and historical-cultural factors, that determine whether an innovation succeeds in the market, relating to outcome H3.1. The distinction is that the eleven design factors concern the design itself, while the six innovation factors concern the conditions surrounding an innovation's success. Two marks: identify the two as distinct sets with their focus, ideally naming a couple from each. This guards against the headline trap of confusing them.
