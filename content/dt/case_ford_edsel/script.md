NARRATOR: On the fourth of September, 1957, huge crowds turned out across America to see a car that had been teased for months. Ford called it E-Day. People had been driven half-mad with curiosity by an advertising campaign that, for weeks, refused to show them the actual car. And they came in their thousands. They looked. They pointed. And then, mostly, they went home without buying one. Within three years the car was dead. Its name has been a byword for failure ever since. This is the story of the Ford Edsel — possibly the most expensive flop in the history of the motor car, and the single best lesson in how a design can do everything by the book and still fail completely.

NARRATOR: Ford did not cut corners on this car. Quite the opposite. They spent ten years and two hundred and fifty million dollars developing it — and remember this is 1950s money. It was the first genuinely new car line the company had launched since the Mercury back in 1938, a twenty-year gap. They built it in eighteen different models. They poured research, design, tooling, and an entire new manufacturing division into it. On paper, this was the most carefully planned car anyone had ever made.

NARRATOR: And the research really did look airtight. Ford's studies showed a clear gap in the market for a medium-priced car to slot in against Chrysler's Dodge and DeSoto, and General Motors' Pontiac, Oldsmobile and Buick. The numbers said that by 1965, half of all American families would be earning five thousand dollars or more, and they'd be moving up into exactly this medium price bracket. The projection was that the Edsel could sell up to four hundred thousand cars a year. They even ran deeper studies to give the car the right "personality" — they decided it should be, and I quote, "the smart car for the younger executive or professional family on its way up." Ford was so meticulous they wrote a sixty-page memo just on how to choose an advertising agency, and rejected nineteen of them before picking one.

QUESTION: So with all that research and all that money, how on earth did it fail?

NARRATOR: Hold that question, because the answer is the whole point. Let's start with the marketing, because the marketing was, briefly, brilliant. In the spring of 1957 Ford launched a teaser campaign built entirely on curiosity. The early ads just said "The Edsel is Coming" — and showed nothing. No car. The mystery built and built. Later ads revealed only a glimpse: a shadow, the hood ornament. Everyone involved was sworn to secrecy. Dealers had to keep the car physically hidden or risk fines and losing their franchise. By E-Day the public anticipation was enormous.

NARRATOR: And that was the trap. When the curtain finally lifted, the car could not possibly live up to months of breathless mystery. The styling was strange and divisive. And there were deeper problems no amount of hype could cover. Start with the name. Ford set the Edsel up as a completely separate division and left the trusted Ford name off it entirely, so the car had no existing customer base to lean on. The advertising agency had actually handed Ford executives a list of eighteen thousand possible names. Ford ignored every single one and named the car after Edsel Ford — the founder Henry Ford's son. It's just not a name that rolls off the tongue. When someone asks what you drive, you want a name that sounds cool, or at least that they recognise. "Edsel" was neither. First-year sales: just sixty-four thousand cars, against a hope of four hundred thousand.

NARRATOR: Now the timing, and this is the killer. The flaw buried inside all that careful research was that it described the world of the early fifties, not the world the Edsel was actually born into. By 1957, the bloom was already off the medium-priced car. The next boom — the one the research had completely overlooked — was in small, compact cars. On top of that, the United States had slid into a recession. So Ford rolled out its expensive new medium-priced models at the exact moment buyers wanted cheaper and smaller, and while rival carmakers were discounting last year's stock. Right car, maybe, for 1953. Dead wrong for 1957.

NARRATOR: And then, the final insult: the car itself was badly built. Here's the irony — the Edsel had genuinely clever features for its time. A rolling-dome speedometer. Ergonomically designed controls. Self-adjusting brakes. And a futuristic push-button transmission called Teletouch, where you changed gears with buttons set in the centre of the steering wheel. But Ford made a fatal management decision: they launched the Edsel as a brand-new division without giving it its own factory. The cars were assembled by regular Ford workers, on Ford lines, and those workers resented building someone else's car. They took little pride in it. Quality control fell apart. Edsels turned up at dealerships with notes taped to the steering wheel listing the parts that hadn't been installed yet. And that fancy Teletouch transmission? It became the car's single biggest mechanical headache, made worse because dealership mechanics were never trained on the new technology.

QUESTION: So before I sum up — pause for a second and try to name the things that actually killed the Edsel. There's more than one.

NARRATOR: Right. It wasn't one mistake, it was a stack of them. The market research was thorough but read the wrong future — it nailed the needs of a market that had already moved on. The timing collided with a recession and a shift to compact cars. The branding threw away Ford's reputation and saddled the car with a clumsy name. The aesthetics divided people. And the quality and the manufacturing were a mess because the production was never properly set up. Ford struggled on for three years before admitting total defeat. As one analysis put it: when they designed the Edsel, the company was focused on the product, and not nearly interested enough in the market.

NARRATOR: That's the lesson, and it's why the Edsel is in this course. A design can be expensively researched, cleverly engineered, and brilliantly marketed, and still fail — if it answers a need that's already gone, arrives at the wrong moment, and isn't built well. When the exam asks you to analyse why a design failed, or how timing and market research and quality affect success, the Edsel gives you every factor in one story. We'll name those factors, one by one, in a later lesson.
