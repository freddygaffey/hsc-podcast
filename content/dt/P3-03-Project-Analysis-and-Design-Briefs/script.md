NARRATOR: Today's home topic is project analysis and design briefs. The syllabus lists design briefs, the appropriateness of design solutions, and criteria for evaluation and the factors to consider. And the skills are to formulate and analyse design briefs, identify the parameters of design, and identify the criteria for success. So this topic is about defining a project properly: what's the problem, what are the limits, and how will we know if we've succeeded. By the end you'll be able to explain what a design brief is, distinguish parameters from criteria, and write a brief with measurable success criteria. Let me start with the brief itself.

NARRATOR: A design brief is a statement of the problem to be solved, together with the parameters and criteria the solution must meet. It's formulated by first analysing the need carefully, the textbook stresses you must establish whether there's a genuine need, often through market research, before you commit. Think of the brief as the contract for the whole project: it defines what you're making, for whom, within what limits, and to what standard, and it's the document every later stage is judged against. A brief has three core parts, and we'll hold them with the hook NCC: the Need, the Constraints or parameters, and the Criteria for success. Need, constraints, criteria. That's in Listing 1, and it's the skeleton of any brief you write or analyse.

NARRATOR: Now the distinction that earns marks in this topic, parameters versus criteria, because the syllabus names both and students blur them. They're in Listing 2. A parameter is a limit or boundary the design must comply with, and crucially, parameters are usually inherited, they're obligations outside your control. The textbook's bedside-lamp example: the parameters might be the lamp's height, the diameter of the base, the voltage it runs off, two hundred and forty volts, and the socket type for the globe. You don't get to choose those, they're fixed by standards, the client, or the context, and for the design to succeed, all parameters must be addressed. Criteria, on the other hand, are the must-haves that define a successful design, and they're set rather than inherited. For the same lamp, criteria might be a particular switching mechanism like a touch sensor, a contemporary style, and the use of recycled materials. So the clean distinction: parameters are the boundaries you inherit and must work within; criteria are the success measures you set and must meet. Inherited limits versus chosen success measures.

NARRATOR: Those criteria come in two flavours the exam likes you to name: functional criteria, what the design must be able to do, and aesthetic criteria, how it must look and appeal. A good brief sets both, and they map straight onto two of our eleven factors from module one, function and aesthetics. The most important quality of a success criterion is that it should be measurable or checkable, so that later, at the evaluation stage, you can objectively test whether the design met it. A vague criterion like "it should be good" can't be evaluated; a criterion like "it must hold three-quarters of a litre and not leak when inverted" can. Functional and aesthetic criteria are in Listing 3, and writing measurable criteria is the single most useful brief-writing skill.

NARRATOR: One more useful distinction, the type of brief, in Listing 4. A brief can be open or closed. An open brief gives the designer broad freedom, it states a general need and lets the designer interpret it, more creative scope but less direction. A closed brief is tightly specified, with detailed parameters and criteria fixed in advance, more direction but less creative freedom. Most real briefs sit somewhere between. Knowing the two lets you answer a question about how much freedom a brief allows, and it connects to last episode, an open brief leaves more room for the creative techniques like SCAMPER.

QUESTION: Retrieval, because this distinction is examinable. What's the difference between a parameter and a criterion in a design brief? Pause the player, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. A parameter is a limit or boundary the design must comply with, usually inherited and outside the designer's control, such as a required size, voltage or standard. A criterion for success is a must-have that defines whether the design has succeeded, set by the designer or client, such as a required function, style or material, and it should be measurable so it can be evaluated. So parameters are the inherited boundaries you work within, and criteria are the success measures you'll be judged against. If you got "inherited boundary versus set success measure", that's the mark. They overlap in feel, but the marker wants the distinction.

NARRATOR: Let me connect this to appropriateness, the factor from module one, because the brief is what makes a solution appropriate. Recall appropriateness means the design is a suitable response to the identified need, for the specific user and context. The brief is precisely where that gets pinned down, by analysing the need and setting parameters and criteria around the real user and context, the brief ensures the eventual solution is appropriate rather than a generic guess. So a well-analysed brief is the guarantee of an appropriate solution, which is why formulating and analysing the brief is its own skill.

NARRATOR: Let me give you a worked brief, because writing one is the skill, and it's in Listing 5. Take a reusable drink bottle. The need: a durable, leak-proof, reusable bottle to reduce single-use plastic, for active adults. The parameters, the inherited limits: it must fit a standard car cup-holder, comply with food-safety standards, and come in under a set retail price. The criteria for success, the measurable must-haves: functionally, it must hold seven hundred and fifty millilitres, not leak when inverted, and keep a drink cold for at least six hours; aesthetically, it must look premium and come in a range of colours for the target market. Notice every criterion is checkable, which means at the evaluation stage you can test the bottle against each one and judge success objectively. Need, parameters, measurable criteria, that's a complete brief.

NARRATOR: Now the headline question: "Explain the purpose of a design brief and the criteria for success." Here's the move. A weak answer says "a design brief says what you have to make", which is true but thin and caps low. A strong answer explains that the brief is the formulated statement of the need plus the parameters and criteria, that it directs the entire project and ensures the solution is appropriate to the need, and that the criteria for success are the measurable must-haves, functional and aesthetic, against which the finished design is evaluated. The key insight, and the link that lifts the answer: the criteria you set in the brief are the same criteria you evaluate against at the end, the brief and the final evaluation are bookends. The weak-versus-strong contrast is in Listing 6.

NARRATOR: One trap to flag. Students write success criteria that aren't measurable, "the product should be good quality and look nice". Those can't be evaluated, so they're weak criteria. Always make criteria specific and checkable, "must withstand a one-metre drop test", "must cost under twenty-five dollars", "must be operable with one hand". Measurable criteria are what make the later evaluation objective, and the marker rewards them.

QUESTION: Question three. Explain the purpose of a design brief and explain why criteria for success should be measurable. Five marks. This is the headline question, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The purpose of a design brief is to define the project clearly: it states the need, the parameters the solution must comply with, and the criteria it must meet, and in doing so it directs every subsequent stage of the design process and ensures the solution is appropriate to the identified need. The criteria for success should be measurable because they are used at the evaluation stage to judge objectively whether the design has succeeded; a measurable criterion such as "must hold seven hundred and fifty millilitres and not leak when inverted" can be tested definitively, whereas a vague criterion such as "should be good quality" cannot, leaving evaluation subjective and unreliable. Measurable criteria therefore make the brief and the final evaluation work together as bookends of the project. Five marks: explain the brief's purpose of defining and directing the project and ensuring appropriateness, explain that measurable criteria enable objective evaluation, contrast measurable and vague criteria, and link the brief to the final evaluation. The connection between the brief and evaluation is the high-band insight.
