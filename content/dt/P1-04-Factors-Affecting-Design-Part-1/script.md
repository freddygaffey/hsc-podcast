A neat way to hold today's group: the first three are about the design doing its job, and the last two are about it fitting the user. Let me teach each one properly, with the syllabus meaning and how to write about it, because a factor you can't define is a factor you can't use in an answer. They're all defined in Listing 2.

NARRATOR: First factor, appropriateness of the design solution. The textbook definition: the design solution should be a manageable response to an identified need, problem or opportunity. It should answer the need, fix the problem, or capitalise on the opportunity. Two things make it appropriate: it must effectively perform the task it was designed for, and it must be acceptable to the target market and suited to the environment in which it's used. The classic example is a product for preschool children: an appropriate solution would use non-toxic materials, be durable, be light, have no sharp corners, use bright colours, and maybe large buttons with symbols instead of words. Notice that "appropriate" always means appropriate for someone and some context. The same product can be appropriate for one user and completely inappropriate for another. That's the key to writing about it: appropriateness is always relative to the end-user and the environment of use.

NARRATOR: Second, needs. Successful designs respond to genuine needs, so identifying a need is the natural starting point of the whole design process, which is why Define is the first stage of DRIPER. A need often shows up as a problem experienced by consumers that a new or improved product could solve. And the syllabus gives you a tool here, the needs analysis: a structured exploration of the problem, where you evaluate existing solutions, examine the consumers in the target market, and judge the potential for a future product. So "needs" as a factor isn't vague, it points to a real activity, the needs analysis, that we'll come back to in the project-analysis topic. The line to remember: design starts from a genuine, identified need, and a needs analysis is how you explore it.

NARRATOR: Third, function. Function refers to the ability of a product to perform the task for which it was designed, what it must be able to do to be successful. And here's the distinction the marker wants: every product has a primary function, its main purpose, the reason a consumer chooses it, and most also have secondary functions, additional features that differentiate it from competitors. That difference even has a name, the point of difference. The textbook's example is a dishwasher: its primary function is to wash and hygienically clean dinnerware and cutlery, but consumers often choose between dishwashers on secondary functions, water and energy efficiency, quiet operation, capacity, or finishes that match the kitchen. So when you analyse function, separate the primary function from the secondary functions. That split is in Listing 3, and naming a product's point of difference is an easy way to show you understand the factor.

NARRATOR: Fourth, aesthetics. Aesthetics refers to the physical appearance of a product and its visual appeal to the target market. How it looks, its form, colour, texture, proportion. And this is where the most famous trade-off in the whole subject lives, the balance between function and aesthetics, so let me set it up carefully because it's a guaranteed exam question. A product can function perfectly but look so unappealing that no one buys it, and a product can look stunning but fail to do its job. The textbook makes the point both ways: a design with poor aesthetics may not attract users, but a design that's all looks may not function. Great design balances the two for its target market. And crucially, which one matters more depends on the product and the user. For a surgeon's scalpel, function dominates. For a perfume bottle, aesthetics carries enormous weight. So the factor isn't "looks good", it's "achieves the right balance of function and aesthetics for this user and purpose".

NARRATOR: Fifth, and this is the one students define worst, so listen closely: ergonomics. The textbook defines ergonomics as the relationship between the human user and their physical or work environment. Its aim is to ensure that products, systems and workplaces are designed to fit the people intended to use them. Say that again, designed to fit the people who use them. Ergonomics draws on data from several disciplines: anthropometry, which is the study of human body sizes and shapes, biomechanics, which is muscles, levers and forces, and environmental physics, things like noise, light, heat and cold. Modern designers gather this with tools like 3D body scanners. The goal of good ergonomics is a product or environment that's comfortable, safe and therefore productive, and the warning the textbook gives is sharp: failure to consider ergonomics can cause discomfort, pain, or physical injury to the user. So ergonomics isn't a comfort luxury, it's the factor that keeps a design from hurting people, which is why it links straight to work health and safety in Part two.

NARRATOR: Let me make ergonomics concrete with the kind of worked reasoning that earns marks, because "it's about comfort" is a band-four answer and the data-driven version is band six. The worked example is in Listing 4. Suppose you're designing a chair, or a door handle, or a workbench. You can't design it for one person, because humans vary in size. So designers use anthropometric data, tables of body measurements across a population, and the standard principle is to design for the range from roughly the fifth percentile to the ninety-fifth percentile. The fifth percentile is a small user, only five percent of people are smaller, and the ninety-fifth percentile is a large user, only five percent are larger. Design to fit that range and you accommodate about ninety percent of users. So a seat height, a handle reach, a doorway width gets set from anthropometric tables, not guessed. That's what it means to say ergonomics uses anthropometry: you're sizing the design to real measured human bodies. If an exam asks how ergonomic data is used in design, that fifth-to-ninety-fifth-percentile reasoning is the answer that scores.

QUESTION: Retrieval time, and this is a guaranteed exam shape. In one sentence each, what's the difference between function and aesthetics as factors, and why is the balance between them important? Pause the player, answer both, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. Function is the ability of a product to perform the task it was designed for, while aesthetics is its physical appearance and visual appeal to the target market. The balance matters because a product that functions well but looks unappealing may not attract users, and one that looks good but functions poorly fails its purpose, so a successful design achieves the right balance of the two for its specific user and context. If you got function and aesthetics defined and said the balance depends on the product and user, that's the mark. Don't just say "they're both important", say what each is and why the balance is product-specific.

NARRATOR: Now the headline extended-response shape for these factors: "Analyse how function and aesthetics were balanced in a product you have studied." This is where students underperform, so here's the move. A weak answer says "the product looks good and works well", which asserts a balance without analysing it, and caps low. A strong answer does four things: it defines both factors, it names the specific design decisions where the two were in tension, it explains which way the balance was struck and why that suited the target market, and it reaches a judgement on whether the balance was appropriate. For example, a smartphone: the aesthetic pull toward a thin, seamless glass body is in direct tension with the function of battery life and durability, and the designer's choice of where to land that trade-off is exactly what "analyse the balance" is asking you to dissect. NARRATOR: One trap before we recap. Students treat "appropriateness" as a vague catch-all and write nothing specific. Don't. Appropriateness is always relative, appropriate for which user, in which environment? Tie it to the target market and the context of use, the preschool-children example is the model, and it stops being woolly. Generic "it's appropriate" earns nothing; "it's appropriate for preschool children because the non-toxic materials and rounded corners suit their safety needs" earns the mark.

NARRATOR: Now the exam-style questions, real NESA format, get a pen. Four questions, model answers in Listing 6.

QUESTION: Question one. Define ergonomics and explain why it is an important factor in design. Three marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Ergonomics is the relationship between the human user and their physical or work environment, and it aims to ensure that products, systems and workplaces are designed to fit the people intended to use them, using data such as anthropometry, the study of body sizes and shapes. It is important because a well-designed, ergonomic product is comfortable, safe and productive, whereas failing to consider ergonomics can cause the user discomfort, pain or physical injury. Three marks: define ergonomics as the user-environment fit, mention it uses human measurement data, and explain its importance through comfort and safety versus injury. The trap is defining ergonomics as just "comfort", it's the fit between the user and the design, measured with real data.

QUESTION: Question two. Distinguish between the primary and secondary functions of a product, using an example. Two marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The primary function is the main purpose for which a product is selected, while secondary functions are additional features that differentiate it from competitors, sometimes called its point of difference. For example, the primary function of a dishwasher is to wash and hygienically clean dishes, while secondary functions include water and energy efficiency, quiet operation and capacity. Two marks: define primary versus secondary function, and give an example showing both. The marker wants the distinction plus a worked example, not just two definitions floating free.

QUESTION: Question three. Analyse how the factors of function and aesthetics were balanced in a product you have studied. Five marks. This is the headline extended response, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer, using a smartphone as the studied product. Function, the product's ability to perform its task, demands a large battery, durability and effective heat management, while aesthetics, its visual appeal to the target market, pulls toward a thin, lightweight, seamless glass body. These two factors are in direct tension: a thinner, more attractive body reduces internal space for the battery and makes the glass more fragile. The designer balanced them by prioritising a premium, slim aesthetic to appeal to a style-conscious target market, while compensating for the functional cost through more efficient components and fast charging. This balance is appropriate for that market, which values appearance highly, though it represents a real trade-off against battery life and durability. Five marks: define both factors, identify the specific design tension between them, explain which way the balance was struck and why it suits the target market, and judge whether the balance was appropriate. The weak version just says "it looks good and works well", with no tension and no judgement, and caps around half marks.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain why appropriateness is always considered in relation to the end-user. Two marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Appropriateness means the design solution is a suitable, manageable response to the need, and what counts as suitable depends entirely on who will use it and in what environment, so the same solution can be appropriate for one user and inappropriate for another. For example, bright colours, non-toxic materials and rounded corners make a product appropriate for preschool children, but those same features would be inappropriate for a professional surgical instrument. Two marks: explain that appropriateness is relative to the end-user and context, and give an example showing the same factor judged differently for different users. The trap is treating appropriateness as an absolute property of the product rather than a relationship to its user.
