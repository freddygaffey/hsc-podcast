### Listing 2 — Part 1 factors defined (does its job / fits the user)
| Factor | Definition | How to write about it |
|---|---|---|
| Appropriateness | A manageable, suitable response to the need/problem/opportunity; performs the task and is acceptable to the target market & environment of use | Always **relative to the end-user and context** (e.g. preschool: non-toxic, durable, no sharp corners, bright colours, symbol buttons) |
| Needs | Successful designs respond to genuine needs; the design's starting point | Name the **needs analysis**: evaluate existing solutions, examine the target market, judge future potential |
| Function | The ability of a product to perform the task it was designed for | Split **primary function** (main purpose) from **secondary functions** (extra features = **point of difference**) |
| Aesthetics | The physical appearance of a product and its visual appeal to the target market | Discuss the **function↔aesthetics balance**, struck differently per product/user |
| Ergonomics | The relationship between the human user and their physical/work environment — designing to **fit the people who use it** | Cite the data: **anthropometry** (body sizes), **biomechanics** (forces), environmental physics; failure → discomfort/injury |

### Listing 3 — Function: primary vs secondary (dishwasher example)
| | Function | Example (dishwasher) |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | The main purpose the product is chosen for | Wash & hygienically clean dinnerware, cutlery, utensils |
| Secondary | Additional features that differentiate it (the **point of difference**) | Water/energy efficiency, quiet operation, capacity, finishes matching kitchen decor |

### Listing 4 — Ergonomics & anthropometry: the worked reasoning
```text
Ergonomics draws on:
  - Anthropometry        = human body sizes & shapes (the key data for sizing a design)
  - Biomechanics         = muscles, levers, forces
  - Environmental physics = noise, light, heat, cold, radiation
  (gathered with tools like 3D body scanners)

DESIGNING TO FIT A POPULATION — the percentile principle:
  Humans vary, so you cannot design for one person. Use anthropometric tables and design for
  the 5th to 95th percentile:
     5th percentile  = a small user (only 5% of people are smaller)
     95th percentile = a large user (only 5% of people are larger)
  -> accommodates ~90% of users.
  Applied to: seat height, handle reach, doorway width, workbench height, control placement.

Aim: comfortable, safe, productive.   Failure -> discomfort, pain, physical injury.
(Ergonomics links forward to Work Health & Safety — Part 2 & the production module.)
```

### Listing 5 — Headline question: "Analyse how function and aesthetics were balanced"
```text
WEAK answer (caps ~half marks):
  "The product looks good and works well." -> asserts a balance, never analyses the tension.

STRONG answer (band ~6):
  1. DEFINE both factors (function = performs its task; aesthetics = appearance/appeal).
  2. NAME the specific design tension (e.g. thin glass body vs battery size & durability).
  3. EXPLAIN which way the balance was struck and WHY it suits the target market.
  4. JUDGE whether the balance was appropriate (the verb is "analyse").
```

### Listing 6 — Model answers to the closing exam questions
```text
Q1 (3 marks) — Define ergonomics and why it matters.
  Ergonomics = the user↔environment relationship; designing products/systems/workplaces to fit
  the people who use them, using data like anthropometry. Important: ergonomic design is
  comfortable, safe & productive; failure causes discomfort/pain/injury. (Not just "comfort".)

Q2 (2 marks) — Primary vs secondary function, with example.
  Primary = main purpose chosen for; secondary = differentiating extra features (point of
  difference). Dishwasher: primary = clean dishes; secondary = efficiency, quiet, capacity.

Q3 (5 marks) — Analyse how function & aesthetics were balanced in a product.
  Smartphone: function (battery, durability, heat) vs aesthetics (thin seamless glass) are in
  tension; designer prioritised slim premium look for a style-conscious market, offsetting the
  functional cost with efficient components/fast charging; appropriate for that market but a real
  trade-off. Define both, name the tension, explain & justify the balance, judge it.

Q4 (2 marks) — Why is appropriateness judged relative to the end-user?
  "Suitable" depends on who uses it and where; the same solution can be appropriate for one user,
  inappropriate for another (bright colours/rounded corners suit preschoolers, not a surgical
  instrument). Appropriateness is a relationship to the user, not an absolute property.
```
