NARRATOR: Today's home topic is research methods, data and ethics. The syllabus lists a lot here: qualitative and quantitative research; questionnaires, surveys, interviews, observation, tests and experiments; statistical analysis; information research from print and electronic sources; interpreting and presenting data; and ethics in research. And the skills are to select and use a variety of research methods, and to analyse, interpret and apply research data. By the end you'll be able to distinguish qualitative from quantitative research, name and choose the right methods, present and interpret data, and apply research ethics. Let me start with the big split.

NARRATOR: The most important distinction in research is qualitative versus quantitative, and it's in Listing 1. Qualitative research gathers data about feelings, opinions and reasons, the why behind behaviour. It's usually presented in text, it draws out rich, valid opinions, but it can't be counted or turned into statistics. Interviews are the classic qualitative method. Quantitative research gathers data that can be counted and measured, the how many and how much. It's analysed statistically and presented in graphs and tables. Surveys with numerical questions are the classic quantitative method. The key point the textbook makes: validating a design usually needs more than just qualitative data, you want both, the numbers to prove a trend and the opinions to explain it. Qualitative is why, quantitative is how many.

NARRATOR: Now the methods themselves, and we'll hold them with the hook QISOTS, in Listing 2. Q, Questionnaires, a set of written questions, which can be open, inviting free answers, or closed, with fixed options, good for reaching many people. I, Interviews, a conversation where the interviewer asks questions, formal or informal, rich qualitative depth but slow. S, Surveys, a systematic set of questions, often numerical, good for quantitative data across a sample. O, Observation, watching how people actually behave or use a product, real behaviour rather than what people say they do. T, Tests and experiments, measuring how a design or material actually performs under conditions. And the second S, Statistical analysis, processing quantitative data to find patterns and trends. QISOTS. Plus the syllabus adds information research, gathering from print and electronic sources, which is your secondary research from last episode. So QISOTS covers the gathering, and you choose among them by what your question needs.

NARRATOR: That choosing is the real skill, selecting the method to suit the question. If your question is "how strong is this joint", that's a test or experiment, quantitative. If it's "do users find this comfortable", that's an interview or observation, qualitative. If it's "how many people would buy this", that's a survey, quantitative across a sample. The method follows the question, and a good design project uses a variety, that's exactly what the syllabus says. Matching questions to methods is in Listing 3, and it's the most examinable skill in this topic.

NARRATOR: Once you've gathered data, you have to interpret and present it, another syllabus point, in Listing 4. Quantitative data goes into tables, graphs and spreadsheets so patterns are visible and can be statistically analysed; qualitative data is summarised and grouped by theme. Two quality words the textbook stresses, and the exam rewards: validity, whether the research actually measures what it claims to, and reliability, whether it would give consistent results if repeated. Research with a tiny or biased sample is neither valid nor reliable, so designers use adequate, representative samples and avoid leading questions. And crucially, you must analyse, interpret and apply the data, research only has value when its findings change a design decision, that's the link to the whole process.

NARRATOR: Last syllabus point, ethics in research, and it matters whenever you involve people, which design research usually does. We'll hold the core ethics with the hook CVA, in Listing 5. C, informed Consent, participants must know what the research involves and agree to take part. V, Voluntary participation, no one is pressured, and they can withdraw. A, Anonymity and privacy, you protect participants' identities and personal information. Add to those: honesty, you report findings truthfully and don't fabricate or cherry-pick data, and no harm, the research mustn't endanger anyone. Ethics isn't optional politeness, it's a syllabus requirement and increasingly a legal one, and a folio that surveys people should note how consent and privacy were handled.

QUESTION: Retrieval. What's the difference between qualitative and quantitative research, and name one method for each? Pause the player, answer both, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. Qualitative research gathers non-numerical data about feelings, opinions and reasons, the why, typically through interviews, and is presented as text. Quantitative research gathers numerical data that can be counted and statistically analysed, the how many, typically through surveys, and is presented in graphs and tables. If you got "qualitative is opinions and reasons via interviews, quantitative is numbers via surveys", that's the mark. The best designs use both, the numbers and the reasons behind them.

NARRATOR: Now the headline question, straight from the recent exam: "Outline how testing and experimenting can improve a design project." Students answer this thinly, so here's the move. A weak answer says "you test it to see if it works", which is true but shallow and caps low. A strong answer treats testing and experimenting as a research method that produces data: by testing a prototype or experimenting with materials, the designer gathers objective evidence about how the design actually performs against the success criteria, problems identified through testing are then fed back to refine the design, the evaluate-and-refine loop of DRIPER, leading to a better final solution. And where testing involves people, ethics, consent and safety, apply. The weak-versus-strong contrast is in Listing 7. Frame testing as evidence that drives the design loop, not just a final check.

NARRATOR: Let me make method selection concrete, in Listing 6. Three research questions for a new chair. Question one, how much weight can the frame hold? That's a test or experiment, you load it and measure, quantitative, present as a table. Question two, do users find it comfortable over a long period? That's observation and interviews, qualitative, you watch people use it and ask them why. Question three, how many people in the target market would buy it and at what price? That's a survey across a sample, quantitative, present as a graph. Same project, three questions, three different methods, each chosen to fit, and the data interpreted and applied to refine the chair. That's research methods working as a system.

QUESTION: Question one. Distinguish between qualitative and quantitative research. Three marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Qualitative research gathers non-numerical data about people's feelings, opinions and reasons, exploring the why behind behaviour, and is typically collected through interviews and presented as text. Quantitative research gathers numerical data that can be counted and statistically analysed, the how many and how much, typically collected through surveys and presented in graphs and tables. The distinction is that qualitative data is descriptive and explains, whereas quantitative data is measurable and proves trends, and effective research often combines both. Three marks: define each with its data type and a method, and state the distinction. The marker wants opinions-versus-numbers, ideally noting both are used together.

QUESTION: Question two. Identify three research methods a designer could use and the type of data each produces. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Any three, for example: an interview, which produces qualitative data about users' opinions and reasons; a survey, which produces quantitative data that can be statistically analysed; and observation, which produces qualitative data about how people actually behave or use a product. Other valid methods include questionnaires, tests and experiments, and statistical analysis. Three marks, one per method correctly paired with its data type. Use QISOTS so you never blank, and pairing each method with qualitative or quantitative is what earns the marks.

QUESTION: Question three. Outline how testing and experimenting can improve a design project. Four marks. This is the headline question, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Testing and experimenting are research methods that produce objective evidence about how a design actually performs, rather than relying on assumption. By testing a prototype or experimenting with materials against the success criteria, for example load-testing a joint or comparing how two materials wear, the designer gathers data that reveals weaknesses or confirms strengths. These findings are then interpreted and fed back to refine the design, the evaluate-and-refine loop, so problems are corrected before final production. This makes the final solution more reliable, safer and better suited to the need. Four marks: state that testing produces objective performance data, that it tests against criteria, that the findings are fed back to refine the design, and that this improves the outcome. The shallow answer, "you test it to check it works", caps low; framing testing as evidence driving the design loop scores.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain why ethics must be considered when conducting research involving people. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Ethics must be considered because research involving people can affect their rights and wellbeing, so designers have a responsibility to protect participants. This means obtaining informed consent so participants understand and agree to take part, ensuring participation is voluntary so no one is pressured and they may withdraw, and protecting participants' anonymity and privacy. Researchers must also report findings honestly without fabricating or selectively using data, and ensure no harm comes to participants. Without these protections, research is unethical and its results may also be unreliable or invalid. Three marks: identify consent, voluntary participation and privacy, and add honesty or no harm, explaining that ethics protects participants and supports valid research. The CVA hook gives you the structure.
