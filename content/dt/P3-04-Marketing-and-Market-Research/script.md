NARRATOR: Today's home topic is marketing and market research. The syllabus names three things: the purpose of market research, the sources of data and information-gathering techniques, and the marketing environment. And the skill is to conduct market research to identify needs and opportunities. So this is about understanding the market before and during design. By the end you'll be able to state the purpose of market research, distinguish its two source types, analyse a market, and explain how research identifies needs and opportunities, with the cautionary tale of what happens when you read it wrong. Let me start with purpose.

NARRATOR: The purpose of market research is to find out what the marketplace wants, and the textbook calls this one of the most interesting kinds of research you can do, because the results may surprise you. Specifically, market research does four jobs: it confirms whether there's a genuine need, so you're not designing something nobody wants; it identifies opportunities, gaps in the market a new product could fill; it identifies and understands the target market, who will buy this; and it analyses the competition, what already exists. Those four purposes are in Listing 1. And one distinction worth making: market research asks "will it sell, and to whom", whereas user or design research asks "what does the user actually need and how do they use it". Both matter, and they overlap, but market research has that commercial edge.

NARRATOR: Now the key distinction in this topic, the one the exam tests: the two source types, primary and secondary research. They're in Listing 2. Primary research is conducted by going directly to the source, you gather the data yourself, fresh. Methods include surveys, interviews, telemarketing, observation and experiments. Primary research is current and specific to your project, but it costs time and money. Secondary research is data obtained from sources other than the original, information someone else has already collected. Sources include the Australian Bureau of Statistics, the CSIRO, Choice magazine, chambers of commerce, reports, books and the internet. Secondary research is cheaper and faster, but it's not tailored to your exact question and may be dated. The clean line: primary is data you collect yourself, secondary is data someone else already collected. And the textbook's warning, always correctly source your secondary research, and you may need permission to use it.

NARRATOR: To research a market, you first identify your target market, and you do that through segmentation, dividing the population into demographics, sections grouped by common characteristics. The categories the textbook lists are age, income, profession, geographic location and gender. Define your demographic and you know who you're designing for, which feeds straight into the appropriateness factor and the brief. Segmentation is in Listing 3.

NARRATOR: The marketing environment, the third syllabus point, is largely about understanding the competition, the existing designs in the market, and the textbook gives a clean framework for it: the Five Ps. Product, what the competition offers, features, materials, construction; Price, what it sells for; Promotion, how it's marketed and advertised; Place, where and how it's sold and distributed; and Packaging, how it's presented. Analyse competitors across the Five Ps and you find where you can do better, more features, lower price, a sustainability angle, a gap they've missed, which is exactly how you spot an opportunity. The Five Ps are in Listing 4, and they're a ready-made structure for any "how would you research the market" answer.

QUESTION: Retrieval. What's the difference between primary and secondary market research, and give one example of each. Pause the player, answer both, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. Primary research is data you collect yourself, directly from the source, for example a survey or interview you conduct with potential customers. Secondary research is data someone else has already collected, for example statistics from the Australian Bureau of Statistics or a Choice magazine product review. Primary is fresh and specific but costly; secondary is cheap and fast but general and possibly dated. If you got "collect yourself versus already collected by others" with an example each, that's the mark. The trap is mixing them up, the test is always who originally gathered the data.

NARRATOR: Now the most important lesson in this topic, and it's a story: market research read wrongly is as dangerous as no research at all. The classic case, which we'll tell in full as a case study, is the Ford Edsel. In the nineteen fifties Ford spent a fortune on market research, then launched the Edsel as a sure thing, and it became one of the most famous flops in history. The research wasn't simply absent, it was misread and overtaken, by the time the car launched, the market had shifted toward smaller, economical cars, and the Edsel's styling and positioning missed what buyers actually wanted. The lesson the exam wants you to draw: research must be accurate, current and correctly interpreted, identifying the need is only valuable if you read it right. Contrast that with the Flow Hive, the beekeeping innovation, whose creators used a crowdfunding campaign as a form of live market research and validation, the public's massive financial response confirmed the need before mass production. Both stories are in Listing 5, the Edsel for research misread, the Flow Hive for research validated.

And a top answer adds the caveat, that the research must be correctly interpreted, citing the Edsel as what happens when it isn't. The weak-versus-strong contrast is in Listing 6. QUESTION: Question one. Distinguish between primary and secondary market research. Three marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Primary market research is data collected directly by the designer from the original source, for example through surveys, interviews or observation of potential customers. Secondary market research is data obtained from sources other than the original, that someone else has already collected, for example statistics from the Australian Bureau of Statistics, Choice magazine reviews, or competitor reports. The distinction is that primary research is gathered first-hand and is current and specific but costly, whereas secondary research is gathered from existing sources and is cheaper and faster but more general. Three marks: define each with an example, and state the distinction, collected yourself versus already collected, with the trade-off. The marker checks who originally gathered the data.

QUESTION: Question two. Explain the purpose of market research in the design process. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The purpose of market research is to find out what the marketplace wants so that a designer develops a product people will actually buy. It confirms whether there is a genuine need, identifies opportunities or gaps in the market that a new design could fill, identifies and defines the target market the product is aimed at, and analyses competitors to find where the design can do better. This research informs the design brief and helps ensure the final solution is appropriate and commercially successful. Three marks: state that it identifies needs and opportunities, that it defines the target market and analyses competitors, and that it informs the brief and commercial success. The breadth, not just "to see what people want", is what scores.

QUESTION: Question three. Explain how market research can be used to identify needs and opportunities. Five marks. This is the headline question, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A designer identifies needs and opportunities by combining primary and secondary market research. Primary research, such as surveys, interviews and observation of the target market, reveals directly what consumers want and what problems they experience, an unmet want is a need. Secondary research, such as industry statistics and competitor analysis using the Five Ps, product, price, promotion, place and packaging, reveals where existing products fall short, and a gap the competition has missed is an opportunity. Segmenting the market into demographics ensures the research targets the right consumers. However, the research must be accurate and correctly interpreted, the Ford Edsel failed despite extensive research because the market had shifted and the findings were misread, so identifying a need is only valuable if it is read correctly. Five marks: explain primary and secondary research with techniques, connect an unmet need to a need and a market gap to an opportunity, mention segmentation, and add the interpretation caveat with an example. That caveat is the high-band move.

QUESTION: Question four. Identify the Five Ps used to analyse competitors in the marketing environment. Two marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The Five Ps are product, price, promotion, place and packaging. Two marks for naming all five. A useful upgrade: note that analysing competitors across these five reveals where a new design can do better, more features in the product, a lower price, better promotion, wider availability through place, or more appealing packaging, which is how an opportunity is identified.
