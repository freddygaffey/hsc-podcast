NARRATOR: Welcome to Design and Technology. This is the very first episode of the whole course, so before we start, one promise about how these episodes earn you marks. Design and Technology looks like a soft subject. It is not. It is a subject with its own precise vocabulary, and the single biggest thing separating a band six from a band four is whether you use that vocabulary deliberately instead of talking about design in everyday words. Markers reward the right term. So from this first episode, we are going to name things properly. Today's job is to answer a question that sounds obvious but is worth real marks when you get it right: what actually is design? And the answer the syllabus wants comes in two halves, design theory and design practice. By the end of this episode you'll be able to explain what design is, explain the difference between art and design in a way that earns marks, define design theory and design practice and the relationship between them, and name the range of design professions this course is about. There's no prior episode to recap yet, so we start clean.

NARRATOR: Here's the hook. Look around the room you're in. Almost nothing you can see happened by accident. The chair you're sitting on, the phone in your hand, the layout of the building, the typeface on a sign, the packaging on whatever's nearest. Every one of those was decided by a person who was trying to solve a problem for somebody else. That is design. The Cambridge text and the course notes put it simply: design is everywhere, it shapes the way we live, work, travel and communicate, and designers respond to society's needs and wants. Hold onto that phrase, needs and wants, because the whole subject orbits it.

NARRATOR: Now the core idea, said the way you'll write it for marks. Design is purposeful problem-solving. It is the deliberate process of creating a product, a system, or an environment to meet an identified need or opportunity. Almost every word in that sentence is doing work, so let's slow down and unpack three parts of it, because each part is examinable on its own.

NARRATOR: First, purposeful problem-solving. Design always has an intention behind it, and there is always a problem, a need or an opportunity, that the design exists to address. A designer is, in the words of your course notes, a problem-solver. Write that down. When an exam asks you what a designer does, "a designer is a problem-solver who creates solutions to meet identified needs and opportunities" is a sentence that scores, where "a designer makes nice-looking things" is a sentence that doesn't.

NARRATOR: Second, the difference between a need and an opportunity, because the syllabus uses both words and they are not the same. A need is a problem that already exists and is waiting to be solved: people in wheelchairs can't reach a high shelf, so there's a need for a solution. An opportunity is a gap a designer spots and chooses to pursue, often before anyone's complaining: nobody demanded a smartphone before it existed, but there was an opportunity to combine a phone, a camera and a computer. Needs are problems you respond to; opportunities are possibilities you reach for. Good designers do both. And notice the word "identified", because it matters: the need or opportunity is found and named first, deliberately, not stumbled into halfway through. Finding and defining that need is literally the first stage of the design process, and we'll come back to it.

NARRATOR: Third, the thing being designed: a product, a system, or an environment. The syllabus uses those three words on purpose, because design is not only about objects, and a question that says "design a solution" might mean any of the three. A product is a physical object: a chair, a water bottle, a pair of headphones. A system is a set of parts and steps organised to do a job: a recycling system in a school, a queue-management system in a café, an app's checkout process. An environment is a designed space: a bedroom layout, a playground, a retail store interior. Same design process, three different kinds of outcome. When you write about design, use whichever of those three words fits, because saying "this product" when you actually designed an environment is the kind of loose wording that quietly costs you. Product, system, or environment. They're set out in Listing 1.

NARRATOR: Let me sharpen all of this with the comparison the syllabus loves, design versus art. This is the cleanest way to understand what design really is, by holding it up against something it's often confused with. Picture two chairs. The first is a chair in a furniture showroom: someone designed it to be sat on comfortably, made affordably, and to look good in a living room. The second is a sculpture of a chair in an art gallery, made of melted glass, that would shatter if you sat on it. Both involved creativity. Both took skill. But they are fundamentally different activities, and the difference is the marker's favourite distinction. Art is primarily self-expression: the artist makes it to express an idea or an emotion, and it answers to the artist. Design is primarily problem-solving for someone else: the designer makes it to meet a user's need, and it answers to that user and that need. Art can be whatever the artist wants. Design has to work: it has constraints, a function, a client, a budget. The full contrast is laid out in Listing 2. The line to remember: art is made to be expressed; design is made to be used.

NARRATOR: One refinement so you don't oversimplify, because the smart version of this distinction earns more. It's not that design has no aesthetics and art has no function. A beautifully designed chair is still partly an aesthetic object, and a piece of architecture can be both art and design at once. The distinction is about the primary purpose and who it answers to. Design's first job is to solve the problem for the user; if it also looks wonderful, even better. Art's first job is to express; if it's also useful, that's a bonus. Lead with primary purpose and you've got the nuance markers reward.

QUESTION: Quick one before we go further, and this is the kind of distinction the HSC opens a paper with. In one sentence, what's the key difference between art and design? Pause the player, say it out loud, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. Art is primarily concerned with self-expression and answers to the artist, whereas design is primarily concerned with solving an identified problem or need and answers to the user. Notice the contrast word "whereas". When an exam says "distinguish between" or "compare", that little word is what turns two separate facts into an answer that earns the mark. If you said something like "design is more useful", that's the right instinct but the wrong words. The mark is in "self-expression" versus "solving an identified need".

NARRATOR: Now the part the syllabus actually names, and the part students usually can't state cleanly: design theory and design practice. The very first content dot-point of this course is "design theory and practice", so let's define both properly, because this is the heart of today.

NARRATOR: Design theory is the body of knowledge, the principles, the concepts and the ideas that inform how design is done. It's the understanding behind the doing. What's actually in it? Quite a lot, and you'll meet it across this course: the principles of good design, the elements and principles of visual design like line, shape, colour, balance and proportion, the knowledge of materials and their properties, the factors a designer must consider, and the structure of the design process itself. All of that knowledge is design theory. It's everything a designer knows that guides their decisions before and during a project.

NARRATOR: Design practice is the application of that theory in the real world: the actual doing of design. Researching a need, sketching ideas, building prototypes, testing them, refining them, and producing a real solution for a real user. If theory is what you know, practice is what you do with it. A designer with theory but no practice is someone who can talk about design but has never made anything work; a designer with practice but no theory is someone making decisions by guesswork. Real design needs both. Theory is what you know; practice is what you do with it. Listing 3 sets the two side by side.

NARRATOR: But here's the bit that lifts the answer from a band four to a band six, and it's exactly the headline exam question for this episode: explain the relationship between design theory and design practice. A weak answer just says "theory is the ideas and practice is the making" and stops. True, but it's a bare restatement, and it earns very little, because it misses the whole point, which is that the two are not separate stages, they feed each other. Theory disciplines practice: a designer's knowledge of materials and factors stops them from making naive decisions. And practice tests theory: when you actually build the thing, you discover whether the principles held up, and that real-world result feeds back and refines your understanding for next time. It's a two-way loop, not a one-way street. The strong answer names that feedback. Let me give it to you in full, because this wording recurs.

NARRATOR: Here's the band-six version. Design theory is the knowledge and principles that inform design decisions, while design practice is the application of that knowledge to create real solutions. The two are interdependent: theory guides and disciplines practice, ensuring decisions are informed rather than arbitrary, while practice tests and refines theory, because the outcomes of real projects reveal which principles work and feed back to improve the designer's understanding. A skilled designer constantly moves between the two. That's the answer. Hear how it does three things the weak one didn't: it defines both terms, it names the relationship as interdependent and two-way, and it gives the mechanism, theory informs practice, practice refines theory. The weak-versus-strong pair is written out in Listing 4 so you can see exactly where the marks live.

NARRATOR: Let me make that loop concrete, because abstract is forgettable. Say you're designing a timber stool for your first project. Design theory tells you, before you cut anything, that timber has particular properties: it's strong along the grain and weaker across it, and that a stool has to bear a person's weight safely, which the syllabus would later call the factors of function and work health and safety. That's theory disciplining your practice: you don't just glue legs on and hope. Then you build it, and in testing, a joint splits. That real outcome, that's practice, sends you back to refine your understanding: maybe the joint design was wrong, maybe the timber selection was. Next stool, your theory is better because your practice tested it. Theory and practice, round and round. That movement between the two has a structured name, the design process, and it's so central it gets its own episode shortly.

NARRATOR: For now, here's a teaser and a memory hook you'll use for the next two years. The design process is a cycle, and we'll remember its stages with the word DRIPER: Define the need, Research, Ideate, which means generate ideas, Produce, Evaluate, and Refine, which loops you back to the start. DRIPER. You'll meet it properly in lesson three; I'm planting it now so the word is familiar when it arrives. It's in Listing 5. The thing to notice already is that the design process is exactly the theory-practice loop made into a sequence of steps: you define and research using theory, you produce using practice, you evaluate and refine to feed the result back. So when you hear DRIPER later, you're hearing today's lesson with stage names attached.

NARRATOR: Now the second half of today's content dot-point: the range of design professions. Design isn't one job, it's a whole family of them, and this course is deliberately broad so you can find the corner of it that interests you. Your course notes list the fields directly: designers come from a range of fields, fashion, industrial, graphic, multimedia, and more. Let me give you the main professions and, more usefully, a way to organise them so they're not just a random list to memorise. I'm not going to give you an acronym here, because there's a natural structure that's far easier to hold than letters, and that's a genuine study principle: when a list has its own logic, learn the logic, not a forced mnemonic.

NARRATOR: Group the professions by what they design. Some design objects: industrial and product designers, who design physical things like furniture, appliances and tools. Some design images and communication: graphic and multimedia designers, who design logos, packaging, websites, the way information looks. Some design spaces: architects and interior designers, who design buildings and environments. Some design what we wear: fashion and textile designers, who design clothing and fabrics. And some design systems and how things are made: engineers and technologists. Objects, images, spaces, garments, systems. That grouping is in Listing 6, and it's a far better answer than reeling off ten job titles, because it shows you understand the breadth of design, which is what the dot-point is really testing. And notice it maps straight back to the product, system or environment idea from earlier: object-designers make products, architects make environments, engineers make systems. The same three outcomes, different specialists.

NARRATOR: Two things to notice about those professions, because they're examinable and they connect forward. First, the notes say designers often work collaboratively with engineers, clients and manufacturers. Design is rarely a solo act: the professions overlap and interact on real projects, an industrial designer working alongside an engineer and a marketer, each bringing different expertise to the same product. We'll study that overlap and how design teams actually work later in the Preliminary course, under collaborative approaches. That's a forward link: hold the idea that professions overlap, because "the interaction and overlap of design professions" is itself a dot-point. Second, and this is the through-line of the whole course, every one of these professions, whatever they design, runs the same fundamental design process and weighs the same fundamental factors. A fashion designer and a bridge engineer both define a need, research, generate ideas, produce and evaluate. That shared process is why this is one subject and not five.

NARRATOR: This is also the moment to flag a forward connection to the HSC year, because it primes the later lesson. The syllabus requires you to investigate at least one real designer and the nature of their work, and in Year 12 you'll be asked to relate the practices and processes of designers to your own major design project. So studying designers isn't trivia; it's a Year 12 outcome being seeded right now. The next episode is entirely about the work of designers, where we'll study a real one properly and start that habit. Today just plant the flag: design is a profession, with many branches, all sharing a process and a set of factors.

NARRATOR: One more forward link and then we consolidate. I keep mentioning factors. The next big thing you'll learn after the design process is the eleven factors affecting design, the master checklist every designer weighs on every project, things like function, aesthetics, finance, ergonomics, and environmental consequences. They're the single most reused idea in the subject, and they're the theory that disciplines practice in the most concrete way. You'll meet all eleven in lesson four. For now, just notice that when I described the stool a moment ago, it has to bear weight, look right, be affordable, be safe, I was already listing factors without naming them. That's the web this course builds: theory, process, factors, professions, all connected. Nothing in Design and Technology stands alone, and these episodes will keep tying the threads together on purpose, because that interleaving is exactly how you remember it under exam pressure.

NARRATOR: Let me recap today the way you'd write it for marks. One: design is purposeful problem-solving, the deliberate creation of a product, system or environment to meet an identified need or opportunity, and a designer is a problem-solver. Two: a need is an existing problem you respond to, an opportunity is a gap you reach for, and design outcomes come in three kinds, product, system or environment. Three: design differs from art in that art is primarily self-expression and answers to the artist, whereas design solves an identified problem and answers to the user. Four: design theory is the knowledge and principles that inform design, design practice is the application of that knowledge, and the two are interdependent, theory disciplines practice, practice tests and refines theory. Five: that movement between theory and practice is structured by the design process, which we'll remember as DRIPER. And six: design is a family of professions, industrial, graphic, fashion, architectural, engineering and more, that all share one process and one set of factors. Hold those and you can answer anything this opening dot-point asks.

QUESTION: Question two. Distinguish between art and design. Two marks. Pause, write a full answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Art is primarily concerned with self-expression and answers to the artist's intention, whereas design is primarily concerned with solving an identified need or problem for a user and is shaped by constraints such as function, cost and the client's requirements. QUESTION: Question three. Explain the relationship between design theory and design practice. Three marks. This is the headline question for this topic, so pause the player and write a full response before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Design theory is the body of knowledge and principles that informs design decisions, while design practice is the application of that knowledge to create real design solutions. The trap, and it's the common one, is stopping after the two definitions and never explaining the relationship, but "explain the relationship" is literally what the question asked, so the third mark is the loop.

QUESTION: Question four. Using an example, distinguish between a need and an opportunity in design. Two marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A need is an existing problem that requires a solution, for example people who use wheelchairs being unable to reach high shelves, which creates a need for an accessible storage design. An opportunity is a possibility a designer identifies and pursues, often before any demand exists, for example combining a phone, camera and computer into a single device. Two marks: one for defining and exemplifying a need as a problem responded to, one for an opportunity as a gap reached for. The distinction the marker wants is that needs are problems you respond to, opportunities are possibilities you pursue.

QUESTION: Question five. Identify three different design professions and outline the nature of the work in each. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer, and any three valid professions work, here are three clear ones. An industrial or product designer designs physical objects such as furniture, appliances and tools, balancing function, aesthetics and manufacturability. A graphic or multimedia designer designs visual communication such as logos, packaging and websites, shaping how information is presented to an audience. An architect or interior designer designs spaces and environments such as buildings and room layouts, considering how people use and move through them. Three marks, one per profession correctly identified and described by what it designs. The way to make this easy, as we said, is to think in groups, objects, images, spaces, rather than scrambling for random job titles.
