NARRATOR: Let's reset. Last episode oriented you to the major design project: its three phases, proposal and management, development and realisation, evaluation, all run as the design process DRIPER; the folio as documented evidence of your process; and the meta-skill, justify, don't narrate, decision, reason, factor, evidence. I planted the hook NICe for what the proposal must contain. Today we start phase one for real, and it begins where every project must, with the need. Reach back to the Preliminary course for two things this builds on directly: the design brief, NCC, from module three, and market research, which is how you confirm a need is genuine. The MDP proposal is essentially a design brief written for your own real project.

NARRATOR: Today's home topic is identifying the need and formulating the project proposal. The syllabus is precise about the skill: develop a major design project proposal that clearly outlines the identification and exploration of the need, the areas of investigation, and the criteria to evaluate success. That's NICe, and those three parts are the whole proposal. By the end you'll be able to identify and analyse a need, and write a proposal that sets your project up to succeed. Let me start with the need itself.

NARRATOR: A project begins by identifying a need or an opportunity, and you met this distinction back in module one. A need is usually consumer-driven, you're responding to a real problem or demand experienced by a target market, the textbook says a need is often the designer responding to the demands of the target market. An opportunity is a gap you spot and choose to pursue, often before anyone's complaining, and it may offer profit or a way to meet needs more effectively than what's on the market. A particularly valuable kind of opportunity is a niche, which the textbook defines as a small, specialised group of consumers for whom no adequate product currently exists. Find a genuine niche and you've found a strong MDP. Need versus opportunity, with niche as a special case, is in Listing 1.

NARRATOR: Once you have a candidate need, you explore it with a needs analysis, and this is the heart of phase one. A needs analysis is a questioning exercise to clarify and define the design task, the textbook's words, and its answers tell you what the project must do to meet the target market. The key questions, in Listing 2, are: What's the situation or context that created this need, and what external influences shaped it? What type of need is it, personal, community, environmental, or other? Are there multiple needs, and can you prioritise them? Who exactly is the target market, and what are their demographics? What must the solution be able to do? And crucially, what's the gap, what existing products are there and how do they fail to meet this need? That last question is where you confirm a niche. And you don't just ask these in your head, you carry out initial research, market research from module three, to confirm the need is genuine before committing. A need that survives a needs analysis and research is a need worth designing for.

NARRATOR: Now the proposal itself, NICe, in Listing 3, the document that formally launches your MDP and the contract everything is judged against. It has three parts. N, the Need: the identification and exploration of the need, stated clearly, who has it, what the problem is, and the evidence from your needs analysis and research that it's real. I, the areas of Investigation: what you'll need to research and explore to solve it, materials, existing products, users, techniques, the map of your research to come. And C, the Criteria to evaluate success: the measurable standards the finished project must meet, functional and aesthetic, exactly as you learned for design briefs. NICe, need, investigation, criteria. It builds directly on the NCC brief from module three, and it's the foundation of the whole project.

NARRATOR: One point that connects forward and is worth real marks: the criteria you set in the proposal are exactly the criteria you'll evaluate the finished project against in phase three. That makes the proposal and the final evaluation bookends, just as the brief and evaluation were in module three. So your criteria must be measurable, "must support a hundred kilograms", "must cost under eighty dollars", not vague, "must be good quality", because a criterion you can't measure is a criterion you can't evaluate against later. Set measurable criteria now and you make your own final evaluation objective.

NARRATOR: Now the headline question: "Explain how a need or opportunity was identified and developed into a project proposal." Here's the move. A weak answer says "I wanted to make a lamp, so I decided to design one", which states a wish, not an analysed need, and caps low. A strong answer shows the process: a need or opportunity identified and then analysed through a needs analysis, who the target market is, what gap exists in current products, confirmed by research; followed by a proposal that states the explored need, defines the areas to investigate, and sets measurable criteria for success. The difference is that the weak answer jumps to a solution, while the strong answer analyses the problem first and derives the proposal from it. The weak-versus-strong contrast is in Listing 5. Analyse the need before you name the solution.

NARRATOR: Let me make it concrete with a worked proposal, in Listing 4, for a real MDP. The need: many home gardeners with limited space and mobility struggle to garden at ground level, a needs analysis of older and apartment-dwelling gardeners, plus research showing existing raised planters are expensive and hard to assemble, confirms a genuine gap, that's the identification and exploration. The areas of investigation: ergonomics of working height, lightweight durable weatherproof materials, simple assembly methods, water management, and cost. The criteria to evaluate success: must allow gardening while seated or standing without bending, around waist height; must be assembled by one person without tools in under thirty minutes; must withstand outdoor weather for at least five years; and must retail under one hundred and fifty dollars. Notice the need is analysed and evidenced, the investigation areas map the research ahead, and every criterion is measurable. That's a proposal that sets a project up to succeed.

NARRATOR: Let me weave the connections. First, back to module three: the proposal is a design brief, NICe builds on NCC, and the needs analysis uses market-research methods to confirm the need. Second, back to module one: need versus opportunity, and the factors affecting design, your criteria are really the factors made specific, function, finance, ergonomics, environmental. Third, forward within this module: the areas of investigation you list here become the research and experimentation of the next episodes, and the criteria you set here are what episode eleven evaluates against, the proposal-to-evaluation bookend. So phase one literally writes the test you'll be marked against, get it right and the rest of the project has a clear target.

NARRATOR: One trap to flag. Students rush the proposal, stating a vague need and a product they already want to build, then set unmeasurable criteria. That undermines the whole project: an un-analysed need may not be real, and unmeasurable criteria can't be evaluated. Spend real effort here, analyse the need with evidence, and write measurable criteria. The proposal is the foundation, a weak one cracks the whole project.

NARRATOR: Recap, the way you'd write it. One: a project starts from a need, usually consumer-driven, or an opportunity, a gap to pursue, with a niche being an unmet specialised market. Two: you explore the need with a needs analysis, a questioning exercise covering the situation, type of need, target market, required impact and the gap in existing products, confirmed by research. Three: the proposal contains NICe, the Need explored and evidenced, the areas of Investigation, and the measurable Criteria to evaluate success. Four: the criteria in the proposal are what you evaluate against at the end, so they must be measurable, the proposal and evaluation are bookends. Five: analyse the need before naming the solution. QUESTION: Question one. Identify the three components of a major design project proposal. Three marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A proposal must outline the identification and exploration of the need, the areas of investigation, and the criteria to evaluate success. Three marks, one per component, NICe. Briefly saying what each is, the explored need, the research map, and the measurable success standards, makes the answer secure.

QUESTION: Question two. Explain the purpose of a needs analysis. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A needs analysis is a questioning exercise that clarifies and defines the design task before designing begins. By systematically asking about the situation and its context, the type of need, the target market and their demographics, the required function of the solution, and how existing products fail to meet the need, the designer confirms that the need is genuine, identifies any gap or niche in the market, and establishes what the solution must achieve. This ensures the project addresses a real, well-understood need rather than an assumed one. Three marks: state that it clarifies and defines the task through questioning, that it confirms a genuine need and identifies the gap, and that it establishes what the solution must do. The marker wants "clarify and define the task" and "confirm a genuine need".

QUESTION: Question three. Explain how a need or opportunity is identified and developed into a project proposal. Five marks. This is the headline question, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A designer first identifies a possible need, a consumer-driven problem, or an opportunity, a gap or niche in the market. This is then explored through a needs analysis, a questioning exercise that examines the situation, the target market and their demographics, the required function of the solution, and how existing products fail to meet the need, and it is confirmed as genuine through market research. From this analysis the designer formulates a project proposal that clearly outlines three things: the identified and explored need, supported by evidence; the areas of investigation to be researched; and measurable criteria to evaluate the project's success. In this way the proposal is derived from a properly analysed need rather than from an assumed solution. Five marks: identify need versus opportunity, explore it through a needs analysis confirmed by research, and formulate the NICe proposal derived from the analysis. The weak answer that jumps straight to "I wanted to build X" misses the analysis and caps low.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain why the criteria for success in a proposal should be measurable. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The criteria for success should be measurable because they are the standards against which the finished project is evaluated in phase three, so the proposal and the final evaluation are linked as bookends. A measurable criterion, such as "must support one hundred kilograms" or "must cost under eighty dollars", can be objectively tested, allowing a clear judgement of success, whereas a vague criterion such as "must be good quality" cannot be measured and leaves the evaluation subjective and unreliable. Measurable criteria therefore make the project's success objectively assessable. Three marks: state that criteria are evaluated against later, that measurable criteria allow objective testing, and contrast with vague criteria. The proposal-to-evaluation link is the high-value insight.
