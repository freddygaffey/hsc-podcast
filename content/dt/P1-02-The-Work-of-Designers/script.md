NARRATOR: Let's pick up where we left off. Last episode, the first of the course, we answered the question "what is design?" Quick reset, because today builds straight on it. We said design is purposeful problem-solving: the deliberate creation of a product, a system, or an environment to meet an identified need or opportunity, and a designer is a problem-solver. We separated a need, an existing problem you respond to, from an opportunity, a gap you reach for. We distinguished design from art: art is primarily self-expression and answers to the artist, whereas design solves an identified problem and answers to the user. We defined design theory, the knowledge and principles that inform design, against design practice, the application of that knowledge, and we said the two form a loop, theory disciplines practice, practice tests and refines theory. And we previewed the design process, DRIPER: Define, Research, Ideate, Produce, Evaluate, Refine. Keep DRIPER handy, because today you'll watch real designers run it.

NARRATOR: Today's topic is the work of designers. This is the second content dot-point of the course, and it has a very practical reason for existing. The syllabus requires you to investigate at least one designer and the nature of their work. And it's not busywork: in Year 12, one of your HSC outcomes, H1.2, asks you to relate the practices and processes of designers and producers to your own major design project. In plain terms, you'll be expected to look at how a real, successful designer works and borrow that discipline for your own project. So studying designers now is laying track for the biggest piece of assessment you'll do in this subject. By the end of today you'll be able to describe the nature and variety of designers' work, explain how design professions interact and overlap, describe the work and the processes of real designers in a way that earns marks, and identify the career opportunities this field opens up.

NARRATOR: Start with the core idea: what is the nature of a designer's work, in general? Your course notes put it cleanly, and these four points are the skeleton of any "describe the nature of a designer's work" answer, so they're collected in Listing 1. Designers come from a range of fields, fashion, industrial, graphic, multimedia and more. They solve problems by combining creativity and technology, you need the idea and the means to make it real. They're influenced by culture, history, environment, and personal experience. And they often work collaboratively, with engineers, clients and manufacturers. So a designer is not a lone genius having flashes of inspiration. A designer is a problem-solver who applies a process, draws on outside influences, and works with other people to turn an identified need into a real solution.

NARRATOR: Let me dwell on that word "influences" for a second, because it's easy to skim past and it's genuinely examinable. The syllabus and the course notes both say a designer's work is shaped by culture, history, environment and personal experience. That means design doesn't happen in a vacuum: a designer's background, the society they live in, the period of history, the climate and resources around them, and their own life all feed into what they make and how. You'll see this concretely in a moment, when a fashion label turns out to have been founded directly from its designers' personal experience. Hold the idea that "where a designer comes from shapes what they design", because in Year 12 it grows into a whole topic, the historical and cultural influences on design.

NARRATOR: Now let me reconnect to last episode's grouping of professions, because today's dot-point is partly about the range and variety of design work. We grouped the professions by what they design, and I said to learn the logic rather than an acronym, that grouping is in Listing 6 of the previous episode if you want it again. Some design objects, the industrial and product designers. Some design images and communication, the graphic and multimedia designers. Some design spaces, the architects and interior designers. Some design garments, the fashion and textile designers. And some design systems and how things get made, the engineers and technologists. Today we add the important next idea: these fields are not sealed boxes. They interact and they overlap.

NARRATOR: Here's what "interaction and overlap of design professions" actually means, and it's a dot-point in its own words, so know it. The distinction is in Listing 2. Interaction is different kinds of designer working together on one project. Think about designing a new car: industrial designers shape the body, engineers handle the mechanics and safety, ergonomists work on the seating and controls, graphic designers do the badging and the dashboard displays, and marketers shape how it's sold. Different professions, one product, constantly interacting. That's interaction. Overlap is slightly different: it's when one designer crosses into another's territory, because the underlying design process is the same. The Cambridge text makes this point directly: because a design process is adaptable to virtually any situation, designers today are not locked to one set of materials or tools. As the textbook puts it, architects can design furniture as well as buildings. The process travels, so the designer can too. Interaction is professions working side by side; overlap is the boundaries between them blurring.

NARRATOR: Time to make this real with actual designers, because the syllabus wants you to study one properly, and worked examples stick far better than definitions. I'll give you three Australians and one international designer, and they're all collected in Listing 4 so you have them exam-ready. Start with a famous Australian industrial designer the textbook itself features: Marc Newson. Newson is one of the most successful industrial designers in the world, and he's Australian, which makes him ideal for the "Australian designer" requirement. His range tells you everything about the nature of design work. He designed a now legendary aluminium chaise, he's worked on watches, on household products for the Italian brand Alessi, and, the example the textbook highlights, Qantas commissioned him to design a business-class seat that folds flat into a bed, which became known as the Skybed. One designer, working across furniture, products, and aircraft interiors.

NARRATOR: Now here's the part that matters for marks, and it's exactly what H1.2 will ask you to do in Year 12. Don't just list what Newson made, that's biography, and biography scores poorly. Describe how he works, his process. The textbook spells it out: Newson's success lies in his flexibility and adaptability to different design situations, using the design process as a project development framework. In other words, the reason one man can design a chair and an aeroplane seat is that he applies the same underlying process, DRIPER, define, research, ideate, produce, evaluate, refine, and adapts it to each new problem. He isn't afraid to work with unfamiliar materials and techniques. The textbook even says students embarking on the major design project can learn from Newson's courage to try new things and learn new skills. That sentence, his work demonstrates the design process applied flexibly across diverse problems, is worth more than a paragraph listing his products, because it connects the designer to the syllabus concept of the design process. That's the move.

NARRATOR: Let me give you a second Australian example, a different field, because variety is the point, and this one shows the need-driven origin, the role of influences, and collaboration beautifully. P.E Nation is an activewear label founded in twenty sixteen by Pip Edwards and Claire Tregoning. Watch how it maps onto everything we've said. The brand started from the founders' own need: they were both working mums who wanted to stay fit but also wanted sportswear they could wear on the street and still look good, design-driven pieces, in Pip's words. That is personal experience, an influence, becoming an identified need, becoming a design opportunity, the very start of the design process. Then the nature of their work: their skill sets were complementary and they collaborated, Claire drew the designs and developed the samples, Pip handled entering the market and the sales. Two designers, different strengths, one brand. Their success leaned heavily on marketing, especially Instagram, where aspirational images built a presence in a market dominated by sporting giants. And their work reflects sustainability, they introduced natural fibres like wool for their biodegradability and performance. So in one designer profile you can see influences, need, collaboration, marketing and sustainability, all syllabus language. When you describe a designer's work, that's the kind of detail that scores: not just what they made, but the need they answered, what shaped them, how they worked, and who they worked with.

NARRATOR: Third Australian example, because it's the cleanest illustration of overlap and collaboration you'll find, and it's straight from the textbook: the design duo known as Tin and Ed, that's Tin Nguyen and Ed Cutting. They trained as graphic designers, but their work refuses to stay in the graphic-design box: they move between the digital and physical worlds, creating images, objects and installations, using technology like 3D scanning and machine learning. Graphic designers making physical installations and AI-driven artworks, that is overlap in action, the boundaries of their original profession blurring because they carry the design process into new territory. And collaboration is at the heart of how they work. They openly say that when they don't know how to do something, they bring in the right person to do it properly, contributing to a better whole. On one project they coordinated a six-hundred-metre mural built from the contributions of around twenty designers from across the world. Tin and Ed show you both ideas at once: overlap, because they cross professional boundaries, and interaction, because they pull in a whole network of collaborators.

NARRATOR: For the international side of the requirement, let me give you one famous non-Australian designer and tie him straight back to last episode's theory-practice loop, because it's a perfect illustration. James Dyson, the British designer and engineer behind the bagless vacuum cleaner. The story everyone cites about Dyson is that he built thousands of prototypes, famously reported as over five thousand, each one a small failure that taught him something, before he had a vacuum that worked. Now hear that through last episode's lens. Each prototype is design practice. Each failure fed back and refined his understanding, his theory, of how the cyclone separation should work. Build, test, learn, refine, build again. That is the theory-practice loop, and it's DRIPER's evaluate-and-refine stage running thousands of times. Dyson is the cleanest example you'll ever get of practice testing and refining theory, and it's why "iterative" is the word markers love to see attached to the design process. One international designer, and he reinforces the loop from episode one.

NARRATOR: Now the third strand of today's dot-point, which the syllabus phrases as "identify a range of career opportunities in design and production". There are traditional career pathways: industrial and product designer, graphic and multimedia designer, fashion and textile designer, architect and interior designer, engineer and technologist, the same family of professions we grouped earlier. NARRATOR: One trap to flag before we recap, because it loses marks every year. NARRATOR: Model answer. QUESTION: Question four. Analyse the nature of collaboration in the design process, referring to the work of two designers. Five marks. This is an extended response, so pause the player and write a full paragraph before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Collaboration is central to the design process because most real design involves several people with complementary skills working together, and communicating, to produce a better outcome than any could alone. In P.E Nation, founders Pip Edwards and Claire Tregoning collaborated by dividing the work according to their strengths, Claire designing and developing the samples while Pip drove marketing and sales, which allowed the brand to launch successfully and build a strong market presence.
