NARRATOR: Let's reset. Last episode was work health and safety: hazard versus risk, the WHS Act, and the hierarchy of control, ES-EAP, eliminate, substitute, engineering, administrative, and PPE last, with the principle that you design safety in rather than bolt it on. Before that, materials and resources, where the big idea was matching a material's properties to function, and where I flagged that every material choice has consequences of use, environmental and social as well as practical. And right back in module one, two of our eleven factors were the short and long-term environmental consequences and we built the life cycle analysis tool, the cradle-to-grave assessment of a product's impact. Today we widen the lens from the workshop to the world: the environmental and social issues that design and technology raise, and the impact a design has on the individual, on society and on the environment.

NARRATOR: Today's home topic is environmental and social issues. The syllabus lists what to consider, and frames the skill clearly: assess the impact of the activities undertaken in developing design projects on the individual, society and the environment, and evaluate examples of design and production in relation to these issues. So two structures run through this topic. The first is the three levels of impact, individual, society and environment, always think in those three. The second is the list of seven issues the syllabus names. By the end you'll be able to assess a design's impact across all three levels using those issues, and define the two that get examined hardest, sustainability and equity. Let me give you the three levels and the seven issues first.

NARRATOR: The three levels of impact are the individual, society, and the environment. When you assess any design, ask: how does it affect the person using it, how does it affect the wider community and society, and how does it affect the natural environment? Those three are in Listing 1, and structuring an impact answer around them is half the marks. The seven issues the syllabus names, the things to weigh, are: personal values, cultural beliefs, sustainability, safety and health, community needs, individual needs, and equity. We'll hold them with the hook PC SCIE: Personal values, Cultural beliefs, Sustainability, Safety and health, Community needs, Individual needs, Equity. They're all in Listing 2. Let me walk them.

NARRATOR: Personal values. We all hold different values, shaped by our upbringing, lifestyle, cultural background and life experiences, and those values affect whether people accept a design. The textbook's example: some groups value recycling and environmental friendliness highly, while others don't, so the same product lands differently with different people. A designer has to be aware of the values of the target market.

NARRATOR: Cultural beliefs. These are shaped by the society we live in, by geography, religion, history, traditional practices and laws. Culture drives design in fashion, jewellery, architecture and art, and a designer must show sensitivity. The textbook gives a pointed Australian example: using sacred Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander artwork in a design may be considered taboo, so cultural appropriation is a real design issue. Multiculturalism enriches design, but only when approached with respect.

NARRATOR: Sustainability, and this is the heavyweight, so here's the definition to memorise, because it's almost word-for-word examinable. Sustainability means addressing the needs of people today without negatively affecting the resources available to people in the future. Meeting present needs without compromising the future. As designers, that means avoiding finite, non-renewable resources where possible, thinking about disposal, and using a life cycle analysis to monitor energy and environmental impact at every stage, in order to minimise the product's carbon footprint, the total greenhouse gas emissions it causes. Notice this connects straight back to module one's life cycle analysis, sustainability is the goal, and life cycle analysis is the tool you use to pursue it. The definition is in Listing 3.

NARRATOR: Safety and health. The safety and health of both the production workers and the final users must be considered at all times, complying with SafeWork New South Wales, Standards Australia and WHS guidelines. This is last episode's content seen as a social issue, the textbook even gives examples like eliminating medium-density fibreboard dust in schools and never using asbestos. Safety and health is where work health and safety meets the broader social responsibility of design.

NARRATOR: The next three go together. Community needs are the needs of a group or the wider society, a community might need shared facilities, local jobs, accessible public spaces. Individual needs are the needs of a single person, comfort, function, affordability. And these two can pull against each other, a design that suits an individual might not serve the community, and vice versa, so a designer often has to balance them. And then equity, the seventh issue and the second heavyweight. Equity means fairness, designing so that all people, regardless of ability, age, income or background, have fair and equal access to a design. This is the principle behind inclusive design and universal design, think wheelchair ramps, step-free access, products usable by people with disabilities. Equity asks: does this design include everyone, or does it leave people out? Equity and sustainability are the two issues the exam leans on hardest, so both are spelled out in Listing 3.

QUESTION: Retrieval, because these two definitions earn marks directly. Define sustainability and equity as design issues. Pause the player, say both, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. Sustainability means meeting the needs of people today without negatively affecting the resources available to people in the future, so designers avoid finite resources, consider disposal, and minimise their carbon footprint, using a life cycle analysis. Equity means fairness, designing so that all people, regardless of ability, background or circumstance, have equal access to and benefit from a design, the principle behind inclusive and universal design. If you had "present needs without compromising the future" for sustainability and "fair, equal access for all" for equity, those are the marking phrases. Lock them in.

NARRATOR: Now the headline skill: "Assess the impact of a design on the individual, society and the environment." The verb assess, like evaluate, wants a judgement, not just a description, and the structure is handed to you by the three levels. A weak answer says "it's bad for the environment", one vague point at one level, and caps low. A strong answer works all three levels, the impact on the individual, on society, and on the environment, draws on the relevant issues, sustainability, equity, safety, cultural beliefs, and reaches a balanced judgement weighing positives against negatives. The weak-versus-strong contrast is in Listing 5. The single best habit for this topic: whenever you see "assess the impact", immediately split your answer into individual, society, environment, and you've built the skeleton of a top-band response.

NARRATOR: Let me make that concrete with a worked example, because applying the three levels is the whole skill. It's in Listing 4. Take fast fashion, cheap, rapidly produced, disposable clothing. Assess its impact across the three levels. To the individual: it offers affordable, fashionable clothing and self-expression, a genuine positive, but encourages a throwaway mindset and poor-quality garments. To society: it can provide employment, but it's associated with poor labour conditions and low wages in manufacturing countries, a serious social and ethical negative, and it raises equity questions about who bears the cost. To the environment: it has a severe impact, huge textile waste as clothes are discarded, high water and energy use, and microplastic pollution from synthetic fibres, a clear failure on sustainability and life cycle analysis. The balanced judgement: while fast fashion delivers affordability and choice to individuals, its social cost in labour conditions and its environmental cost in waste and emissions make it difficult to justify as responsible design. That answer hit all three levels, named the issues, and judged, that's the model.

NARRATOR: Let me weave the connections. First, back to module one: this topic is where the environmental factors and life cycle analysis become a full social-and-environmental analysis, sustainability is the goal that the environmental factors serve. And safety and health here is last episode's WHS seen as a social responsibility. Second, forward to Year 12, and this is a direct line: outcome H2.2 is evaluating the impact of design and innovation on society and the environment, the exact skill we just practised, and outcome H6.2 assesses the impact of emerging technologies. The Section three extended response in the HSC very often asks you to analyse ethical, social and environmental implications, which is this topic at full stretch. Third, two case studies cash in here. Central Park in Sydney, an award-winning building marketed as green, is the cautionary tale of a design whose environmental image outran its measured impact, the gap a real assessment exposes. And the Flow Hive, a beekeeping innovation, used sustainability as a genuine selling point, the positive side. Hold both: Central Park for green image versus real impact, Flow Hive for sustainability as a design strength.

NARRATOR: One trap to flag. Students collapse "assess the impact on the individual, society and the environment" into a single blurry paragraph about the environment, and lose the marks for the other two levels. The three levels are a checklist, address each one explicitly. And don't forget the positives, assess and evaluate want both sides, a design usually has benefits as well as costs, and a one-sided answer caps below a balanced one.

NARRATOR: Recap, the way you'd write it. One: assess a design's impact across three levels, the individual, society and the environment, and use them as your answer's structure. Two: the seven environmental and social issues are PC SCIE, personal values, cultural beliefs, sustainability, safety and health, community needs, individual needs and equity. Three: sustainability is meeting present needs without compromising the future, pursued through life cycle analysis and a smaller carbon footprint. Four: equity is fairness and equal access for all, the basis of inclusive and universal design, and it, with sustainability, is examined hardest. Five: assess and evaluate want a balanced judgement across all three levels, with positives and negatives. Hold those five.

NARRATOR: Now the exam-style questions, real NESA format, get a pen. Four questions, model answers in Listing 6.

QUESTION: Question one. Define sustainability in the context of design. Two marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Sustainability means meeting the needs of people today without negatively affecting the resources available to people in the future. In design, this means avoiding finite, non-renewable resources where possible, considering a product's disposal, and minimising its environmental impact and carbon footprint, often assessed using a life cycle analysis. Two marks: give the present-needs-without-compromising-the-future definition, and connect it to design practice such as resource choice or life cycle analysis. The trap is a vague "being green", the marker wants the future-resources definition.

QUESTION: Question two. Explain what equity means as a social issue in design, with an example. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Equity means fairness, designing so that all people, regardless of ability, age, income or background, have fair and equal access to and benefit from a design. It underlies inclusive or universal design, which aims to make products and environments usable by as many people as possible. For example, designing a public building with ramps and step-free entrances as well as stairs ensures people who use wheelchairs have equal access, satisfying equity. Three marks: define equity as fair, equal access, link it to inclusive or universal design, and give a concrete example. The key word the marker wants is fairness or equal access for all.

QUESTION: Question three. Assess the impact of a product you have studied on the individual, society and the environment. Six marks. This is the headline question, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer, using fast fashion, and notice it works all three levels and judges. To the individual, fast fashion provides affordable, fashionable clothing and a means of self-expression, but it encourages a disposable, throwaway mindset and the garments are often low quality. To society, it creates employment, but it is widely associated with poor labour conditions and low wages in manufacturing countries, raising serious ethical and equity concerns about who bears the human cost. To the environment, its impact is severe: large volumes of textile waste from discarded clothing, high water and energy consumption in production, and microplastic pollution from synthetic fibres, a clear failure against sustainability. On balance, although fast fashion delivers affordability and choice to individuals, its social costs in labour exploitation and its environmental costs in waste and emissions make it difficult to justify as responsible design. Six marks: assess each of the three levels explicitly, draw in relevant issues like equity and sustainability, and reach a balanced overall judgement. The weak answer talks only about the environment in general terms and caps low.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain how cultural beliefs can influence design. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Cultural beliefs, shaped by a society's religion, history, traditions and laws, influence what designs are acceptable and desirable, and a designer must show sensitivity to them. For example, religion can shape design in fashion, jewellery and architecture, and using sacred Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander artwork in a design without permission may be considered culturally inappropriate or taboo. A designer therefore researches and respects the cultural context of their target market to avoid causing offence and to create designs that are accepted. Three marks: explain that cultural beliefs shape acceptability, give a concrete example such as the use of sacred Indigenous artwork, and note the designer's responsibility to be culturally sensitive. The marker rewards a real, specific example of cultural sensitivity.

QUESTION: Question five. Explain how community needs and individual needs can conflict in design, using an example. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Community needs are the needs of a group or wider society, while individual needs are the needs of a single person, and the two can pull against each other, so a designer must balance them. For example, a new high-density apartment development might serve the community need for affordable housing and efficient land use, while conflicting with an individual resident's need for privacy, space and access to sunlight. A designer resolves this by seeking solutions that meet the community need without unduly sacrificing individual needs, such as good acoustic separation and shared green space. Three marks: define community and individual needs, explain that they can conflict, and give an example showing the tension and how a designer balances it. The insight the marker wants is that design often involves trade-offs between the group and the individual, not a single right answer.
