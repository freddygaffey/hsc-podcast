NARRATOR: Let's reset. Last episode, the six factors of innovation success, TEMPLE, timing, emerging technologies, marketing, political, legal-economic, era, with the Flow Hive as our worked success. Today we look behind those factors at who actually drives an innovation, the entrepreneur, and what helps or blocks them, the agencies. This connects to two TEMPLE factors directly, political and legal-economic, and back to module three and H1, where you managed a project, entrepreneurs manage ventures. So today is the human and institutional side of innovation.

NARRATOR: Today's home topic is entrepreneurial activity and the agencies of innovation, part of outcome H3.1. The syllabus covers the nature of entrepreneurial activity, its role in design and technology, the agencies that influence it, management and entrepreneurial activity, and the legal and ethical issues, plus the role of a variety of agencies that influence the development, implementation and acceptance of innovation. The skills are to discuss the influence of entrepreneurial activity on successful design and innovation, and the legal and ethical issues around it. By the end you'll be able to explain what entrepreneurs do and name the agencies that shape innovation. Let me start with the entrepreneur.

NARRATOR: Entrepreneurial activity is taking an idea or innovation and turning it into a viable, usually profitable, venture, while bearing the risk of doing so. That risk-bearing is the heart of it: an entrepreneur invests time and money with no guarantee of success. The role of the entrepreneur, in the textbook's words, is to find new uses for ideas and successfully bring innovations to market, the bridge between a clever idea and a real product people can buy. Entrepreneurial activity also involves management, running the venture, the action, time and finance skills from H1, and it raises legal and ethical issues, protecting intellectual property, being honest with investors, and managing risk responsibly. Nature, role, management, and legal-ethical, that's entrepreneurial activity, in Listing 1.

NARRATOR: But no entrepreneur succeeds alone, which brings us to the agencies that influence innovation, the institutions that help or hinder it, and we'll hold them with the hook GASP-I, in Listing 2. G, Government: through funding, policy and support, the textbook notes governments support sustainable living and fund research, and bodies like the CSIRO use government funding to develop new technologies. A, Associations and standards bodies: Standards Australia and non-government organisations that set rules and build confidence. S, Sponsors and funders: investors who provide capital, and increasingly crowdfunding platforms. P, Patent and intellectual-property offices: IP Australia and its Patents Office, where innovators protect their designs. And I, Industry partners: manufacturers and businesses that help develop and distribute the innovation. Government, associations, sponsors, patent offices, industry, GASP-I, the web of agencies around every innovation.

NARRATOR: One agency deserves special attention because it's a favourite exam point and a major legal-ethical issue: intellectual property and patents, in Listing 3. A patent, granted through IP Australia's Patents Office, gives an innovator exclusive rights to their design, protecting it from copycats. The textbook gives a cautionary tale: the Australian inventor of the powerboard in nineteen seventy-one failed to patent it, and so lost the rights and the rewards to others. Contrast that with the Flow Hive, whose inventors filed a patent before they launched their crowdfunding campaign, protecting their innovation precisely when it was about to become public and copyable. Patent first, then reveal, that's the lesson, and failing to protect intellectual property is one of the most common ways an innovator loses out.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. Entrepreneurial activity is taking an idea or innovation and turning it into a viable, profitable venture while bearing the risk. Three agencies, any of GASP-I: government, through funding and policy; sponsors and funders, including investors and crowdfunding; patent and intellectual-property offices like IP Australia; standards bodies and associations; and industry partners. If you defined entrepreneurship as turning an idea into a venture with risk, and named three agencies, that's the mark. The risk-bearing is the part students leave out.

NARRATOR: Now the headline question: "Discuss the influence of entrepreneurial activity on successful design and innovation." Here's the move. A weak answer says "entrepreneurs take risks and start businesses", a definition with no influence and no example, capping low. A strong answer defines entrepreneurial activity, explains how it drives innovation, bringing ideas to market, attracting investment, managing the venture, and supports it with a real example and the agencies involved, and weighs the legal and ethical issues: "the Flow Hive's inventors acted entrepreneurially, self-funding development, protecting their idea with a patent, and using a crowdfunding agency to raise capital and validate demand, which turned a clever idea into a global product; without that entrepreneurial drive and the agencies that supported it, the innovation would not have reached the market." The weak-versus-strong contrast is in Listing 5. Define, explain the influence, exemplify, weigh the issues.

NARRATOR: Let me make it concrete, in Listing 4, with the Flow Hive's entrepreneurial path mapped to agencies. The inventors, Cedar and Stuart Anderson, self-funded years of research and development, bearing the risk. They protected the innovation through the patent office, IP Australia, filing before going public. They raised capital through a crowdfunding agency, Indiegogo, breaking records with over twelve million dollars, which also validated demand. And they partnered with industry to manufacture at scale using injection moulding. So at each step an agency, the patent office, the crowdfunding platform, industry, enabled the entrepreneurial activity. That mapping, entrepreneur plus the agencies that supported them, is exactly what a discuss-the-influence question wants.

NARRATOR: Now the exam-style questions, real NESA format, get a pen. Four questions, model answers in Listing 6.

QUESTION: Question one. Define entrepreneurial activity. Two marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Entrepreneurial activity is the process of taking an idea or innovation and developing it into a viable, usually profitable, commercial venture, while accepting the financial and personal risk involved. It includes bringing the innovation to market, attracting investment and managing the venture. Two marks: capture turning an idea into a venture, and the risk-bearing. The trap is defining it as just "starting a business", the risk and the bringing-to-market are the marks.

QUESTION: Question two. Identify three agencies that can influence the success of an innovation and explain how one of them does so. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Three agencies are government, sponsors or funders such as investors and crowdfunding platforms, and the patent or intellectual-property office. Government, for example, can influence an innovation's success by providing funding and supportive policy, as it does for sustainable technologies and through bodies like the CSIRO, which lowers the cost and risk of development and improves the innovation's chance of reaching the market. Three marks: name three agencies, GASP-I, and explain how one influences success. The explanation, not just the list, earns the third mark.

QUESTION: Question three. Discuss the influence of entrepreneurial activity on the success of an innovation you have studied. Five marks. This is the headline question, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer, using the Flow Hive. Entrepreneurial activity was central to the Flow Hive's success. Its inventors acted as entrepreneurs, self-funding years of research and development and bearing the risk of an unproven product. They managed the venture and engaged key agencies: they protected their intellectual property by filing a patent through IP Australia before launch, guarding against copycats; they raised capital and validated demand through a crowdfunding agency, Indiegogo, breaking records with over twelve million dollars; and they partnered with industry to manufacture at scale. This entrepreneurial drive, supported by these agencies, transformed a clever idea into a globally successful innovation. Without it, the design might never have reached the market. Five marks: define entrepreneurial activity, explain its influence, support with a real example and the agencies involved, and ideally note the legal-ethical IP point. A bare definition caps low.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain why protecting intellectual property is important for an innovator. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. Protecting intellectual property, typically by applying for a patent through IP Australia, is important because it gives the innovator exclusive rights to their design, preventing competitors from copying and profiting from it. This protects the time and money invested in the innovation and allows the innovator to reap the rewards of its success. For example, the Flow Hive's inventors patented their design before crowdfunding, whereas the Australian inventor of the powerboard failed to patent it and lost the rights and rewards to others. Three marks: state that a patent gives exclusive rights and prevents copying, that it protects the investment and rewards, and give an example. The contrast between protecting and failing to protect is the high-value detail.
