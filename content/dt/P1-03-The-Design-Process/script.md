NARRATOR: Let's reset the last two episodes before we start, because today pulls them together. Two episodes back, episode one, we defined design as purposeful problem-solving: the deliberate creation of a product, system or environment to meet an identified need or opportunity. We split a need, a problem you respond to, from an opportunity, a gap you reach for. We separated design from art, design answers to the user, art answers to the maker. And we set up the big relationship, design theory, the knowledge that informs design, and design practice, the doing of it, locked in a loop where theory disciplines practice and practice tests and refines theory. Then last episode, episode two, we looked at the work of real designers, Marc Newson, P.E Nation, James Dyson, and the single most important lesson there was: to describe a designer for marks, you describe their process, not just their products. We said Newson succeeds by adapting one underlying process across furniture, products and aircraft seats, and Dyson's thousands of prototypes were that theory-practice loop running over and over.

NARRATOR: So we keep arriving at the same word: process. Today we finally open it up. The design process is the home topic, and it is the backbone of this entire subject, every module, the major design project, all of it, runs on the design process. The syllabus dot-point is "design processes used in domestic, community, industrial and commercial settings, from initial contact with clients to final presentation", and the skill it wants is to "describe and analyse the processes undertaken when designing". Notice that verb pair, describe and analyse, because it tells you the exam doesn't just want the list of stages. It wants you to understand how the process actually behaves. By the end of today you'll be able to name and explain every stage of the design process, analyse why it's a cycle rather than a straight line, apply it to a real product, and see how the same process scales from a kid making something at home to a factory making millions.

NARRATOR: Here's the core, and here's the memory hook I planted in episode one, now made permanent: DRIPER. The design process, boiled down to six stages, spells DRIPER. D, Define. R, Research. I, Ideate. P, Produce. E, Evaluate. R, Refine. Write DRIPER down the second a design-process question appears. Let me walk each stage, because you need to be able to explain what happens in each, not just recite the word. They're all in Listing 1.

NARRATOR: D, Define. This is where you identify and clearly state the need or opportunity and turn it into a design brief. The syllabus phrase "from initial contact with clients" lives here: a designer meets the client, works out what's actually required, and writes it down as a brief with criteria for success. Define badly and everything downstream is aimed at the wrong target, so this stage is more important than beginners think. You are answering: what problem am I solving, for whom, and how will I know if I've succeeded?

NARRATOR: R, Research. Now you investigate. You gather information: market research to understand the need and existing products, research into materials and their properties, into the users, into how similar problems have been solved. The textbook calls this investigation and analysis, and it stresses analysing existing products, systems and environments to see how they work and what you can adapt. Research is where design theory gets loaded in before you commit to anything.

NARRATOR: I, Ideate, which just means generate ideas. This is the creative explosion: sketching, brainstorming, generating many possible solutions rather than marrying the first one. You'll learn formal idea-generation techniques later, things like brainstorming and a technique called SCAMPER, in the creativity topic. The rule of this stage is quantity before quality, get lots of options on paper, because the best idea is rarely the first.

NARRATOR: P, Produce. Now you turn ideas into something real: developing the chosen idea, modelling it, building prototypes, and ultimately realising the final solution. This is design practice at full tilt, working with materials, tools and techniques. Prototyping sits here, and prototypes matter because they're how you test an idea cheaply before committing to the full build.

NARRATOR: E, Evaluate. You test the solution against the criteria you set back in Define. Does it work? Does it meet the need? The textbook is emphatic that successful designers test, reflect and evaluate constantly. Evaluation isn't a single event at the end; it's continual, which is the hinge of today's analysis point, so hold that thought.

NARRATOR: And the second R, Refine. Based on what evaluation told you, you improve the design, and critically, you loop back. A problem found in evaluation sends you back to an earlier stage, maybe back to Produce to rebuild a joint, maybe all the way back to Research if your whole approach was wrong. Refine is what makes the process a cycle. That final R is an arrow pointing back to the start.

NARRATOR: And that brings us to the single most important idea in this episode, the one that turns a "describe" answer into a "describe and analyse" answer and earns the real marks. The design process is not a straight line. It is a cycle. It is iterative. Beginners draw it as a checklist: define, research, ideate, produce, evaluate, refine, done, marching top to bottom once. That is wrong, and the textbook says so directly: it is often a cyclic process, with the designer returning to different stages throughout, and the stages are not always in the same order. Evaluation constantly feeds back. When you test a prototype and it fails, you don't carry on regardless, you loop back, fix the idea or the research, and go round again. Dyson went round thousands of times. That looping, that feedback, is the whole point of the process, because it's how a design gets progressively better instead of being a single guess. Listing 2 draws the feedback loops out.

NARRATOR: Let me give you the exam framing precisely, because this is where students leave marks on the table. If a question says "describe the design process", a clean list of the stages with a sentence each can score well. But the moment the verb is "analyse", or "describe and analyse the processes undertaken when designing", listing the stages is only half the answer. The analysis is explaining that the process is iterative and cyclic: that evaluation is continual and feeds back, that a failure at one stage sends the designer back to an earlier stage, and that this looping is what produces a quality, refined solution. The weak answer lists DRIPER as a fixed recipe. The strong answer lists the stages and then analyses the looping. That contrast is in Listing 3, and it's the difference between a band four and a band six on this dot-point.

NARRATOR: Let me say the strong version out loud so you have the wording. Here it is. The design process is a structured but iterative sequence of stages: defining the need and brief, researching, generating ideas, producing and prototyping, evaluating against the criteria, and refining. Crucially, it is cyclic rather than linear: evaluation is conducted continually throughout, and where a solution fails to meet the criteria the designer returns to an earlier stage, regenerating ideas or revising research, and repeats the cycle. This iterative looping is what allows the design to be progressively improved until it satisfies the identified need. Hear how that does both jobs: it names the stages, that's describe, and it explains the cyclic feedback, that's analyse. The verb in the question tells you how much of that to give.

QUESTION: Quick retrieval before the worked example, because this is the exact thing students forget under pressure. Why is the design process described as cyclic or iterative rather than linear? Pause the player, answer it in a sentence, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. Because the designer does not pass through the stages once in a fixed order; evaluation happens continually, and when a design fails to meet its criteria the designer loops back to an earlier stage to regenerate ideas or revise research, repeating the cycle until the solution succeeds. That looping is what progressively improves the design. If you said "because you go back and improve it", you've got the kernel, now dress it in that language, evaluation feeds back, you return to an earlier stage, repeat until it meets the criteria. That sentence is worth marks every single time this topic appears.

NARRATOR: Now let's make DRIPER concrete by running a real product through it, because an abstract process is forgettable and a worked example is not. Take a reusable drinks bottle, the kind designed to replace single-use plastic, and it's a nice example because the HSC has literally asked about drink bottles. The full walk-through is in Listing 4. Define: the need is a durable, leak-proof, reusable bottle that reduces single-use plastic waste, and the criteria for success might be holds three-quarters of a litre, doesn't leak, keeps drinks cold for hours, and is easy to clean. Research: you investigate existing bottles and their weaknesses, you research materials, stainless steel versus aluminium versus plastic, their properties and their environmental footprint, and you research what users actually complain about, lids that leak, bottles that don't fit a car cup-holder. Ideate: you sketch many concepts, different lid mechanisms, shapes, insulation approaches, single wall versus double wall. Produce: you develop the strongest concept and build a prototype, say a double-walled stainless-steel bottle with a screw lid.

NARRATOR: Now watch the loop, because this is the part that demonstrates the analysis. Evaluate: you test the prototype against the criteria, and the screw lid leaks when the bottle's on its side. It fails a criterion. Refine: you don't ship a leaking bottle, you loop back, to Ideate, to design a better lid, maybe a silicone-sealed flip lid, then back to Produce to build the new version, then Evaluate again. Round the cycle a second time. Maybe this time it passes leakage but the insulation underperforms, so you loop back again, perhaps as far as Research to reconsider the wall design. Each loop makes the bottle better. That, right there, is the iterative design process, and describing that loop-back is exactly what "analyse the processes undertaken when designing" is asking for. One product, the whole cycle, with the feedback made visible.

NARRATOR: Last piece of the dot-point, and it's a forward-looking one: the same design process is used across different settings, "domestic, community, industrial and commercial". This is a genuinely important idea that we'll expand into a whole lesson next module, so let me plant it cleanly. Domestic is design at home, you making something in your garage. Community is a club or small group, a school or a local organisation designing something. Industrial is large-scale manufacturing, a factory. Commercial is design driven by selling, a business designing a product for market. Here's the key point the marker wants: the design process itself is the same in all four, define, research, ideate, produce, evaluate, refine. What changes is the scale, the resources, the formality, and the documentation. A child designing a billycart at home runs the same DRIPER as an engineering firm designing a car, the firm just does it with bigger budgets, formal management, teams, and far more rigorous evaluation. Same process, different scale. That's in Listing 5, and those four settings come back in detail next module under the production topic.

Define becomes design briefs and project analysis. Research becomes research methods and market research. Ideate becomes the creativity and cognitive-organisers topic. When you hear "major design project" later, hear "DRIPER, done properly, documented". Third, back to episode one: the process is just the theory-practice loop given six named stages, you research using theory, you produce using practice, you evaluate and refine to feed the result back. And back to episode two: it's the framework Newson adapts and the loop Dyson ran thousands of times. Nothing here is new information, it's the spine everything hangs on.

NARRATOR: One trap to flag. Students often think "evaluation" only happens at the very end. It doesn't, and saying so costs marks. Evaluation is continual, you evaluate throughout, at every stage, against your criteria. The textbook hammers this: successful designers test, reflect and evaluate constantly. If an exam asks where evaluation fits and you put it only at the end, you've missed the iterative nature, which is the heart of the topic. Evaluation is everywhere in the cycle.

NARRATOR: Recap, the way you'd write it for marks. One: the design process is DRIPER, Define, Research, Ideate, Produce, Evaluate, Refine, and you should be able to explain what happens at each stage. Two: the process is cyclic and iterative, not linear, evaluation is continual and feeds back, and a failure loops the designer to an earlier stage to repeat the cycle, which is what "analyse the processes" is asking you to explain. Three: it runs from initial client contact, the brief, to final presentation. Four: the same process is used across domestic, community, industrial and commercial settings, what changes is the scale and formality, not the process. NARRATOR: Now the exam-style questions, real NESA format, get a pen. Four questions, model answers in Listing 7.

QUESTION: Question one. Outline the stages of the design process. Three marks. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The design process consists of defining the need and brief, researching and investigating, generating and developing ideas, producing and prototyping the solution, evaluating it against the criteria, and refining it. You can use DRIPER as your structure. Three marks for outlining the stages in a logical order with a brief sense of what each involves. To be safe, add the line "and the process is cyclic, with the designer returning to earlier stages", because even on an "outline" it shows you understand the process properly.

QUESTION: Question two. Explain why the design process is described as iterative. Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The design process is iterative because the designer does not complete the stages once in a fixed linear order. Evaluation is carried out continually, and when a design does not meet its criteria for success the designer returns to an earlier stage, such as regenerating ideas or revising research, and repeats the cycle. This repeated looping progressively improves the design until it satisfies the identified need. Three marks: state it's non-linear, explain the feedback and looping back to earlier stages, and explain the effect, progressive improvement. The trap is describing it as a one-way list; the word "iterative" demands you explain the loop.

QUESTION: Question three. Describe and analyse the processes undertaken when designing a product of your choice. Six marks. This is the headline extended response for this topic, so pause the player and write a full answer before you play.

NARRATOR: Model answer, using the reusable drinks bottle. Describe the stages: define the need, a reusable, leak-proof bottle to reduce single-use plastic, with criteria such as capacity, leak-proofing and insulation; research existing bottles, materials and user needs; generate ideas through sketching multiple lid and body concepts; produce a prototype, a double-walled stainless-steel bottle; evaluate it against the criteria; and refine it. Then analyse the process: it is iterative and cyclic, not linear. When evaluation revealed the lid leaked, the designer looped back to the ideation stage to design a sealed flip lid, rebuilt the prototype, and re-evaluated, and a subsequent insulation failure prompted a further loop back to research. This continual evaluation and looping back is what allowed the design to be progressively improved until it met all the criteria. Six marks: the marks split between accurately describing the stages applied to a real product, and analysing the iterative looping with specific feedback examples. The students who only describe, and never analyse the loop, cap out around half marks. Both halves, every time.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain how the design process used in an industrial setting compares to one used in a domestic setting. Four marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The design process itself is fundamentally the same in both settings, both follow the stages of defining, researching, generating ideas, producing, evaluating and refining. What differs is the scale and rigour. In an industrial setting the process is conducted by teams with large budgets, formal management of action, time and finance, specialised equipment and rigorous, documented evaluation, because the product will be mass-produced and failure is costly. In a domestic setting the same process is run by an individual on a small scale, with limited resources, informal management and less formal documentation. Four marks: state that the process is the same, then contrast the scale, resources, formality and documentation, with an example of each. The key insight the marker wants is that the process does not change between settings, only its scale and formality do.
