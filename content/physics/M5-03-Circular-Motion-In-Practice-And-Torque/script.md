NARRATOR: Third scenario, the one students find hardest and the one examiners love: the vertical circle. Now swing the ball, or a bucket of water, in a vertical loop, over the top and under the bottom. This is not uniform circular motion anymore, the speed changes as it rises and falls, but at any instant the net inward force still equals mass times speed squared over radius, so net to the centre still rules. The trick is that the two real forces, gravity and tension, line up differently at different points. At the very top of the loop, both gravity and the tension point downward, and down is toward the centre, so they add: the tension plus the weight together provide the centripetal force. At the very bottom, gravity points down, away from the centre, but the tension points up, toward the centre, so now they fight: the tension has to overcome the weight and provide the centripetal force on top of that, so the tension equals mass times speed squared over radius plus the weight. That's why the string is under the most tension at the bottom and the least at the top, and it's exactly why a string is most likely to snap at the bottom of a swing.

NARRATOR: The top of the loop gives the single most famous result here, so let's derive it in your head. At the top, the centripetal force is the tension plus the weight. Now slow the ball down. As it goes slower, less centripetal force is needed, so the string goes slack, the tension drops. Keep slowing until the tension reaches zero: now gravity alone is providing all the centripetal force. That's the slowest the ball can possibly go and still make it round the top, the minimum speed. Set the weight equal to mass times speed squared over radius, the mass cancels, and the minimum speed at the top is the square root of g times the radius. Below that speed, gravity is more than enough to pull the ball toward the centre, so the ball falls away from the circle, the string goes fully slack, and the bucket of water lands on your head. This is why you have to swing a bucket fast enough: above the square root of g times the radius, the water stays in; below it, gravity wins. NARRATOR: Now the promised clean result, and it's a favourite two-mark exam grab: the centripetal force does no work. Here's the reasoning, and it comes straight from the work-energy episode in Module Two. Work equals the force times the displacement, but only the component of the force along the direction of motion counts. The centripetal force points toward the centre, and the object's motion, its velocity, points along the tangent, perpendicular to the radius. The force is at ninety degrees to the motion the whole way round. A force at right angles to the displacement does zero work, always. So the centripetal force does no work on the object. And that's not just a technicality, it explains something: because no work is done, no energy is transferred, so the object's kinetic energy doesn't change, so its speed stays constant. That's the deep reason uniform circular motion can go round forever at a steady speed while still being accelerated the whole time. The satellite in a circular orbit is the grand example, coming in two episodes: gravity is the centripetal force, gravity does no work on the circular orbit, so the satellite's speed and kinetic energy stay constant with no engine, forever. No work, constant speed. Listing five states it cleanly.

QUESTION: Quick retrieval, because this catches people out. If the centripetal force does no work, what does that tell you about the speed of an object in uniform circular motion, and does the same hold in the vertical circle? Pause the player, answer both, then play.

NARRATOR: In uniform circular motion, the centripetal force does no work, so no energy is transferred to or from the object, so its kinetic energy is constant, so its speed is constant. That's uniform circular motion by definition, constant speed. But in the vertical circle it's different, and here's the nuance: the centripetal force still does no work, because it's still perpendicular to the motion. What changes the speed in a vertical circle is gravity, specifically the component of gravity along the direction of motion, which is not the centripetal part. As the ball rises, gravity does negative work and it slows; as it falls, gravity does positive work and it speeds up, trading kinetic and gravitational potential energy exactly as you learned in the work-energy episode. So the centripetal component never changes the speed anywhere; in the vertical circle the along-track component of gravity does. Clean distinction, and it interleaves circular motion straight back into energy conservation.

NARRATOR: Now we change gears to the last dot-point of the topic, torque. Everything so far has been about forces causing an object to move along a curved path. Torque is about a force causing an object to rotate, to turn about a pivot. They're cousins, both about circular geometry, but don't confuse them: centripetal force is the net inward force keeping something on a circular path; torque is the turning effect of a force about an axis. Think of a spanner on a bolt, a hand on a door, a foot on a bike pedal. You already know intuitively how to make something turn more easily: push harder, and push further from the hinge. Everyone pushes a door at the handle, far from the hinges, not right next to them. Torque, symbol the Greek letter tau, is the quantity that captures exactly that, and its unit is the newton metre.

NARRATOR: The equation is torque equals the radius, times the force, times the sine of the angle between them. Radius times force times sine theta. Let me unpack the three ingredients, because each one is a way to get more turn. The radius is how far from the pivot you apply the force, the length of the spanner, the distance from the hinge, and a longer arm gives more torque, which is why a longer spanner loosens a stubborn bolt. The force is obvious, push harder, get more turn. And the sine of the angle is the subtle one: the angle is between the arm and the force, and the sine is largest, equal to one, when the angle is ninety degrees, when you push at right angles to the spanner. Push straight along the spanner, angle zero, sine zero, and you get no turning at all, you're just trying to stretch or compress it. So maximum torque comes from pushing at ninety degrees, square-on. Here's a memory hook for the three ways to boost torque: reach, force, square-on. Reach further out, push with more force, and apply it square-on at ninety degrees. Reach, force, square-on. That's your torque mnemonic; write it in the margin. The definition and worked example are in Listing six.

NARRATOR: One more way to read that sine, because the exam splits it two ways and rewards either. You can either take the force times the sine of the angle, which is the component of the force perpendicular to the arm, the only part that actually turns things, times the full radius. Or you can keep the full force and use the radius times the sine of the angle, which is the perpendicular distance from the pivot straight to the line of the force, a length engineers call the lever arm or moment arm. Both give the identical torque, radius times force times sine theta, they're just two ways of slicing the same product. If a question gives you the perpendicular distance directly, use force times that distance; if it gives you an angle, use radius times force times sine of the angle.
