NARRATOR: This is part of Module 7, The Nature of Light. And I want to start with something that sounds impossible. Imagine you have a wall with two narrow openings in it, and beyond that wall, a screen. You shine light at the wall, and some of that light slips through the two openings and lands on the screen. Now here is a spot on that screen, a particular point, that gets brightly lit when only one of the openings is uncovered. But the moment you uncover the second opening, that same spot goes dark. You just added more light, and part of the screen got dimmer.

QUESTION: How can adding a second source of light make a place darker?

NARRATOR: That single question, that little pocket of darkness that appears only when you let more light in, is one of the most important observations in the history of physics. It cracked open a debate that had raged for more than a century, it settled an argument between two of the greatest minds who ever lived, and it did it with an apparatus so simple you could build a version of it on your kitchen table tonight. But to understand why it mattered so much, you have to understand what people believed light was. And for a hundred years, the smart money was on the wrong answer.

NARRATOR: Go back to the end of the sixteen hundreds. There were two rival pictures of what light actually is, and they could not both be true. On one side stood Isaac Newton. Newton believed light was made of tiny particles, little corpuscles, he called them, streaming out from a source like an unimaginably fine spray of bullets. It was a beautiful idea because it explained so much. Light travels in straight lines, and particles fly in straight lines. Light bounces off a mirror, and particles bounce off a wall. Shadows have sharp edges, and of course they do, because the little bullets either get through or they get blocked. The corpuscular model just felt right, and it had Newton's name on it. And in the fields of physics in that era, Newton's name was very nearly the last word.

NARRATOR: On the other side stood a Dutchman, Christiaan Huygens. Huygens said no. Light is not a hail of particles. Light is a wave, a disturbance that spreads out through space the way ripples spread across a pond. Every point that a wave reaches becomes a new little source of ripples, and all those ripples add up to make the wave that rolls forward. It was an elegant idea too, and it also explained reflection, and it explained the way light bends when it passes from air into water or glass. So here were two models, both from brilliant men, both able to explain the everyday behaviour of light. And for the better part of a century, the argument sat in a kind of stalemate, with Newton's enormous reputation tipping the balance toward particles.

QUESTION: So how do you break a tie like that?

NARRATOR: You find something the two models disagree about. Not the things they both explain, but the one thing where they part ways, where one says this will happen and the other says the opposite. And there was such a thing. It came down to two behaviours that everyone agreed belonged to waves, and only to waves. The first is diffraction. When a wave passes the edge of an obstacle or squeezes through a narrow gap, it doesn't just carry straight on. It bends and spreads out into the space beyond, fanning outward. Think of ocean waves rolling through a narrow harbour mouth and then spreading in curves into the calm water inside. That spreading is diffraction.

NARRATOR: The second behaviour is interference, and this is the strange and beautiful one. When two waves overlap, they combine. Where a crest meets a crest, they reinforce, and you get a bigger crest, a taller wave. That's called constructive interference. But where a crest meets a trough, where one wave is pushing up exactly as the other pushes down, they cancel. They flatten each other out to nothing. That's destructive interference. You can watch it in a pond. Drop two stones a little apart, let the two sets of ripples spread until they cross, and you'll see a pattern where in some lines the water heaves up strongly and in other lines, in between, the surface goes eerily flat and still. Two sources of waves, and yet stillness. Two disturbances adding up to nothing.

NARRATOR: Here is the crucial point. Particles cannot do that. Two streams of little bullets, wherever they land, only ever add up. More bullets means more hits, means more, not less. There is no way for one bullet to cancel another and leave a bare patch. So interference was the fingerprint of a wave, unforgeable by any particle. If you could catch light in the act of interfering, of cancelling itself out to make darkness, then the argument was over. Newton's corpuscles could never do it. And that is exactly the trap that a remarkable Englishman set for light at the very start of the eighteen hundreds.

NARRATOR: His name was Thomas Young, and Young was almost absurdly gifted. He was a physician by training, but he was also one of those people who seems to be brilliant at everything he touches. He helped decode Egyptian hieroglyphics, working on the very Rosetta Stone. He studied the way the human eye focuses and understood colour vision. He read fluently as a small child and mastered a shelf of languages. And around the year eighteen hundred and one, Young turned that restless mind onto the oldest question about light, and he designed an experiment of breathtaking simplicity to answer it once and for all.

NARRATOR: Here was his reasoning, laid out as cleanly as a chess move. If Newton is right, and light is a stream of particles, then when you send light through two narrow slits, the particles fly straight through and you should see just two bright bands on the screen behind, one lit patch directly behind each slit, exactly where the corpuscles landed. Two slits, two stripes. Simple. But if Huygens is right, and light is a wave, then something much richer should happen. The light should diffract as it passes through each slit, spreading out into two overlapping fans. And where those two fans of light waves cross, they should interfere, crest reinforcing crest and crest cancelling trough, and paint the screen not with two stripes but with a whole series of bright and dark bands, a striped pattern, marching out from the centre. The two models made flatly different predictions. Look at the screen, and light itself would tell you which man was right.

QUESTION: So what did Young actually build?

NARRATOR: The setup was almost humble. He took a source of light and passed it first through a single narrow slit, and that first slit matters more than it looks, because it makes the light that comes through behave as though it comes from one clean, well-defined source, all marching in step. Then he let that light fall on a barrier with two slits in it, set close together, side by side. And beyond the two slits, at some distance, he placed a screen to catch whatever pattern the light chose to make. That's the whole machine. A source, a first slit to tidy the light, two slits to split it, and a screen to catch the verdict. There are no lenses doing anything clever, no elaborate machinery. The genius is entirely in the idea, in knowing exactly what to look for.

NARRATOR: And when the light came through and landed on that screen, it did not make two stripes. It made many. A row of bright bands separated by dark ones, a striped pattern spreading out across the screen, brightest in the very middle and fading gently as it went out to the sides. Bright, dark, bright, dark, bright, an orderly procession of light and shadow that no stream of particles could ever produce. Young was looking at an interference pattern made of light.

NARRATOR: And now go back to that impossible thing from the very beginning, because this is where it lives. Those dark bands are not places where light failed to arrive. Light from both slits reaches those dark bands. It arrives, and it cancels. At those spots, the wave from one slit is cresting exactly as the wave from the other slit is dipping, and they annihilate each other, and you get darkness. That is why uncovering the second slit can make a bright spot go dark. You didn't take light away. You added a second wave arriving perfectly out of step, and the two of them together add up to nothing at all. Only waves can do that. Only waves cancel. There it was, on the screen, in plain sight.

QUESTION: Why do some spots reinforce and others cancel?

NARRATOR: It all comes down to distance. Pick any point on the screen. The light reaching it has travelled from two different slits, and because those slits sit in slightly different places, the two paths are almost never exactly the same length. Off to one side, the wave from the far slit has to travel a little farther than the wave from the near slit. If that extra distance happens to be exactly one whole wavelength, or two, or any whole number of wavelengths, then the two waves arrive back in step, crest lined up with crest, and you get a bright band. But if the extra distance is half a wavelength, or one and a half, so that one wave arrives crest-up while the other arrives crest-down, they cancel, and you get a dark band. As you sweep your eye across the screen, that path difference changes smoothly, running through in-step, out-of-step, in-step, out-of-step, over and over, and that is precisely why the pattern is a regular march of bright and dark, and why it depends on the angle across the screen. The geometry writes the stripes.

NARRATOR: So there was Young's answer, painted in light and shadow. The diffraction pattern appeared. Light had diffracted through the slits and interfered with itself, and it had done the one thing a particle could never do. It had cancelled itself into darkness. And that settled it. Huygens' wave model of light was superior to Newton's when it came to describing how light travels through space, because the interference and the diffraction that Young had just shown, plain and repeatable, simply cannot be explained by a model of particles. Those properties belong to waves and to waves alone.

NARRATOR: Now think about the weight of what that meant. For a hundred years, the particle model had ridden on the authority of the greatest physicist who had ever lived. And here was a physician with two slits and a screen, quietly demonstrating that on this question the great man had been wrong. It took time, of course. Overturning Newton was not something the scientific world did lightly, and Young met real resistance. But the evidence was on the screen, and it would not go away. Anyone could set up the slits and see the bands for themselves. Over the following decades the wave theory of light won the argument comprehensively, and it opened the door to the whole science of light as a wave, eventually to the understanding of light as an electromagnetic wave, a ripple in electric and magnetic fields crossing empty space.

NARRATOR: And there is one final twist, a coda that Young could never have seen coming, because it makes his little experiment even stranger than he knew. Around a century later, physicists learned to send light through those two slits one tiny piece at a time, one particle of light, one photon, so faint that only a single one is passing through the apparatus at any moment. And the interference pattern still builds up, band by band, dot by dot, as if each single particle of light somehow travels through both slits at once and interferes with itself. That discovery cracked open the whole world of quantum mechanics, and physicists still argue about what it means today. But that is a story for another episode. What matters here is that it all begins with Thomas Young, two narrow slits, and a patch of darkness that should not exist.
