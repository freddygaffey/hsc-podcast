NARRATOR: This is part of Module Five, Advanced Mechanics. And I want to start with a confession made by a man staring at a table of numbers in the year sixteen eighteen. He wrote that when he first saw the pattern hiding in his data, he thought he was dreaming. He genuinely believed his tired eyes were playing a trick on him. But he checked it, and he checked it again, and it held. A single, exact relationship, buried in the wandering of the planets, that nobody in the history of the human race had ever seen before.

QUESTION: So what could possibly be in a column of numbers that would make a grown astronomer think he had lost his mind?

NARRATOR: Hold that thought, because to feel why it mattered, you have to understand what people believed about the sky, and how spectacularly wrong the smartest people alive had been about it for two thousand years.

NARRATOR: For most of human history, the answer to where we live was obvious. You are standing still. The ground does not move. And above you, the whole of the heavens turns once a day, the sun, the moon, the stars, all of it wheeling around the Earth. This is the geocentric model, the Earth at the centre, and it was not a stupid idea. It matched what your eyes tell you. It matched a deep human intuition that we are the point of the whole arrangement. And for over a thousand years it was backed by the full authority of the ancient philosophers and the church.

NARRATOR: But there was a crack in it. A handful of lights in the sky refused to behave. The ancient Greeks called them the wanderers. We call them the planets. Most stars hold perfect formation, night after night, year after year. But Mars, and Jupiter, and the others, drift against that background, and worse than that, every so often a planet stops, reverses, loops backwards across the sky, and then carries on forwards again.

QUESTION: Why would a planet appear to stop and travel backwards, if everything is neatly circling the Earth?

NARRATOR: In the geocentric model, it shouldn't. To rescue the theory, astronomers had bolted on wheels within wheels, little circles riding on big circles, called epicycles, dozens of them, tuned by hand until the model coughed up roughly the right positions. It was a machine of pure patchwork. And it still didn't quite work.

NARRATOR: Then a Polish astronomer named Nicolaus Copernicus dared to move the furniture. What if, he said, the Earth is not the centre. What if the sun sits at the middle, and the Earth is just one more planet, falling around it like all the others. This is the heliocentric model, the sun at the centre. And suddenly that maddening backwards loop of Mars had a clean explanation. Mars only appears to reverse when we, on a faster inner track, overtake it, the way a car you pass on the motorway seems to slide backwards even though it is moving forwards the whole time.

QUESTION: So if the sun-centred picture explained the loops so beautifully, why didn't everyone just accept it?

NARRATOR: Because it still didn't predict the planets accurately enough. Copernicus had kept one fatal assumption from the ancients, the belief that the heavens must be perfect, and perfection meant circles. He built his sun-centred cosmos out of circular orbits, and circles simply are not the true shape. So his predictions drifted off from reality, and for a long time his model was, if anything, no better at forecasting than the old one. A more beautiful idea, yes. But science does not run on beauty. It runs on prediction. And on prediction, the sun-centred model had not yet earned its crown.

NARRATOR: This is the world that Johannes Kepler was born into. A brilliant, half-mystical German mathematician, convinced deep in his bones that the universe was written in the language of geometry, and that God would not have built it sloppily. Kepler believed the sun sat at the centre. But belief was not enough. He needed proof. He needed the sky to obey a law, an exact mathematical rule, and if he could find one, it would be evidence no epicycle could fake.

NARRATOR: And here is the second piece of luck in this story, because a law is only as good as the data you test it on. Kepler had access to the finest set of astronomical observations that had ever been made, gathered by a flamboyant Danish nobleman named Tycho Brahe. Tycho had a silver nose, having lost his real one in a duel, a private island observatory, and an obsession with precision that bordered on the fanatical. For decades, before the telescope, using giant metal instruments and nothing but his own trained eye, Tycho measured the positions of the planets night after night, more accurately than anyone in history.

QUESTION: So what happens when the best data ever collected meets a man who refuses to accept anything but an exact law?

NARRATOR: You get one of the great obsessive struggles in the history of science. Kepler wrestled with Tycho's numbers for years. First he cracked the shape of the orbits, proving they were not circles at all but ellipses, gentle ovals, with the sun sitting slightly off to one side. That was his first law. Then he worked out how a planet speeds up when it swings close to the sun and slows down when it drifts far away. That was his second. But the prize he was really hunting was different, and bigger. He wanted a single rule that tied all the planets together. Not a law for Mars, and a separate one for Jupiter. One law for the entire solar system at once.

NARRATOR: So think about what he had in front of him. For each planet, two numbers. How far it sits from the sun, its mean distance, and how long it takes to go around once, its period. Mercury, hugging the sun, whips around in about eighty-eight days. Earth takes a year. Jupiter, far out in the cold, lumbers around in nearly twelve years. Saturn, further still, takes almost thirty. So the further out you go, the longer the year. Everybody already knew that much. The question was the exact shape of the relationship. Not that distance and period are linked, but precisely how.

QUESTION: Is it simply that if a planet is twice as far out, its year is twice as long?

NARRATOR: No, and that is the trap. It is not a simple doubling. Saturn is roughly nine and a half times further from the sun than the Earth, but its year is not nine and a half times longer, it is almost thirty times longer. The relationship is stretched, lopsided, and for a long time it looked like it might just be a mess with no clean rule inside it at all.

NARRATOR: And this is the moment. Kepler took each planet's distance and cubed it, multiplied it by itself three times, distance times distance times distance. Then he took each planet's period and squared it, multiplied it by itself. And then he divided the one by the other. He was looking to see whether that ratio, distance cubed divided by period squared, came out the same for every single planet.

NARRATOR: Let me read you what fell out. For Mercury, the number comes to about seven point six. For Venus, seven point five. For Earth, seven point five. For Mars, seven point five. For Jupiter, seven point five. For Saturn, seven point four. Six planets. Six wildly different worlds, from scorched little Mercury to distant frozen Saturn, spanning distances and years that differ by enormous factors. And when you cube the distance and square the period and take the ratio, they all land on the same number.

QUESTION: Do you feel it? Do you feel why he thought he was dreaming?

NARRATOR: Six independent worlds, obeying one identical rule to within a fraction of a percent. This is Kepler's third law. The cube of a planet's mean distance from the sun is directly proportional to the square of its orbital period. Distance cubed over period squared, always the same constant, for everything that orbits the sun. In his own words, translated from his book The Harmonies of the World, he wrote that the ratio between the periods of any two planets is precisely the ratio of the three-halves power of their mean distances. He had found the single law binding the whole solar system together.

NARRATOR: And notice what he did with the small imperfections. The numbers are not identical to the last decimal. Mercury's ratio sits a touch high, Saturn's a touch low. A lesser scientist might have panicked, or fudged the data to make it perfect. Kepler did not. He understood that Tycho's instruments, magnificent as they were, were not flawless, and that the planets tug gently on one another with their own gravity, nudging the orbits away from perfect ellipses. The tiny discrepancies were not a failure of the law. They were the honest fingerprints of a real universe, measured by real hands.

QUESTION: So a man finds a neat pattern in six numbers. Why does that change physics forever?

NARRATOR: Because this was the hard, quantitative evidence the sun-centred universe had always been missing. The old Earth-centred model gave you no reason to expect any relationship at all between a planet's distance and its period. But here was a law, exact, universal, that only makes sense if the planets truly circle the sun, ruled by something reaching out from the centre. Kepler could not yet say what that something was. That answer would come decades later, when Isaac Newton showed that this exact law drops out, inevitably, from a single idea, universal gravitation, the same force that pulls an apple to the ground reaching all the way out to hold Saturn in its orbit. Kepler's third law was the empirical footprint. Newton found the creature that made it.

NARRATOR: And it did not stay locked in the seventeenth century. That same relationship, distance cubed proportional to period squared, is the tool engineers use today to place a satellite in exactly the right orbit. Want a satellite that hangs motionless over one spot on the equator, for television or weather or navigation? Kepler's third law tells you precisely how high to put it, so its period matches one turn of the Earth. Four centuries later, we are still flying on the law that made a man doubt his own eyes.

NARRATOR: So the next time someone tells you data is just numbers, remember a half-mystical astronomer in sixteen eighteen, cubing distances and squaring periods by candlelight, until six planets whispered back the same answer, and the shape of the solar system finally gave up its secret.
