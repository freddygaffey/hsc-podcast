NARRATOR: This is part of Module 8, From the Universe to the Atom. And it begins with a family feud settled by physics. Here is the puzzle. A father wins the Nobel Prize for proving, beyond any doubt, that the electron is a tiny particle, a little grain of matter with a fixed mass and a fixed charge. Then his own son wins a Nobel Prize for proving the exact opposite, that the electron is a wave, spread out, rippling, interfering with itself. And here is the part that should stop you cold. They were both right. So how can the same thing be a solid particle to the father and a spreading wave to the son? That contradiction is the whole story.

QUESTION: Who were these two men?

NARRATOR: The father was J. J. Thomson. In the eighteen nineties, working at Cambridge, he took a sealed glass tube, pumped out the air, and fired a mysterious glowing ray across it. By bending that ray with electric and magnetic fields, he showed it was made of tiny negatively charged pieces, far smaller than any atom. He had discovered the electron. And in his hands, the electron behaved exactly like what you would expect a piece of matter to be. It flew in straight lines, it carried momentum, it hit a screen and made a spot. A particle, plain and simple. The world agreed. The electron was the smallest brick in the wall of matter.

NARRATOR: But by the nineteen twenties, a strange new idea was loose in physics. A young French aristocrat named Louis de Broglie had made an outrageous suggestion. If light, which everyone had thought was a wave, could sometimes behave like a stream of particles, then maybe matter, which everyone thought was particles, could sometimes behave like a wave. He even wrote down how. The wavelength of a piece of matter, he said, equals a tiny fixed number of nature, Planck's constant, divided by the object's momentum. Small momentum, longer wave. Large momentum, vanishingly short wave. For a cricket ball the wave is so absurdly tiny you could never see it. But for something as light and fast as an electron, the wave might just be big enough to catch.

QUESTION: How would you ever catch a wave that small?

NARRATOR: You use the one trick that only waves can do. Diffraction and interference. Send a wave through a gap about the same size as its wavelength, or past a regular array of obstacles, and it bends, spreads, and overlaps with itself. Where crests meet crests you get a bright band. Where crests meet troughs, they cancel to nothing. The result is a pattern, alternating bright and dark, that particles simply cannot make. Pattern like that is the unmistakable fingerprint of a wave. And here is the beautiful part. Physicists already knew a natural array with just the right spacing. The neat, repeating rows of atoms inside a crystal. The gaps between those atomic layers are almost exactly the size you would need. Years earlier, they had already fired X-rays, which everyone agreed were waves, through thin crystals and thin metal foils, and watched them come out the far side spread into rings. So there was a template. If electrons were waves, they should do the very same thing.

NARRATOR: This is where the son walks in. George Paget Thomson, G. P. Thomson, son of the man who had proved the electron was a particle. In nineteen twenty seven, in his laboratory, he set out to test de Broglie's wild idea with an experiment of striking simplicity. He took a beam of electrons and accelerated it inside a tube from which almost all the air had been pumped, as near to a perfect vacuum as he could reach, so the electrons could fly cleanly without colliding with stray gas molecules. Then he aimed that beam at a target so thin it was barely there. A sheet of gold foil, hammered down to a whisper of thickness, just a few hundred atoms deep. The electrons punched straight through it and carried on to a photographic plate on the other side.

QUESTION: If electrons are just particles, what should appear on that plate?

NARRATOR: If the electron were only the hard little pellet his father had discovered, the answer is boring. The particles would rush through the foil in roughly straight lines and pile up as a single bright blob in the centre, and nothing more. That is what a stream of bullets through a screen door would do. But that is not what G. P. Thomson found.

NARRATOR: When he developed the plate, there around the central spot were rings. Clean, concentric circles of light and dark, ringed around the middle like ripples frozen on a pond. Bright ring, dark gap, bright ring, dark gap, spreading outward in perfect symmetry. That pattern cannot come from particles travelling in straight lines. It can only come from waves, waves that had passed through the ordered rows of gold atoms, bent around them, and interfered with themselves, adding up to bright rings in some directions and cancelling to darkness in others. And crucially, it was the very same kind of ring pattern the X-rays had made. The electrons, streams of the most particle-like thing anyone knew, had behaved exactly like a wave.

QUESTION: So had he proved de Broglie's whole equation?

NARRATOR: This is worth being careful about, because it is a favourite trap. What Thomson showed, beyond argument, was that matter has a wave-like nature. The diffraction rings are direct, visible evidence that electrons diffract and interfere, just as de Broglie had dared to predict. But notice what this particular experiment does and does not nail down. Unlike the famous Davisson and Germer experiment being done at almost the same moment across the Atlantic, Thomson's gold-foil work is remembered as evidence for the wave nature itself, rather than as a measurement that pinned down the exact wavelength and momentum and confirmed de Broglie's precise formula, that wavelength equals Planck's constant over momentum. So if an exam ever asks you for the one experiment that quantitatively confirms the de Broglie relationship, reach for Davisson and Germer. Thomson is your evidence that matter waves are real.

NARRATOR: And now, back to the family. J. J. Thomson had discovered the electron and shown it was a particle. His son, G. P. Thomson, had shown that same electron was a wave. Both men were right, because the electron is genuinely both, a particle when you count it and a wave when you let it spread. That is wave-particle duality, made visible on a photographic plate. Ten years after these rings appeared, in nineteen thirty seven, G. P. Thomson stood up to collect a Nobel Prize for proving the electron is a wave, forty years after his father had won one for proving it is a particle. Two Nobel Prizes, one family, two opposite truths, and no contradiction at all.
