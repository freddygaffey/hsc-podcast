NARRATOR: This is part of Module Seven, The Nature of Light. And I want to start with something that sounds impossible. Light is the fastest thing in the universe. In one single second, a beam of light could circle the entire Earth more than seven times. It crosses the gap from the Sun to your eyes in about eight minutes, having travelled a hundred and fifty million kilometres. Now imagine trying to catch that. Imagine trying to time it, not by looking at the stars, not by watching distant moons, but with a bench, some mirrors, and a room barely twenty metres across. Standing still, in a Paris laboratory. It seems like measuring the length of a lightning bolt with a ruler.

QUESTION: So how do you time the fastest thing there is, inside a single room?

NARRATOR: That was the puzzle. And for centuries, the honest answer was: you don't. Because for most of human history, a huge number of very clever people believed you couldn't. They believed light didn't take any time at all.

NARRATOR: Go back far enough and the greatest minds were split. Aristotle insisted that light was simply there, instantaneous, present everywhere the moment a candle was lit. When you open your eyes at dawn, the light of distant stars arrives at that very instant. No delay. No travel time. Light, to them, wasn't a thing that moved. It just was. And when you think about everyday life, you can see why. Flick a switch and the far wall lights up. There's no waiting, no gap you can feel. It looks, for all the world, infinitely fast.

NARRATOR: The first crack in that idea came not from a laboratory but from the heavens. A Danish astronomer named Ole Romer, watching the moons of Jupiter, noticed that their eclipses ran late when the Earth was far from Jupiter and early when it was close. The only explanation that fit was that light took time to cross the extra distance. It was finite after all. But that measurement leaned entirely on the solar system, on planetary distances nobody knew precisely. And there was a nagging, almost philosophical worry underneath it all. What if light behaved differently out there in the vacuum of space than it did down here on Earth? What if the number you got from Jupiter's moons wasn't the number you'd get in a room?

QUESTION: Could anyone measure the speed of light without ever leaving the ground?

NARRATOR: This was the challenge that lit a fire under French physics in the middle of the eighteen hundreds. And the first man to really answer it was Hippolyte Fizeau. About a decade before our story, Fizeau built an ingenious contraption: a spinning toothed wheel that chopped a beam of light into flashes, fired those flashes at a mirror kilometres away, and caught them again in the gaps between the teeth. It worked. For the first time in history, a human being had measured the speed of light without looking at the sky. It was a genuine triumph. But it wasn't precise. The toothed wheel was a blunt instrument, and Fizeau's answer was in the right neighbourhood but not much better than that.

NARRATOR: And that gap, that "close but not quite," is where our man walks in. Leon Foucault. A former medical student who had abandoned the study of medicine because he couldn't stand the sight of blood, and who instead became one of the most gifted experimental physicists France ever produced. If that name sounds familiar, it's because Foucault is the same man who hung an enormous pendulum from the ceiling of the Pantheon in Paris and let the whole world watch the Earth turn beneath it. He had a genius for taking a truth too big to see and shrinking it down onto a bench where you could point at it. And now he set his sights on light itself. He didn't just want to measure it. He wanted to do it better than anyone had, and to do it inside a single room.

NARRATOR: Here is the beautiful idea at the heart of what he built. Forget the toothed wheel. Foucault took a small mirror and set it spinning. Spinning astonishingly fast, hundreds of turns every second. He shone a beam of light at that spinning mirror, and the mirror flung the beam across the room to a second mirror, a stationary one, standing still and facing back. That far mirror bounced the light straight back the way it came, back toward the spinning mirror, and back toward the source.

QUESTION: So if the far mirror just sends the light straight back, how does that measure anything?

NARRATOR: Here's the trick, and it's exquisite. Light is fast, but it is not infinitely fast. While the beam was away, travelling to the far mirror and back, the spinning mirror never stopped turning. In the tiny sliver of time that round trip took, the mirror had rotated onward, just a hair, to a slightly new angle. So when the returning light finally arrived back at that spinning mirror, it didn't meet the same mirror it had left. It met a mirror that had turned. And a turned mirror reflects light in a turned direction. So the beam came back not to where it started, but deflected, thrown off to the side by a tiny angle. A little smudge of displacement. And that tiny deflection is a stopwatch. It is time, written in angle.

NARRATOR: Think about what Foucault now had in his hands. He knew how fast the mirror was spinning, because he set that himself, its angular velocity was known. He knew the distance from the spinning mirror to the far stationary mirror. And he could measure that tiny angle of deflection in the returning beam. Three numbers he could pin down. And from those three, the speed of light falls out.

NARRATOR: Let me walk you through the logic, because it's cleaner than it sounds. Speed is just distance divided by time. The distance the light travelled is out to the far mirror and back, so it's twice the gap between the mirrors. That's the distance. Now the time. The time for that round trip is exactly the time the mirror spent rotating through its little angle. And here's the thing about a spinning object: the time it takes to turn through an angle is simply that angle divided by how fast it's spinning. So if you know the angle it swept and you know its angular velocity, you know the time. The mirror has quietly become a clock, its own rotation ticking off the fractions of a second the light was in flight.

QUESTION: But wait, how much did the mirror actually rotate compared to how much the beam swings?

NARRATOR: This is the subtle point, and it's pure law of reflection. The angle the mirror turns through is only half the angle you see in the deflected beam. When you rotate a mirror by some amount, the reflected ray doesn't swing by that same amount, it swings by twice as much. Rotate the mirror one notch, and the plane of reflection turns one notch, but the angle of incidence changes too, so the outgoing ray is thrown a further notch on top. The beam always swings double. So when Foucault measured the deflection of his returning light, he had to halve it to get the true angle the mirror had rotated. Miss that factor of two and your answer is wrong by exactly half.

NARRATOR: Put it all together and the whole experiment collapses into one relationship. The speed of light equals twice the distance between the mirrors, multiplied by the mirror's angular velocity, divided by the angle it turned through. Twice the distance, times how fast it spins, over the deflection angle. Distance he knew. Angular velocity he knew. The angle he measured. And out the other side came a number.

NARRATOR: Two hundred and ninety-eight thousand kilometres per second. Two hundred and ninety-eight thousand kilometres, every single second. Sit with that for a moment, because of what it represents. Not a guess from the motion of Jupiter's moons. Not a rough figure from a chopping wheel. This was the most successful measurement of the speed of light anyone had ever made, and it was done on a table, over a distance of only about twenty metres. Twenty metres. The length of a swimming pool. From that little bench, Foucault had caught the fastest thing in existence and pinned a number to it that stands remarkably close to the value we use today.

NARRATOR: And it did more than just get the number right. Because Foucault could shrink the whole thing into a room, he could do something Romer, staring at the sky, never could. He could fill the space between his mirrors with water instead of air, and watch what happened to the speed. When he did, the light slowed down. And that single result was a hammer blow in one of the oldest fights in physics, the war over whether light was a stream of particles or a wave. The particle theory, Newton's theory, had demanded that light speed up in water. The wave theory demanded it slow down. Foucault's spinning mirror said, plainly, slower. Waves won. A quiet contraption on a bench had reached out and settled an argument that had raged for a hundred and fifty years.

NARRATOR: That's the thing about this experiment. It looks, at first, like it shouldn't be possible. Time the fastest thing there is, standing still, in a room. But Foucault found the one place time was hiding, in the turn of a spinning mirror, and he let the mirror do the timing for him. He turned a smudge of light, deflected by a fraction of a degree, into one of the great numbers of physics.

NARRATOR: If you want to see where this fits, take it back to your work on the nature and speed of light in Module Seven, and set Foucault's rotating mirror right alongside Fizeau's toothed wheel and Romer's moons, three different ways humanity finally caught the light.
