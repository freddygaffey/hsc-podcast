NARRATOR: This is part of Module Seven, The Nature of Light. And I want to start with a number that almost everyone in physics believed for nearly fifty years. The speed of light, they said, was very close to two hundred and ninety-nine thousand, seven hundred and seventy-four kilometres per second. That number was in the textbooks. It was in the tables engineers trusted. It had been measured by the most famous experimentalist in America, a Nobel laureate, using mirrors spinning so fast they screamed. And it was wrong. Not by a lot. But by enough. And the man who proved it wrong did it in a lab in England, with a copper box the size of a loaf of bread, and no spinning mirrors at all.

QUESTION: So how do you measure the fastest thing in the universe with a box that doesn't move?

NARRATOR: That is the puzzle at the heart of this story, and to feel why it's so strange, you have to understand how everyone else had been doing it. For centuries, the speed of light was measured the obvious way. You take light, you send it a very long distance, and you time it. The trouble is, light is absurdly fast. In one second it could circle the Earth more than seven times. So to time it over any distance a human can build, you need a clock that can slice a second into millionths, and in the eighteen hundreds, nobody had one.

NARRATOR: So the great experimenters got clever. A Frenchman named Fizeau sent a beam of light through the gap in a spinning toothed wheel, off to a mirror miles away, and back. Spin the wheel fast enough, and the returning light would be blocked by the next tooth instead of slipping back through the gap. From the speed of the wheel and the distance to the mirror, he could work out how fast the light had travelled. Then came Foucault with a rotating mirror, and then the master of the whole art, Albert Michelson, who spent decades building spinning mirror machines across the mountains of California, timing light as it raced between distant peaks.

NARRATOR: And these were beautiful experiments. Michelson's final numbers were treasured. But every single one of them shared the same weakness. They all depended on measuring an enormous distance and an unimaginably short time, and both of those measurements had errors baked in. The haze in the air, the exact position of a mirror on a far-off mountain, the flex of a spinning machine. The tiny uncertainties piled up, and the accepted value of the speed of light drifted, and no one could shrink the error much further. The whole approach had hit a wall.

QUESTION: What if you never had to time the light at all?

NARRATOR: This is where Louis Essen comes in. Essen was a physicist at the National Physical Laboratory in England, and he was, by every account, stubborn, exacting, and gloriously willing to tell famous people they were wrong. He had a hunch. He believed that if you stopped trying to race light across a valley, and instead trapped it, and studied the pattern it made when it stood still, you could measure its speed far more precisely than anyone had managed by chasing it.

NARRATOR: To see what he meant, forget light for a moment and think of a guitar string. Pluck it, and it vibrates in a fixed pattern. The ends are pinned down and never move; those still points are called nodes. Between them, the string swings its widest; those are the antinodes. The pattern doesn't travel anywhere. It just sits there, humming. We call it a standing wave. And here's the key idea Essen was chasing. If you can measure the physical distance between two nodes, you know the wavelength of that wave, because the distance between two nodes is exactly one half of a wavelength. Measure that gap, double it, and you have the wavelength, from a simple measurement with a ruler.

NARRATOR: Now, waves obey one of the most reliable relationships in all of physics. The speed of a wave equals its frequency multiplied by its wavelength. Frequency is just how many times per second the wave oscillates. So if Essen could set up a standing wave, measure its wavelength from the distance between the nodes, and separately measure its frequency, he could simply multiply the two together and get the speed. No mountains. No spinning mirrors. No timing a flash across a valley.

QUESTION: But light waves are far too small to lay a ruler against. So what did he trap instead?

NARRATOR: Essen didn't use visible light. He used its slower, fatter cousin, microwaves. Microwaves are the same stuff as light, the same electromagnetic waves travelling at the same speed, but with wavelengths you can actually measure with instruments, wavelengths of centimetres rather than millionths of a millimetre. And to trap them, he built a cavity resonator: a hollow, precisely machined box of copper, its inside walls polished so the microwaves would bounce cleanly back and forth without leaking away.

NARRATOR: He fed microwaves into this copper cavity from an oscillator whose frequency he could tune by hand. And here's the elegant part. Most frequencies do nothing special; the waves bounce around and interfere with themselves into a mush. But at certain exact frequencies, the wave fits the box perfectly. The reflections line up, crest on crest, and a clean, powerful standing wave springs into existence inside the cavity, the electromagnetic version of a plucked guitar string. Essen would slowly turn the dial, and watch for the sudden, unmistakable surge that told him the wave had locked into resonance.

NARRATOR: And now everything he needed was inside that box. Because he had machined the cavity himself, he knew its internal dimensions to extraordinary precision. And because he knew how many nodes the standing wave contained, the number of loops fitting between the walls, he could work backwards to the exact wavelength of the microwaves. Meanwhile the frequency came from his oscillator, and frequency was the one thing physicists could already measure with staggering accuracy, far better than any distance or any time interval in the old mirror experiments. So he had a superbly precise wavelength, and a superbly precise frequency. He multiplied them together.

NARRATOR: The answer came out to two hundred and ninety-nine thousand, seven hundred and ninety-two point five kilometres per second, give or take one kilometre per second.

QUESTION: So who was right? Essen in his quiet lab, or Michelson on his mountains?

NARRATOR: Essen's number was faster than the long-accepted value by something like sixteen or seventeen kilometres per second. Now that sounds tiny against three hundred thousand. But in this game, precision is everything, and the gap was far larger than the error bars on either side. Somebody was wrong. And when a lone physicist with a copper box contradicts decades of celebrated mirror experiments, the natural reaction is not applause. It's doubt. Essen's result was met with real resistance. The old value had authority, history, and a Nobel Prize behind it.

NARRATOR: But Essen was right. As other laboratories built their own resonant-cavity and microwave experiments through the nineteen fifties, they didn't drift back toward the old figure. They closed in on Essen's. The standing-wave method turned out to be so much cleaner, so much less prone to the creeping errors of distance and timing, that it simply blew the old approach away. The accepted speed of light was revised, upward, to match the man with the copper box.

NARRATOR: And that mattered far more than winning an argument. A precise, trusted value for the speed of light sits underneath a huge amount of modern science and technology. It's woven into radar, into the interpretation of astronomical distances, into the equations engineers use to build the world. Essen had turned the speed of light from a quantity you chase across a landscape into a quantity you pin down on a laboratory bench. And it was so reliable that eventually, physicists did something remarkable. They stopped measuring the speed of light altogether, and instead fixed it as an exact defined number, and used it to define the metre itself. The measurement became the ruler.

NARRATOR: There's one last, almost cheeky footnote. The physics Essen used is not some exotic, unreachable thing. Standing electromagnetic waves are all around you. In fact, you can find the antinodes of a microwave standing wave in your own kitchen. Take the turntable out of a microwave oven, lay a flat sheet of chocolate inside, and run it for a few seconds. The chocolate melts first at the antinodes, the points of maximum energy, leaving a row of melted spots. Measure the distance between them, double it for the wavelength, multiply by the frequency printed on the back of the oven, and you will get, roughly, the speed of light. In your kitchen. With chocolate. The very same principle that overturned fifty years of physics.
