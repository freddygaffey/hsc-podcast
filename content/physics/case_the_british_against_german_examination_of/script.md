NARRATOR: This is part of Module 8, From the Universe to the Atom. And I want to start with a mystery that split the greatest physicists in Europe into two camps who could not stand to be wrong. Picture a sealed glass tube, most of the air sucked out of it, with a metal plate at one end wired up to a fearsome voltage. Switch it on, and something invisible streams out of that plate, crosses the empty space, and makes the far end of the glass glow a soft, eerie green. Nobody could see the thing itself. They could only see where it landed. They called it a cathode ray. And here is the question that had the finest minds of two nations at each other's throats.

QUESTION: Was that glowing beam a wave, like light, or was it a hail of tiny particles?

NARRATOR: It sounds like a small, technical squabble. It was not. The answer would crack the atom open and reveal the first piece of matter smaller than an atom itself. But in the eighteen nineties, nobody knew that. What they had was a beautiful glowing tube and two completely opposite explanations for it.

NARRATOR: On one side stood the Germans, led by Heinrich Hertz, the man who had already proven that radio waves were real. Hertz and his school were sure the cathode rays were some kind of wave, some disturbance travelling through the ether like light. On the other side stood the British, and above all a sharp, restless experimenter at Cambridge named Joseph John Thomson, known to everyone simply as J. J. The British bet on particles. Tiny lumps of something, flying through the tube.

NARRATOR: And here is what makes this story delicious. For years, the evidence was on the German side.

QUESTION: So why did the smart money say wave?

NARRATOR: Three reasons, and every one of them looked convincing. First, the cathode rays could pass straight through a thin sheet of aluminium. Think about that. If you fire pebbles at a metal plate, they bounce off. But these rays slipped through a solid aluminium foil as if it were a window. Waves do things like that. Light passes through glass. So maybe these rays were waves that could sail through certain materials. Point to the Germans.

NARRATOR: Second, and this was the big one, Hertz tried to push the beam sideways with an electric field. He put charged metal plates above and below the beam. If the rays were charged particles, an electric field should shove them off course, bending the beam toward the positive plate. Hertz switched on his field and watched. The beam did not budge. Not a flicker. And to Hertz that was close to proof. A charged particle cannot ignore an electric field. This thing ignored it. Therefore, he reasoned, it cannot be charged, and if it is not charged, it is not the stream of particles the British keep talking about.

NARRATOR: Third, there was the matter of magnetism. Everyone knew, from Ampere, that a moving electric charge creates a magnetic field around it, a field strong enough to twitch a nearby compass needle. So Hertz looked for that magnetic signature near his beam. He did not find it. No moving charges, it seemed. Another point for the wave.

NARRATOR: So there it was. Rays that pass through metal, ignore electric fields, and show no sign of moving charge. If you had to bet in the year eighteen ninety-four, you would have bet German. You would have bet wave. And you would have been wrong. Which brings us to the man who was about to overturn all of it.

QUESTION: What did Thomson see that Hertz missed?

NARRATOR: Thomson started not with electricity but with magnets. He brought a magnetic field near the cathode ray beam, and the beam swung aside effortlessly, dramatically, like a rope caught in a gust. That alone was interesting. But Thomson watched two things very carefully. First, the direction the beam bent told him the sign of the charge. Working through the geometry of the magnetic force, he found the beam behaved exactly as a stream of negative charges should. And second, when he turned up the strength of the magnet, the beam bent further, and it bent in proportion. Double the field, double the deflection. That clean, proportional response is the fingerprint of a charged particle obeying a force, not a vague wave sloshing around. To Thomson this was the tell. These rays carried negative charge. They were particles.

NARRATOR: But he still had Hertz's electric field result staring him down. Hertz had tried to bend the beam with an electric field and failed. If these really were charged particles, why on earth had the electric field done nothing?

QUESTION: How do you get a charged particle to ignore an electric force?

NARRATOR: Thomson worked out that Hertz had been sabotaged by his own apparatus, and the culprit was the gas. Hertz had not pumped enough air out of his tube. Here is what was happening. When you put a strong electric field across a tube that still has gas in it, the field rips electrons off the gas atoms and creates ions, charged fragments of gas. Those ions drift and pile up against the walls of the tube. The positive ions gather along the side that is supposed to be repelling the beam, and they form a screen. Physicists call it shielding. That cloud of stray charge cancels out the field inside the tube, so the delicate cathode ray beam feels almost nothing. Hertz's field was never really reaching the beam. It was being smothered by the very gas Hertz had failed to remove.

NARRATOR: There was a second, subtler thing too. The electrons in the beam were moving astonishingly fast. A weak sideways electric field barely nudges something screaming past at enormous speed. The magnetic force, by contrast, actually grows with the particle's velocity, so it gets a helping hand from that same blistering speed. That is part of why magnets deflected the beam so easily while electric fields seemed feeble. So Thomson did the obvious, difficult thing. He pumped his tube down to a far harder vacuum than Hertz ever managed. He got the gas out. And with the shielding gone, he switched on his electric field, and the beam bent. The result Hertz called impossible was sitting right there, once you cleaned up the experiment.

NARRATOR: Now Thomson had both. A magnetic field bent the beam one way, an electric field bent it the other, and he could tune them to fight each other to a standstill. And in that balance lay a number. By balancing the two forces, and then letting the magnetic force act alone, Thomson could extract the ratio of the particle's charge to its mass. When he did the arithmetic, the answer was staggering. The charge-to-mass ratio was more than a thousand times larger than that of a hydrogen ion, the lightest thing anyone knew. Either these particles carried an enormous charge, or they were unbelievably, impossibly light. Thomson made the bold call. They were light. Fantastically light. Nearly two thousand times lighter than a hydrogen atom.

NARRATOR: And crucially, it did not matter what metal he made the cathode from, or what trace of gas remained. The same particle came out every time, with the same tiny mass and the same negative charge. This was not a property of one material. This was something universal, a building block hiding inside every atom of every element on Earth. Thomson had found the first subatomic particle. We call it the electron.

NARRATOR: There were other clues in the ring, and it is worth being honest about them, because good science means knowing which evidence was real. Cathode rays cast a sharp shadow. Put a solid object, a little Maltese cross, in the beam, and a crisp black cross appears on the glowing glass, as if something is travelling in straight lines and being blocked. And there was a famous demonstration where a tiny paddle wheel placed in the beam actually turned, seeming to prove the rays carried momentum, and therefore mass, like a stream of pellets. That one felt like a knockout blow for the particle side. But here is the twist. Physicists later realised the paddle wheel was not being punched by particles at all. It was mostly a heat effect, the beam warming the gas and the vanes, a little thermodynamic engine, not a hail of bullets. The cathode rays do carry momentum, that part is true. But the spinning wheel was, for once, the wrong reason for the right answer.

NARRATOR: So Thomson's real case never rested on the paddle wheel or the shadow. It rested on the deflections, the clean proportional bending, the negative sign of the charge, and above all that impossible charge-to-mass ratio pointing to a particle far, far smaller than any atom. That is what won the argument. The British were right. The cathode ray was a stream of particles. And each particle was a piece of the atom itself.

NARRATOR: Step back and feel the size of this. For over two thousand years the atom had been, by definition, the thing you cannot cut. The word itself means uncuttable. And here was Thomson, in a Cambridge basement with a glass tube and two kinds of field, holding proof that the atom has parts. That there is a whole layer of reality underneath it. The solid, indivisible atom was gone, and the age of subatomic physics had begun, all from arguing about why a piece of glass glowed green.

NARRATOR: If you want to see where this goes next, follow the electron into the teaching episode on the structure of the atom, where Thomson's discovery forces a brand new question. If the atom has these light, negative pieces inside it, then what on Earth is holding the rest of it together?
