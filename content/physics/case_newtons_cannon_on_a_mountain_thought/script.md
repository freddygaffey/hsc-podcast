NARRATOR: This is part of Module 5, Advanced Mechanics. And I want to start with a question that sounds almost too simple to be interesting. Why does the Moon not fall down? Go outside tonight, find it hanging there, pale and heavy in the dark, and ask yourself honestly. Everything else falls. Drop an apple, it falls. Throw a ball, it falls. Fire a cannon and the ball arcs up, slows, and comes crashing back to the earth. Falling is the one thing the universe seems absolutely certain about. So why does the Moon, this enormous rock a quarter of a million miles away, just hang up there, going round and round, year after year, never once hitting the ground?

QUESTION: What holds it up?

NARRATOR: For most of human history, the answer was that nothing holds it up, because it does not need holding up. The Moon, and the planets, and the stars, belonged to a completely different world from ours. That was the deep, unquestioned belief, and it went all the way back to Aristotle. There were two realms. Down here, the earthly realm, where everything is corruptible and messy, where things fall and rot and decay, where the natural state of a thing is to come to rest on the ground. And up there, the heavenly realm, where everything is perfect and eternal, made of a fifth pure element, and where the natural motion is not falling but endless, effortless circles. Two sets of rules. One for the earth, one for the sky. And a hard wall between them that no one thought to climb over.

NARRATOR: Think about how reasonable that felt. Nobody had ever seen a rock fall from the ground up into the sky. Nobody had ever seen the Moon come tumbling down. The evidence of your own eyes told you these were separate kingdoms, governed by separate laws. The physics of the falling apple and the physics of the wheeling planets had nothing to do with each other. To suggest otherwise would have sounded, frankly, insane.

NARRATOR: And then, in sixteen eighty-seven, a difficult, secretive, brilliant man published a book that tore that wall down with a single mental picture. No new observatory. No expensive instrument. No expedition. Just an idea you could run entirely inside your own head. The man was Isaac Newton, and the book was the Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica, which everyone just calls the Principia. It is one of the most important books ever written, and near the very start of it, before the heavy mathematics begins, Newton does something disarmingly gentle. He asks you to imagine a cannon on top of a mountain.

QUESTION: A cannon? On a mountain?

NARRATOR: Stay with it, because this is the whole trick. Picture an impossibly tall mountain, so tall its peak pokes up above the atmosphere, so there is no air to slow anything down. On top, you place a cannon, and you point it dead level, straight out at the horizon. Now you fire.

NARRATOR: With a modest charge, the ball flies out, and it does exactly what every cannonball has ever done. It races forward, but at the same time it falls, curving down, and after a mile or two it buries itself in the ground. Nothing surprising. Newton himself puts it beautifully. He says a stone thrown from your hand is forced out of the straight line it would otherwise follow by the pressure of its own weight, and made to describe a curved path in the air, and is at last brought down to the ground. And, he adds, the greater the velocity with which it is projected, the farther it goes before it falls.

NARRATOR: That last line is the hinge on which the whole universe turns. The faster you throw it, the farther it goes before it lands. So Newton says, fine, let us just keep turning up the power. Load more charge. Fire again. Now the ball travels five miles before it hits. More charge. Now ten. More still, and it is describing a great arc across the countryside, twenty miles, fifty, a hundred, before it finally comes down.

NARRATOR: And here is the part you have to feel rather than just hear. The Earth is not flat. It is a ball. So while your cannonball is falling toward the ground, the ground itself is curving away beneath it, dropping off, bending down and out of the way. The horizon keeps running from you. And Newton asks the question nobody had dared to ask. What if you fired the ball so fast, so incredibly fast, that as it fell toward the Earth, the surface of the Earth curved away by exactly the same amount?

QUESTION: What happens then?

NARRATOR: Then the ball never gets any closer to the ground. It is falling, genuinely falling, plunging toward the center of the Earth the entire time, its own weight dragging it down every single second. But the planet keeps bending away underneath it at precisely the rate it drops. And so it falls, and falls, and falls, and never lands. It sweeps all the way around the mountain and comes roaring in from behind you and hits you in the back of the head. Newton, in his own words, says we may suppose the velocity to be so increased that the ball would describe an arc of one, two, five, ten, a hundred, a thousand miles, until at last, exceeding the limits of the Earth, it should pass into space without ever touching it.

NARRATOR: That is an orbit. Newton has just built an orbit out of nothing but a cannonball and a curve. And now watch what he has quietly done to Aristotle's wall between the two worlds, because it is devastating. The cannonball in orbit is not doing anything mystical. It is not made of a special heavenly element. It is not obeying a separate law of the skies. It is doing the most ordinary, earthly thing imaginable. It is falling. The exact same falling as the apple. The exact same weight, the same downward pull, the same force. The only difference between a cannonball that lands in a field and a cannonball that circles the Earth forever is speed. That is it. That is the whole difference.

QUESTION: So what is the Moon actually doing up there?

NARRATOR: The Moon is falling. Right now, tonight, the Moon is falling toward the Earth, exactly the way the apple falls, pulled by exactly the same force. It is simply moving sideways so fast that the Earth curves away beneath it forever, and it never arrives. The Moon is Newton's cannonball. It is a stone that was thrown so hard it missed the ground and kept missing it for four and a half billion years. There is no second kingdom. There is no wall. The force that pulls the apple from the branch is the same force that holds the Moon in the sky and swings the planets around the Sun. One force. One set of laws. Reaching from the ground beneath your feet all the way out to the farthest star.

NARRATOR: This is what people mean when they say Newton united the heavens and the earth. Before him, the sky was another country. After him, it was the same country, running on the same rules, and those rules could be written down as equations. The very same book lays out his law of universal gravitation, the idea that every mass in the universe pulls on every other mass, and with it Newton could do something no one had ever done. He could take Kepler's laws, those precise patterns that Kepler had wrung out of decades of raw astronomical data, the way the planets sweep out equal areas in equal times, the exact relationship between a planet's distance and the length of its year, and he could derive them. Not observe them. Derive them, from a single force, from first principles, with mathematics. The patterns in the sky were no longer brute facts to be catalogued. They were consequences. They had a cause.

NARRATOR: And the astonishing thing, the thing I keep coming back to, is that the door to all of it was a thought experiment. Newton did not need to build the mountain. He did not need the cannon. He never fired a single shot. He simply saw, in his mind, with total clarity, that falling and orbiting are the same act performed at different speeds, and that one insight cracked the cosmos open. Every satellite over your head right now, every space station, every probe we have flung to the outer planets, is that cannonball. We just learned to fire it fast enough.

NARRATOR: So the next time you see the Moon hanging there, remember it is not hanging at all. It is falling, endlessly, toward you, and missing, endlessly, because it is moving sideways just fast enough. That is not magic. That is Module Five. If you want to see the numbers behind it, and work out exactly how fast the cannonball has to go to fall forever, head to the teaching episode on circular motion and gravitation, where we turn Newton's mountain into equations you can actually solve.
