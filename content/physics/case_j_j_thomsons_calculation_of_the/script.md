NARRATOR: This is part of Module 8, From the Universe to the Atom. And I want to start with a glowing tube in a dark laboratory, because in the last years of the eighteen hundreds, that glow was the most argued-about light in all of physics. Seal two metal plates into a glass tube. Suck out almost all the air. Then run a fierce voltage across it. And something streams out of the negative plate, invisible in itself, but you know it's there because when it slams into the far end of the glass, the glass shines. A soft, eerie green. Nobody had ever poured it into a bottle. Nobody could tell you what it was made of. They just called it a cathode ray.

QUESTION: So what is it? A ray of light, or a stream of something?

NARRATOR: That is the fight. And here is the twist that makes it a real Veritasium kind of puzzle. Ask a physicist in Germany, and many of them would tell you, with total confidence, that a cathode ray is a wave. A disturbance rippling through some invisible medium, a cousin of light itself. Ask a physicist in Britain, and you'd often hear the opposite: no, no, it's a hail of tiny charged bullets. Both camps were serious, brilliant people. Both were sure. And both could not be right. The whole argument, this transnational shouting match about a green glow, was about to be settled by one careful man measuring one number. And that number would crack the atom open.

NARRATOR: To feel why this mattered, you have to understand what an atom was supposed to be in eighteen ninety-seven. The word itself comes from the Greek for uncuttable. For nearly a century, chemistry had run on John Dalton's picture: the atom is the smallest possible piece of matter, a hard little sphere, indivisible, the fundamental brick of the universe. Hydrogen atoms, oxygen atoms, gold atoms, each a solid nugget you could never break, never split, never look inside. Matter was made of these, full stop. There was no inside. Asking what an atom was made of was like asking what a point was made of. It was, by definition, the end of the line.

NARRATOR: So the stakes here are quiet but enormous. If cathode rays are just waves, the atom stays whole and Dalton sleeps soundly. But if they're particles, and if you could show they came out of atoms, then the uncuttable had been cut. Enter Joseph John Thomson, known to everyone as J.J., director of the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge. He was, by the accounts of the day, a hopeless experimenter with his own hands, so clumsy that his assistants preferred he stay away from the delicate glassware. But his mind for an experiment was ruthless. And he decided he would not argue about cathode rays. He would weigh them.

QUESTION: But how do you weigh something you can't even see, that flies through a vacuum at a fraction of the speed of light?

NARRATOR: You don't, not directly. You do something cleverer. You measure how hard it is to push around. Think of it this way. If I shove two objects with exactly the same force and one barely moves while the other flies off, I've learned about their masses without ever putting them on a scale. Thomson's plan was to shove his mysterious rays with forces he controlled precisely, forces from electricity and magnetism, and watch how much they swerved. The amount of swerve would betray a hidden ratio: the charge of each particle divided by its mass. Physicists write it as charge over mass, q over m. Get that ratio, and the wave-versus-particle war is over.

NARRATOR: Now, the tool at the heart of this is one of the most beautiful ideas in the whole module, so let's build it carefully. There's a rule that governs how a charged particle feels electric and magnetic fields at once. It's called the Lorentz force, and it has two pieces. The first piece is the electric part: the force is the charge times the electric field, and it pushes the particle straight along the direction of that electric field. Simple. A charge in an electric field gets shoved one way. The second piece is the magnetic part, and this is the strange one. The magnetic force doesn't push along the magnetic field. It pushes sideways, at right angles both to the field and to the particle's own motion. It's a force that always acts perpendicular to where the thing is heading.

NARRATOR: Hold on to that difference, because Thomson is about to exploit it in the neatest way. Picture the cathode ray streaming left to right down the tube. Now Thomson sets up an electric field pointing, say, straight down. That electric force pushes the charged particle straight down too. Fine. Then he adds a magnetic field, and he arranges it so that the sideways magnetic kick points straight up, directly opposing the electric shove. So now the particle is being pressed down by electricity and up by magnetism, both along the very same vertical line, fighting each other, while it races forward.

QUESTION: And what happens when those two forces are perfectly balanced?

NARRATOR: Nothing happens. That's the whole trick. The particle sails dead straight, no deflection at all, the green spot on the glass sitting exactly where it would with no fields at all. Thomson would switch everything on and then patiently tune the strength of his fields until that spot stopped moving. And when it sat perfectly still, he knew something exact. He knew the downward electric force and the upward magnetic force had cancelled to zero. The total force was nothing. And a beam with no net force on it doesn't accelerate. It just cruises.

NARRATOR: Watch what that gives him. When the forces balance, the electric force equals the magnetic force. The electric force is the charge times the electric field. The magnetic force is the charge times the velocity times the magnetic field. Set them equal, and look, the charge appears on both sides. It cancels clean away. What's left is astonishingly simple: the velocity of the particle equals the electric field strength divided by the magnetic field strength. And Thomson knows both of those. He built the fields. He can read their values straight off his apparatus. So for the first time, he knows exactly how fast these invisible things are travelling. He has clocked a ghost.

NARRATOR: This device even has a name that survives to this day. It's called a velocity selector, because only particles at exactly that speed, electric field over magnetic field, glide through undeflected. Send in something faster or slower and the balance breaks and it curves away. It's a gate that only lets one speed pass.

NARRATOR: But knowing the speed is only half the job. Speed alone won't give you the charge-to-mass ratio. So now comes the second move, and it's elegantly economical. Thomson simply switches off the electric field and leaves the magnetic field on. Suddenly the tug-of-war is over and only the magnetic force remains. And remember what magnetism does: it always pushes at right angles to the motion. A force that's forever perpendicular to your velocity doesn't speed you up or slow you down. It just bends your path, endlessly, into a circle. The beam curves. It arcs.

QUESTION: So a sideways-only force turns a straight beam into a circle. How does that hand you the mass?

NARRATOR: Because uniform circular motion has its own iron law. To hold anything in a circle you need a centre-seeking force, a centripetal force, and its size is fixed: the mass times the velocity squared, divided by the radius of the circle. Here, the thing bending the beam is the magnetic force, which is the charge times the velocity times the magnetic field. So Thomson sets those two equal. The magnetic force provides exactly the centripetal force the circle demands. And when you tidy up that equation, a velocity cancels from each side, and you're left with a jewel: the charge over the mass equals the velocity, divided by the magnetic field times the radius.

NARRATOR: Every single thing on the right is measurable. He already knows the velocity from the balancing trick, electric field over magnetic field. He knows the magnetic field, because he made it. And the radius of the curve he can get from how far the glowing spot shifts on the end of the tube. Three measurable quantities. One hidden ratio falls straight out. He didn't need to know the charge on its own. He didn't need to know the mass on its own. The clever design made both of those cancel out along the way and left him with just the thing he was hunting: charge over mass.

NARRATOR: So he does the sums. And the number that comes out is roughly one point seven six times ten to the eleven. In full units, one point seven six times ten to the eleven coulombs per kilogram. Now, a raw number like that means nothing until you have something to compare it to. So compare it. The best-known charged particle of the era was the hydrogen ion, the lightest atom stripped of its charge, and chemists already had a rough sense of its charge-to-mass ratio from electrolysis. Thomson's cathode-ray number wasn't a little bigger than the hydrogen ion's. It was something like two thousand times bigger. His particles carried a charge-to-mass ratio thousands of times larger than anything anyone had ever measured for matter.

QUESTION: So which is it? A monstrous charge, or a ridiculously tiny mass?

NARRATOR: That is exactly the fork in the road, and this is where Thomson's judgement shines, because the equation alone can't tell you. A huge charge-to-mass ratio could mean the charge is enormous, or it could mean the mass is minuscule. Both would fit the number. So Thomson reasons his way past it. If these particles carried some gigantic charge, then a stream of them, which is just an electric current, would fling a powerful magnetic field around itself. A current makes magnetism, and more charge streaming past means more magnetism. You would see it wrenching a nearby compass needle. But the cathode ray barely nudges a compass at all. The magnetic effect was feeble. So the charge could not be monstrous. Which leaves only one door open. Not a huge charge. A ridiculously, almost unbelievably small mass.

NARRATOR: Let that land. Whatever these particles were, they weighed something like one two-thousandth of the lightest atom known. A thousand times lighter than hydrogen, the smallest thing the whole edifice of chemistry insisted was the smallest thing. And Thomson found one more detail that turned the screw all the way. He tried different metals for his cathode. Aluminium. Iron. Platinum. Different gases in the tube. And no matter what he made the tube out of, the cathode rays came out with the very same charge-to-mass ratio. The same tiny particle, pouring out of every material he tried. It wasn't a quirk of one metal. It was universal. Something common to all matter.

NARRATOR: Now put those two facts together and feel the ground move. There is a particle far, far lighter than any atom, and it can be knocked out of every kind of atom you care to try. Which means it was inside them all along. Which means the atom is not the end of the line. The uncuttable had just been cut. Dalton's hard little indivisible sphere, the bedrock of chemistry for a hundred years, was simply wrong. There was stuff inside the atom, and Thomson was holding a piece of it.

NARRATOR: He'd found the first subatomic particle. In time it would take the name we still use, borrowed and reshaped from an older word for the fundamental unit of charge: the electron. And Thomson, having pulled these light negative specks out of atoms, faced an obvious next question. If atoms are neutral overall, but they contain these little negative bits, then something in there must be positive to balance the books. His answer was a model, a first rough sketch of the atom's insides: a ball of positive charge with the tiny negative electrons studded through it, like fruit in a pudding. History remembers it, a little unkindly, as the plum pudding model. It wasn't right either, as it turned out. But that's not the point. The point is that for the first time, anyone was drawing the inside of an atom at all. The question had gone from forbidden to open.

NARRATOR: And it all turned on that one measurement. Not a lucky glimpse, not a philosophical argument, but a chain of clean reasoning: balance an electric push against a magnetic one to clock the speed, then let magnetism alone bend the beam into a circle to weigh the charge against the mass. Two forces, one ratio, and a hundred-year-old certainty overturned. The green glow in the tube had been trying to tell everyone the same thing all along. There is something smaller than the atom, and everything is made of it.
