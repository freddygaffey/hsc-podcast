NARRATOR: This is part of Module Seven, The Nature of Light. And I want to start with a question that sounds almost too simple to ask. When you flip a light switch, and the room fills with light, how long does that take? Not the bulb warming up. The light itself, crossing the room to your eyes. Is it a fraction of a second? A millionth of a second? Or is it, as nearly every great thinker for two thousand years insisted, no time at all?

QUESTION: Could light really move so fast that it takes no time whatsoever?

NARRATOR: That was the serious, mainstream view. Aristotle believed it. Kepler believed it. Descartes, one of the sharpest minds of the age, argued flatly that light was instantaneous, and he staked his whole philosophy on it. Their reasoning felt airtight. Look at a distant mountain, then open your eyes toward a far-off star. There is no waiting, no lag, no delay you could ever catch. Light just arrives. And if you cannot measure a delay, the natural conclusion is that there is no delay to measure. The speed of light, they said, was infinite.

NARRATOR: Galileo actually tried to test it. The story goes that he stood on one hilltop with a lantern, and sent an assistant to another hilltop with a second lantern, miles away. He would uncover his lantern, and the moment the assistant saw the flash, the assistant would uncover his. Galileo timed how long the return flash took. But of course, all he was ever really measuring was human reaction time. Light crossed those few miles so fast that the delay was utterly swamped. He came away unable to prove anything, except that if light did have a finite speed, it was extraordinarily, hopelessly fast. Too fast for any lantern on any hill.

NARRATOR: So here is the trap. To catch light in the act of travelling, to actually see the delay, you do not need a faster stopwatch. You need a longer racetrack. And there was no racetrack on Earth long enough. The answer, it turned out, was not on Earth at all. It was hanging in the night sky, in the clockwork of Jupiter and its moons.

QUESTION: What does Jupiter have to do with the speed of light?

NARRATOR: To understand that, meet a young Danish astronomer named Ole Rømer, working at the Royal Observatory in Paris in the sixteen seventies. His job was mundane and precise. Jupiter has a moon called Io, the innermost of its bright moons, and Io whips around the giant planet in a tight, fast orbit, once every roughly forty-two hours. On each lap, Io swings behind Jupiter and vanishes into the planet's shadow. It gets eclipsed. From Earth, through a telescope, you see the little point of light blink out, and then, later, blink back into view.

NARRATOR: Now this is the beautiful part. That blinking is a clock. A natural, celestial clock, ticking out there in the dark. And thanks to Kepler's laws of planetary motion, that clock should be almost perfectly regular. Io's true orbital period does not change. Astronomers of the day were compiling tables of these eclipses, partly for a very practical reason. If you knew exactly when an Io eclipse was due, and you observed it from a ship or a distant colony, you could compare the times and work out your longitude. A moon of Jupiter as a clock in the sky, to help sailors find their way home.

NARRATOR: But the clock would not behave. When Rømer and his colleagues checked the eclipses against their predictions, the timings drifted. Sometimes Io emerged from Jupiter's shadow right on schedule. And sometimes it emerged minutes late. The eclipses were running behind, and then, months later, running ahead again. The clock in the sky seemed to be speeding up and slowing down.

QUESTION: Why would a moon locked in orbit around Jupiter keep bad time?

NARRATOR: This is where most people would have blamed the moon. Maybe Io's orbit was wobbling. Maybe Kepler was wrong. But Rømer noticed something that reframed the entire puzzle. The drift was not random. It tracked, precisely, where the Earth was in its own orbit around the Sun. When the Earth was moving away from Jupiter, the measured time between eclipses stretched out. Each period ran a little long. And when the Earth was swinging back toward Jupiter, the periods ran short. The eclipses came early. The behaviour of a moon half a billion kilometres away was somehow synchronised with our own planet's motion.

NARRATOR: Let me put the logic the way Rømer did, because it is gorgeous, and it needs no advanced mathematics at all. Think about what it actually means to observe an eclipse. You do not see it when it happens. You see it when the light from it reaches your eye. So the time you record is the true time of the event, plus however long the light took to travel to you. Every observation carries a hidden delay, the travel time of the light.

NARRATOR: Now, if that delay were exactly the same for every eclipse, it would cancel out. You would measure the true period perfectly, just shifted a bit later. The delay only matters if it changes between one eclipse and the next. And what changes it? The distance. If the Earth is moving away from Jupiter, then each successive eclipse happens with the Earth a little farther away, so its light has a little farther to travel, so each one arrives a little more delayed than the last. The gaps stretch. And if the Earth is moving toward Jupiter, each eclipse's light has a shorter trip than the one before, so they bunch up, and arrive early.

NARRATOR: Sit with what that requires. For the delay to change at all, light must take time to cross the extra distance. If light were instantaneous, if the old philosophers were right, then the delay would always be zero, no matter where the Earth sat in its orbit. The eclipses would never drift. But they did drift. And they drifted in perfect step with the Earth's distance from Jupiter. There was only one honest conclusion. Light does not arrive instantly. Light has a finite speed.

NARRATOR: That, on its own, is the revolution. Two thousand years of assumption, overturned by a moon that would not keep time. But Rømer could push it further, into an actual number. Here is the elegant trick. Instead of trying to measure one tiny delay across a single forty-two hour orbit, which would be maddeningly small, he added them all up. He took the whole stretch of about six months, while the Earth travelled from the near side of its orbit to the far side, moving steadily away from Jupiter. Over all those dozens of eclipses, every little bit of extra delay accumulated.

NARRATOR: And the sum of all those delays came out to about twenty-two minutes. That was the headline figure. Twenty-two minutes. Think about what that number is. Over those six months, the Earth had moved from one side of its orbit to the other, so the light now had to cross the entire extra width of the Earth's orbit around the Sun. Twenty-two minutes was, in Rømer's reasoning, roughly the time it takes light to cross the diameter of the Earth's orbit.

QUESTION: So how do you turn twenty-two minutes into a speed?

NARRATOR: The same way you turn any time into a speed, once you know a distance. Speed is distance divided by time. Rømer himself, cautiously, never published a hard number for the speed. But his friend, the great Dutch physicist Christiaan Huygens, took the final step. Huygens had an estimate for the size of the Earth's orbit. He reckoned the Earth's diameter at about twelve thousand seven hundred and fifty kilometres, and figured the width of the Earth's orbit was something like twenty-four thousand times that. Divide that enormous distance by the twenty-two minutes of accumulated delay, and out drops a speed of light. Huygens, knowing this was only a rough ballpark, rounded it off to a clean thousand Earth-diameters every minute, and got a value of around two hundred and twelve thousand kilometres per second. In the same ballpark as the roughly two hundred and twenty thousand kilometres per second that this experiment is remembered for.

NARRATOR: Now, that is not the modern value. The true speed of light is close to three hundred thousand kilometres per second, so Rømer's number was low, off by roughly a third. But pause on why. The physics was flawless. The method was sound. The error was in the inputs, the rough seventeenth century guesses at the size of the Earth's orbit, and a slightly generous figure for that delay. In fact, years later, in sixteen ninety-four, Edmond Halley reexamined the data and argued the delay should be closer to seventeen minutes than twenty-two. Feed in seventeen minutes, and the speed jumps up to about three hundred thousand kilometres per second. Almost exactly right. The instrument that was wrong was the ruler, never the idea.

NARRATOR: And that is the payoff. Ole Rømer did not just measure a number. He settled an argument that had run since antiquity. Light is not instantaneous. It travels, it takes time, and by watching a moon blink behind Jupiter, humanity had, for the very first time, caught light in the act of moving. From that moment, the speed of light stops being a philosophical opinion and becomes a physical quantity, one you can measure, and refine, and eventually build whole theories of relativity upon. All from noticing that a clock in the sky ran slow whenever we drifted away from it.
