NARRATOR: This is part of Module Seven, The Nature of Light. And I want to start with a claim that sounds like nonsense. If you get on a fast enough spaceship and fly away from me, then from where I'm standing, your watch will tick slower than mine. Not because it's broken. Not because the springs inside are cold or the battery is dying. Time itself, for you, will run slow. And here's the part that really breaks people's brains: you won't feel a thing. To you, everything is normal.

QUESTION: So who's right, you or me?

NARRATOR: That's the trap. And the answer, the one almost everyone gets wrong the first time, is that we're both right. There is no master clock in the universe that settles the argument. But before we can see why, we have to go back to a young man working in a patent office in Bern, Switzerland, in the year nineteen oh five.

NARRATOR: His name was Albert Einstein, and he was not a professor. He'd been turned down for academic jobs. He examined patents by day and did physics in the evenings, chewing on a problem that had quietly been eating away at the foundations of physics for decades.

NARRATOR: For two hundred years, everyone had agreed on a comfortable picture of the world. Time was a great river that flowed at the same rate everywhere, for everyone, forever. If a second passed for me, a second passed for you, no matter where you were or how fast you were moving. Isaac Newton had built all of physics on top of that assumption, and it worked beautifully. It got us the tides, the planets, the moons of Jupiter.

NARRATOR: But there was a crack. When physicists studied light, and the equations that describe electricity and magnetism, they found something strange. Those equations predicted that light always travels at one exact speed. Roughly three hundred million metres every second. And the equations didn't say relative to what. They just gave one number, full stop.

QUESTION: Why is that a problem?

NARRATOR: Because nothing else in the world behaves that way. Imagine you're on a train doing a hundred kilometres an hour, and you throw a ball forward at ten. To someone standing on the platform, that ball is doing a hundred and ten. Speeds add up. That's just common sense. So if you're racing alongside a beam of light, chasing it, you should measure it going a little slower, the same way a car next to you on the motorway seems to crawl. Everyone expected that. Einstein asked a heretical question. What if light refuses to play along? What if no matter how fast you chase it, light always screams past you at exactly the same speed?

NARRATOR: That was his postulate. His starting assumption. The speed of light is the same for every observer moving at a steady speed, no matter how they're moving. And Einstein said, let's just take that seriously and follow it wherever it goes, even if the destination is insane.

NARRATOR: So let's follow it. And to do that, physicists use a wonderfully simple imaginary device. We call it a photon clock, or a light clock. Picture a light source at the bottom, and a mirror a distance above it. A single flash of light leaves the source, races straight up to the mirror, bounces, and comes straight back down to a detector. Up and back. Tick. That round trip is one tick of our clock. The longer the round trip takes, the slower the clock is running.

NARRATOR: Now put that clock on a spaceship, with an astronaut, and fly the whole thing past the Earth at a constant speed. And we watch the same clock two ways. First, through the astronaut's eyes. Then, through the eyes of an observer standing on Earth.

NARRATOR: For the astronaut, life is boring. The clock is sitting right there next to her. The light just goes straight up to the mirror and straight back down. A nice clean vertical line. She measures the time for that round trip, and we'll call her time the astronaut's time. As far as she can tell, nothing unusual is happening at all.

QUESTION: But what does the observer on Earth see?

NARRATOR: Ah. Here's where it gets beautiful. From the Earth, the whole spaceship is sliding sideways while the light is in flight. So during the time it takes the flash to go up, the mirror has moved forward. By the time it comes back down, the detector has moved forward again. So from the Earth, that light does not travel in a straight vertical line. It travels in a long diagonal. Up and over, then down and over. A stretched-out letter V, wider than the astronaut's simple up-and-down path.

NARRATOR: And now, listen closely, because this is the whole trick. That diagonal path is longer than the straight-up path. Simple geometry, longer distance. Under the old Newtonian rules, that would be fine, because you'd just say the light goes a bit faster to cover the extra ground. But Einstein's postulate forbids that. The speed of light is locked. It cannot go faster for the Earth observer. It is exactly the same speed for both of them.

QUESTION: So if the speed is the same but the Earth observer's light has further to travel, then what has to give?

NARRATOR: Time. That's the only thing left. If distance goes up and speed stays fixed, then the time must go up too. The Earth observer measures a longer time for that one tick than the astronaut does. The moving clock, from the ground, is running slow.

NARRATOR: And you can pin this down exactly, with nothing more than Pythagoras's theorem, the thing you learned about triangles at school. The Earth sees a triangle. One side is the straight-up distance the light would travel. Another side is how far the ship slid sideways. And the long diagonal, the hypotenuse, is the actual path the light took. Square the sides, add them, take the root, and out drops one of the most famous relationships in all of physics.

NARRATOR: The time measured on Earth equals the astronaut's time, divided by the square root of one minus the speed squared over the speed of light squared. That denominator is always a little less than one, so the Earth's time always comes out bigger. The faster the ship, the bigger the stretch. Physicists gave that stretching factor its own name, the Lorentz factor, written as the Greek letter gamma, after Hendrik Lorentz. And the whole result collapses to a single, clean line: the time on Earth equals gamma times the astronaut's time.

NARRATOR: Take a breath and feel what just happened. We did not build a machine or split an atom. We took one stubborn fact about light, drew a triangle, and out fell the conclusion that moving clocks run slow. Time is not the fixed universal river Newton promised. It bends. It depends on how you move.

QUESTION: But is this real, or just a clever story with triangles?

NARRATOR: It's real. Fast-moving particles born in the upper atmosphere should decay and vanish long before they reach the ground, and yet they arrive in their millions, because at their speed their internal clocks are running slow, exactly by this factor. And the satellites that run the maps on your phone carry atomic clocks that tick at a different rate up in orbit. If engineers didn't correct for it, using this very effect, your navigation would drift off by kilometres within a day. This little thought experiment with a mirror is quietly keeping you from getting lost.

NARRATOR: And it all came from a patent clerk who refused to look away from one uncomfortable fact, and asked what would happen if he simply believed it.

NARRATOR: To see where the equation goes next, and how a moving object shrinks in the direction it's travelling, head to the teaching topic on special relativity, time dilation, and length contraction.
