NARRATOR: This is part of Module 7, The Nature of Light. And I want to start with a claim that sounds like nonsense. There is a particle raining down on you, right now, that should not be here. By every ordinary rule of motion, it should have died in the thin air miles above your head, long before it ever reached the ground. And yet detectors at sea level catch it, again and again, hour after hour. Something is letting it cheat death. The question is what.

NARRATOR: To feel why this is strange, you have to go up. Up past the clouds, past the cruising height of airliners, to the very top of the atmosphere, where the air thins into almost nothing and the sky above turns from blue to black. Out there, streaming in from exploding stars and the violent hearts of distant galaxies, is a constant sleet of what we call cosmic rays. Over ninety-nine percent of them are just bare protons and alpha particles, atomic nuclei stripped naked, moving at almost the speed of light.

NARRATOR: And when one of those protons slams into the nucleus of an oxygen or nitrogen atom at the top of the sky, it hits so hard that it does something almost magical. The collision pumps so much energy into the quarks inside that nucleus that the energy itself congeals into new matter, a fresh quark and antiquark torn out of pure motion. Out of that violence come new particles called pions.

QUESTION: So what happens to these pions?

NARRATOR: Most of them don't last. The neutral pions are gone almost before they exist, with a half life of about eighty-five attoseconds. That is eight point five times ten to the minus seventeen seconds, a length of time so short that light, the fastest thing there is, crosses barely the width of an atom before the pion is gone, flashing into gamma rays. But the charged pions do something far more interesting. A charged pion decays into a muon and a tiny ghost of a particle called a neutrino, driven by the weak nuclear force.

NARRATOR: And here is where the physics gets beautiful. A charged pion has a mass of about one hundred and thirty-nine point six mega electron volts over c squared. The muon and the neutrino it turns into together weigh only about one hundred and five point seven. Mass has vanished. And by the conservation of energy, that missing mass cannot simply disappear. It reappears as motion. The muon is flung out with an enormous helping of kinetic energy. And that is before you even count the speed the pion already had. The result is a muon travelling at a staggering pace, at ninety-nine point five percent of the speed of light, some even faster.

NARRATOR: Physicists have a shorthand for this. They write beta for the fraction of light speed. So these muons are born at a beta of about nought point nine nine five, screaming down toward the Earth almost as fast as light itself.

QUESTION: So why should a fast muon be doomed?

NARRATOR: Because a muon is a ticking clock, and the clock is short. A muon is a second-generation cousin of the electron, and like most heavy particles, it is unstable. Left to itself, it decays, throwing off an electron and a couple more neutrinos. And it does this on a remarkably reliable schedule. The average muon lives, at rest, for about two point two microseconds. Two point two millionths of a second. That is its lifetime, measured over and over in the lab, always the same.

NARRATOR: So think about what that means. Here is a particle that behaves like a tiny, precise stopwatch, always ticking down the same interval before it dies. And it is moving at almost the speed of light. That combination, a perfect clock strapped to something moving that fast, is exactly what you would design if you wanted to test the strangest idea in all of physics.

NARRATOR: Because Albert Einstein had made a prediction that most people, deep down, simply refused to believe. He said that time is not universal. That a clock in motion runs slow, not because of some mechanical fault, but because time itself stretches for anything that moves. The faster you go, the slower your clock ticks compared to someone standing still. To a physicist in the nineteen fifties this was not folklore, it was mathematics, but mathematics is one thing and a real object you can catch in a detector is another. Someone had to catch the universe in the act.

NARRATOR: In nineteen sixty-two, two physicists, David Frisch and James Smith, decided that muons would be their witness. And they chose a wonderfully simple stage for the experiment. A mountain. Mount Washington, in New Hampshire, one thousand nine hundred and seven metres above the sea.

NARRATOR: Here is the plan, and it is elegant enough to take your breath away. Put a detector at the top of the mountain and count how many muons decay in an hour. Then carry the same detector down to sea level and count again. In between those two heights, the muons have to make a journey through the air, and while they travel, their little clocks are ticking, and some of them will die on the way. So the number that survive to the bottom tells you exactly how much time the muons think has passed. The mountain becomes a stopwatch nearly two kilometres tall.

NARRATOR: Up at the summit, Frisch and Smith measured the energy of the incoming muons and pinned down their speed, that beta of around nought point nine nine five. And they counted. At the top of Mount Washington they saw about five hundred and sixty-three muon decays every hour.

QUESTION: So how many should survive the trip down?

NARRATOR: Let's do the ordinary, common-sense calculation first, the one where Einstein is wrong and time is just time. The drop from the summit to their sea level station is about one thousand nine hundred and four metres. The muons are moving at essentially the speed of light, so the time for that fall is just the distance divided by that speed. Work it out and you get about six point three four microseconds.

NARRATOR: Now hold that number against the muon's lifetime. The muon lives, on average, only two point two microseconds. And we are asking it to survive a journey lasting six point three four microseconds. That is nearly three lifetimes. It is like asking how many people in a room live to two hundred and ten years old when the average lifespan is seventy. The radioactive decay law is merciless about this. Start with five hundred and sixty-three muons per hour, let almost three lifetimes pass, and the number that should still be alive at the bottom collapses to about thirty-one.

NARRATOR: Thirty-one decays an hour. That is the honest prediction of a universe where time is absolute. Five hundred and sixty-three at the top, a miserable thirty-one at the bottom. Almost all of them should be gone.

NARRATOR: So Frisch and Smith took their detector down the mountain to sea level, and they counted. And they did not find thirty-one. They found about four hundred and nine muon decays every hour.

NARRATOR: Sit with that number. Four hundred and nine, where plain common sense screamed thirty-one. More than ten times as many muons survived the trip as had any right to. It is as though you followed a crowd of mayflies, insects that live a single day, on a journey that should take three days, and found that most of them arrived alive and well at the other end. The muons were not ageing the way they should. Their clocks had slowed down.

QUESTION: So does the number match Einstein?

NARRATOR: This is the moment of truth, and it comes down to a single Greek letter. Einstein's theory says the amount by which a moving clock slows is set by a factor called gamma, the Lorentz factor. For a muon moving at that summit speed, gamma comes out to about ten point two. In other words, for every second the muon experiences, more than ten seconds pass for us watching from the ground.

NARRATOR: But there is a subtlety Frisch and Smith were careful about, and it is why this experiment was so good. As the muons plough down through thickening air, they collide with atoms and lose energy, so they slow down a little on the way. So the team measured the muon speed again, down at sea level, and found it had dropped to a beta of around nought point nine eight eight, giving a gamma of about six point eight at the bottom. Averaging the fast top and the slower bottom, the muons should have felt a Lorentz factor of roughly eight point four across the whole journey.

NARRATOR: So now the test is clean. Take the number that actually survived, four hundred and nine out of five hundred and sixty-three, feed it back through the decay law, and ask, what value of gamma does the universe need for this to happen? What stretching of time explains why so many lived? Turn the mathematics around, and out drops a number. Gamma equals about eight point nine.

NARRATOR: Eight point nine, measured. Eight point four, predicted by Einstein. Those two numbers, arrived at from completely different directions, one from counting dying particles and one from a theory about the nature of time, land almost on top of each other. And in the actual paper, Frisch and Smith argued their true agreement was even tighter than that. Time dilation was not a mathematical curiosity. It was sitting there in the count, real and measurable, written in the survival of particles falling off a mountain.

QUESTION: But wait, what does the muon think is happening?

NARRATOR: And this is the part that turns a good experiment into a profound one. Because time dilation is our story, told from the ground, in the lab frame. But the muon would tell the story completely differently, and it cannot be wrong either. From the muon's point of view, it is not moving at all. The Earth is rushing up to meet it. And in the muon's own frame, its clock runs perfectly normally. It only ever feels its two point two microseconds. So from where it sits, it does not have nearly enough time to fall the full one thousand nine hundred metres. It should still die in mid-air.

NARRATOR: So how does it reach the ground? Because for the muon, the mountain is not one thousand nine hundred metres tall. Einstein's relativity says that when time stretches for one observer, distance contracts for the other, by exactly the same factor. The muon sees the height of Mount Washington squashed, shrunk by that Lorentz factor. The journey it has to make is far shorter than the one we see, short enough that it can cross it comfortably in the little life it has. When you write the mathematics down, the length contraction the muon experiences and the time dilation we measure are the very same equation, seen from two sides.

NARRATOR: Both stories are true. From the ground, the muon survives because its clock runs slow. From the muon, it survives because the mountain is short. Neither observer is wrong, neither is privileged, and both agree on the one thing you can actually check, the number of muons that make it to the bottom. That is the deep symmetry at the heart of special relativity. Space and time are not the fixed, separate stage we always assumed. They bend into each other, trading places depending on who is watching.

NARRATOR: And it all falls out of a simple thing you can hold in your mind. A clock that ticks the same everywhere. A mountain. A count of particles at the top and a count at the bottom. Numbers that should not agree, and the only way to make them agree is to accept that a moving clock runs slow and a moving ruler shrinks. The muons falling through the air above you right now are quietly proving Einstein right, over and over, every single hour.
