NARRATOR: This is part of Module 8, From the Universe to the Atom. And I want to start with a strange fact. Right now, as you listen to this, invisible bullets are passing through your body. Tiny fragments of shattered atoms are streaking through the air around you, through the walls, through your skull, through your beating heart, at nearly the speed of light. Hundreds of them every minute. You cannot feel them. You cannot see them. For most of human history, nobody even suspected they existed.

QUESTION: So how do you photograph a ghost?

NARRATOR: That is the puzzle a young Scottish physicist set himself more than a century ago. And the answer he found did not just make the invisible visible. It handed physicists a window into a world they had only been able to imagine, and through that window they would discover particles nobody knew were real.

NARRATOR: The man was Charles Thomson Rees Wilson. And the strange thing is, he did not set out to hunt subatomic particles at all. He set out to make clouds.

NARRATOR: Wilson was a mountain man at heart. He grew up in Scotland, and in the eighteen nineties he spent a few weeks working at the observatory on the summit of Ben Nevis, the highest peak in Britain. Up there, in the thin cold air, he watched sunlight break around the mountaintop into rings of colour, and he saw his own shadow cast onto the clouds below, ringed by a halo of light. He was captivated. He wanted to recreate that beauty in the laboratory. He wanted to build clouds in a jar.

NARRATOR: Now, to understand what he did next, you need to understand how a cloud actually forms. When warm, moist air rises and cools, the water vapour in it wants to condense into liquid droplets. But here is the catch. Water molecules cannot just condense out of thin air. They need something to grab onto, a tiny seed, a speck of dust, a grain of salt. We call these seeds condensation nuclei. Every raindrop in every storm you have ever seen began its life clinging to one.

NARRATOR: So Wilson built himself a sealed glass chamber filled with air and saturated with water vapour. And he could cool that air suddenly by letting it expand, by yanking a piston so the gas inside puffed outward and its temperature dropped. Cool it fast enough, and the vapour becomes supersaturated. It is holding more moisture than it has any right to. It is desperate to condense. It is a loaded gun waiting for a trigger.

NARRATOR: Wilson expected that without dust in his clean chamber, nothing would happen. And at first, that is exactly what he saw. He filtered out every speck of dust, expanded the air, and the vapour stayed clear. No dust, no droplets. Fine.

QUESTION: But then he expanded it harder. What happened?

NARRATOR: When he cooled the air more aggressively, a thin wisp of cloud appeared anyway. In dust-free air. With nothing, apparently, for the water to cling to. Something invisible was seeding those droplets. Something was in there.

NARRATOR: This was the eighteen nineties, and the timing could not have been more perfect. Because at almost exactly that moment, physics was being turned upside down. Wilhelm Roentgen had just discovered X-rays. Henri Becquerel had just stumbled onto radioactivity, uranium salts fogging a photographic plate in a locked drawer. And a whole new picture of matter was emerging, one where atoms were not solid, indivisible balls, but were themselves breaking apart and firing out invisible radiation.

NARRATOR: Wilson had a hunch. What if those invisible rays were the seeds? What if radiation, passing through his chamber, was leaving behind something for the water to grab? So he brought a source of X-rays up to his glass chamber, expanded the air, and the inside filled with a dense fog. The radiation was seeding clouds. He had his answer, and it was the beginning of everything.

NARRATOR: Here is the physics, and it is beautiful. When an energetic charged particle, an alpha particle, say, or a fast electron, comes tearing through the chamber, it does not pass cleanly. It slams into the molecules of the vapour along its route, and every collision knocks electrons loose. It leaves behind a trail of electrically charged fragments, a wake of positive ions marking exactly where it has been.

NARRATOR: And this is the key. Wilson used a vapour whose molecules are polar. Water works, alcohol works even better, because these molecules have a positive end and a negative end, like tiny magnets for electric charge. So when the supersaturated vapour meets that trail of ions, it is pulled straight to it. The molecules crowd around each charged fragment and condense. Droplets bloom along the exact path the particle took. And suddenly, hanging in the air in front of you, glinting in the light, is a thread of mist tracing the flight of a single subatomic particle.

QUESTION: A particle far too small to ever see, drawing its own picture. Do you understand how astonishing that is?

NARRATOR: For the first time in history, a human being could watch the track of an individual piece of a broken atom. Not infer it. Not calculate it. See it, with the naked eye, like a jet's contrail scratched across a clear sky. Wilson had turned the invisible visible.

NARRATOR: And the tracks were not all the same. That was the genius of it. Different particles wrote in different handwriting, and once you learned to read it, the chamber told you what had flown through.

NARRATOR: Alpha particles left thick, bold, prominent lines. An alpha particle is heavy and doubly charged, the strongest ioniser of them all, so it rips electrons loose ferociously and leaves a dense, dramatic trail. And because it is so massive, it barrels through in a nearly straight line. The little collisions along the way barely deflect it, because acceleration is force divided by mass, and with a large mass the deflection is tiny. But that same ferocity burns through its energy fast, so alpha tracks are short. Thick, straight, and stubby.

NARRATOR: Beta particles, which are just fast electrons, wrote something completely different. Their tracks are thin and faint, because an electron is tiny and ionises far less as it goes. And their paths are wonky, jagged, wandering, because an electron is so light that every collision knocks it off course. Same law, force over mass, but now the mass is minuscule, so the deflections are wild. Long, thin, and crooked. You could tell an alpha from a beta at a glance.

QUESTION: And gamma rays, the most penetrating radiation of all?

NARRATOR: Gamma rays are sneaky. Gamma radiation itself leaves no track, because it carries no charge, so it ionises nothing directly as it passes. But every so often a gamma ray strikes an electron and knocks it free, or, more rarely, vanishes entirely and conjures a particle and antiparticle pair out of pure energy. So gamma radiation reveals itself indirectly, as a scattering of short, faint electron tracks appearing seemingly out of nowhere. A ghost that only shows up in the footprints it leaves in others.

NARRATOR: In nineteen twenty-seven, Charles Wilson was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics for the cloud chamber. But the real payoff was what other people did with it. Because once you can photograph particle tracks, you can do something powerful. You can put the whole chamber inside a magnetic field.

NARRATOR: A magnetic field bends the path of a moving charge into a curve. The direction it bends tells you the sign of the charge, positive or negative. And the tightness of the curve, the radius, tells you about the particle's charge-to-mass ratio and its speed. So now the cloud chamber was not just showing tracks. It was weighing and identifying the particles that made them.

NARRATOR: And this is where it got wild. Physicists pointed cloud chambers at cosmic rays, the shower of particles raining down on us from deep space, and they started seeing tracks that made no sense. Curved tracks with the wrong radius. Particles that were clearly negative, like electrons, but that curved too gently, as though they were far too heavy to be electrons. Something with a negative charge, but with a charge-to-mass ratio nothing like an electron, a proton, or an alpha.

NARRATOR: It was a new particle. Heavier than an electron, but behaving like one. What we now call the muon, a kind of heavy cousin of the electron, a particle nobody had predicted and nobody had asked for. When one physicist heard it had been discovered, he famously asked, who ordered that? Nature, it turned out, was stranger and more crowded than anyone had guessed. And a chamber built by a man who wanted to make pretty clouds had cracked open the whole subatomic zoo.

NARRATOR: That is the arc of it. A dreamer trying to bottle a mountain sunset ends up building the instrument that lets humanity watch single atoms shatter, and in those glinting threads of mist, physicists read the fingerprints of particles from the edge of the known universe.

NARRATOR: When you meet the muon again, in the story of the Frisch and Smith experiment, and see how these fleeting cosmic visitors gave us some of the clearest proof that time itself can stretch, remember where we first learned to see them. In a jar of supercooled fog, watching ghosts draw their own picture.
