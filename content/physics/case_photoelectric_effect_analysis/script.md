NARRATOR: This is part of Module 7, The Nature of Light. And I want to start with an experiment you can almost picture on a bench in front of you. Take a clean plate of metal, and shine a light on it. If the light is bright enough, electrons should come flying off the surface. That's the idea. Now here's the strange part. Take a brilliant, blazing red light, as intense as you like, and aim it at the plate. Nothing happens. Not a single electron leaves. Now take a feeble, dim violet light, so faint you can barely see it, and point that at the same plate. Electrons pour off instantly.

QUESTION: How can a dazzling bright light do nothing, while a dim one does everything?

NARRATOR: That question doesn't just sound odd. For the physicists of the year nineteen hundred, it was impossible. It broke the rules of everything they thought they knew about light. And the man who cracked it didn't win his Nobel Prize for relativity, or for the famous equation about energy and mass. He won it for solving this. This is the story of the photoelectric effect, and how a puzzle about a metal plate tore a hole in classical physics and let the quantum world climb through.

NARRATOR: To feel why this was so shocking, you have to understand how sure everyone was about light. By the end of the eighteen hundreds, the question of what light is felt settled, and settled beautifully. Thomas Young had passed light through two narrow slits and watched it produce bands of bright and dark, an interference pattern. Only waves do that. Waves overlap, reinforce, cancel. Particles don't. Then James Clerk Maxwell had gone further and shown, in a set of equations of almost frightening elegance, that light simply is a wave, a ripple of electric and magnetic fields marching through space at one fixed, glorious speed. Light was a wave. It was proven. It was done.

NARRATOR: And a wave has a very particular way of carrying energy. Think of the ocean. What decides how hard a wave hits you? Its height. A tall wave carries more energy than a small one, no matter how quickly the waves are rolling in. For light, the height of the wave is its brightness, its intensity. So the prediction was clear and confident. If you want to knock an electron out of a metal, you pour energy into it. A brighter light carries more energy. So a bright enough light, of any colour at all, should always work. And if the light were dim, the electron would simply have to sit and soak up the energy for a while, like a bucket filling under a slow tap, until it had enough to jump free. Wait long enough, and any light should do the job.

NARRATOR: Every single part of that prediction turned out to be wrong. And the first crack appeared, with delicious irony, in the hands of the very man who had just proved light was a wave.

NARRATOR: His name was Heinrich Hertz. In eighteen eighty-seven, Hertz was generating sparks across a tiny gap between two metal balls, doing the experiments that confirmed Maxwell's waves were real. And he noticed something he couldn't explain. When he shone ultraviolet light onto the metal, the sparks jumped more easily. The light was somehow helping electricity leap the gap. Hertz wrote it down, found it curious, and moved on. He had no idea he had just seen the first hint of a revolution. The clue sat there, waiting.

NARRATOR: It was picked up by another physicist, Philipp Lenard, who decided to measure this effect properly. And his setup is the heart of the whole story, so let me put you in the room. Inside a sealed glass tube with the air pumped out, he placed two metal electrodes. He shone light onto one of them, the emitter, and light knocked electrons off its surface. Those electrons flew across the gap to the other electrode, and that flow of electrons was a tiny electric current he could measure on a meter. So now he had a dial that told him, in real numbers, how many electrons were coming off and how much energy they carried.

NARRATOR: Here's the clever trick. To measure the energy of the ejected electrons, he pushed back against them. He put a voltage on the far electrode that repelled the electrons, trying to turn them around before they arrived. A slow electron gets stopped by a gentle push. A fast one needs a harder push. So he turned up the opposing voltage until the current fell to exactly zero, until even the fastest electron was just barely turned back. That special voltage is called the stopping voltage, and it's a direct measurement of the maximum energy those electrons had when they left the metal.

QUESTION: So what did the dial actually say?

NARRATOR: It said something that made no sense. First, Lenard changed the brightness of the light while keeping its colour the same. Wave theory said brighter light means more energetic electrons. But the energy of each electron didn't budge. A brighter light kicked out more electrons, yes, but every one of them came off with exactly the same maximum energy as before. The stopping voltage didn't move at all.

NARRATOR: Then he changed the colour of the light, its frequency, and that changed everything. Shift the light towards the violet and ultraviolet, towards higher frequency, and suddenly the electrons came off faster, with more energy. Shift it towards the red, lower frequency, and they came off slower. The energy of each electron depended only on the colour of the light, not its brightness. And there was a cliff edge. Below a certain frequency, a certain colour, no electrons came off at all. Not a trickle. Nothing. You could make that red light as blindingly bright as you wanted, and shine it for an hour, and the metal just sat there. That threshold frequency was the wall the red light could never climb.

NARRATOR: And the timing was the final blow. Wave theory said a dim light should need time, the electron slowly filling its bucket. But whenever electrons came off at all, they came off instantly, the very moment the light arrived, with no waiting, no delay, even for the faintest beam. Four results, and all four of them were things a wave was simply not allowed to do.

NARRATOR: For five years, nobody could explain it. And then, in nineteen oh five, a twenty-six-year-old clerk in a Swiss patent office published a short paper that quietly detonated the whole picture. His name was Albert Einstein.

NARRATOR: Einstein's idea was almost absurd in its simplicity. What if light isn't a smooth, continuous wave after all? What if light comes in tiny, indivisible packets of energy, little bullets we now call photons? And the energy carried by each single photon depends only on the light's frequency, its colour. High frequency, violet light, means high-energy photons. Low frequency, red light, means low-energy photons. The precise rule is that a photon's energy equals a fixed number of nature, called Planck's constant, multiplied by the light's frequency. Planck's constant is a tiny thing, about six point six three times ten to the minus thirty-four joule seconds, but it sets the size of every packet.

NARRATOR: Now watch how every mystery falls apart at once. When light hits the metal, it's not a wave washing over the whole surface. It's a hail of individual photons, and it's one photon, one electron, a private collision. To escape the metal, an electron has to pay a fixed energy toll to break free of the surface, a toll called the work function. If a single photon carries more energy than that toll, the electron escapes, and it keeps whatever energy is left over as its speed. If the photon carries less than the toll, the electron cannot leave, full stop, no matter how many such photons arrive.

NARRATOR: That's the red light. Each red photon is too weak, too low in frequency, to pay the toll. Send a billion of them, and you just have a billion failed payments, because no electron can save up energy from two photons at once. But a single violet photon, higher frequency, carries enough on its own. That's the threshold. That's the cliff. Make the light brighter and you just send more photons, so more electrons come off, but each one still carries the same leftover energy, because each was freed by one photon of the same colour. And there's no delay, because the moment a photon arrives, the collision happens. Every single one of Lenard's impossible results, explained, in one stroke.

NARRATOR: Einstein even wrote it as a straight line. The maximum energy of an ejected electron equals the photon's energy minus the work function toll. Plot the electron's energy against the frequency of the light and you get a perfect straight line, and the slope of that line is Planck's constant itself, the same number, showing up in an experiment about metal plates.

NARRATOR: But a beautiful idea is not proof. And one man set out to destroy it. The American physicist Robert Millikan thought Einstein's photon was nonsense, a betrayal of everything wave theory had earned. So he spent about a decade, ten painstaking years, building exquisite experiments designed to prove Einstein wrong.

QUESTION: And did he?

NARRATOR: He proved him exactly right. Millikan's careful measurements traced out Einstein's straight line perfectly, and the slope handed him a value for Planck's constant that agreed with everything else. Grudgingly, he had confirmed the very theory he set out to demolish. That is how you know a result is real, when even its enemies, trying their hardest to bury it, end up carving its name in stone.

NARRATOR: And so this modest experiment, light on a metal plate, became one of the hinges of modern physics. It proved that light is not only a wave. Light also arrives in particles, in photons, carrying energy in discrete packets set by its frequency. Light is somehow both at once, and that dual nature, wave and particle together, is the strange foundation the entire quantum world is built on. When the Nobel committee honoured Einstein in nineteen twenty-one, it was not for relativity. It was for this, for the law of the photoelectric effect.

NARRATOR: A dim violet light did what a blazing red one never could, and in explaining why, physics was never the same again. To follow the physics itself, head to the teaching topic on the photoelectric effect and the quantum nature of light.
