NARRATOR: This is part of Module Seven, The Nature of Light. And I want to start with a promise that sounds impossible. If I told you that I could hand you a clock, send it around the world on an ordinary passenger jet, and have it come back reading a genuinely different time than the clock sitting on your desk, you would probably assume I meant it drifted, or got knocked, or lost a few seconds because of the cold at altitude. You would assume it was a mistake. It was not a mistake. The clock really did tick a different amount of time. Not because it was faulty. Because time itself, for that clock, ran at a different rate than it did for you.

QUESTION: How can two clocks, built identically, honestly disagree about how much time has passed?

NARRATOR: That is the question at the heart of everything Einstein tried to tell us, and for more than half a century almost nobody had ever actually watched it happen. To understand why that is so strange, we have to go back to what people believed before, and why it took so long to prove them wrong.

NARRATOR: When Einstein published special relativity in nineteen oh five, it landed like a provocation. He was claiming that time is not some universal river flowing at the same rate for everyone everywhere. He was claiming that a moving clock runs slow, and that the faster it moves, the slower it ticks, relative to one standing still. This idea, which we call time dilation, is not a trick of the instruments. It is not that motion damages clocks. It is that motion changes time.

NARRATOR: And here is the thing that makes it so hard to accept. It goes against every instinct you have ever had. That is not a failing on your part. It is because in ordinary life you never move fast enough, and you never measure time precisely enough, to notice. The effect is real at every speed, but at the speeds of a car, or a train, or even a bullet, it is so unimaginably tiny that no watch, no stopwatch, no human sense could ever catch it. So for decades, relativity sat there, mathematically beautiful, experimentally almost untouchable, asking the world to believe something it could not see.

QUESTION: If nobody could see it, how did physicists become convinced it was true at all?

NARRATOR: By the nineteen seventies they had gathered real evidence, and the best of it came from tiny particles called muons. Muons are created high in the atmosphere when cosmic rays strike it, and they are unstable, they decay in a flicker of a moment. By rights, moving as they do, they should decay long before reaching the ground. Yet they arrive at the surface in numbers that only make sense if their internal clocks are running slow, stretched out by their enormous speed, exactly as Einstein predicted. That muon evidence is, honestly, the cleaner proof. If you ever have to argue for special relativity, that is the one I would reach for first.

NARRATOR: But the muon experiment has a catch. It is invisible and abstract. You are not watching a clock. You are counting particles and inferring a decayed lifetime from a statistical distribution. Nobody had ever stood in a room, looked at two real clocks, the kind with a face and a reading, and pointed and said, this one ran slow, and here is the proof in seconds. Nobody had ever seen a moving clock actually running slower. And that, it turns out, is a completely different kind of convincing.

NARRATOR: Which brings us to a man preparing for a lecture. In nineteen seventy, an assistant professor named Joseph Hafele was writing up notes for a physics class, trying to explain time dilation to his students. And almost as a doodle, a back of the envelope calculation, he worked out how big the effect would be for something ordinary. Not a cosmic ray. Not a particle accelerator. A commercial airliner.

QUESTION: Why an airliner, of all things?

NARRATOR: Because two technologies had quietly arrived at exactly the right moment. First, commercial jets could now hold a relatively high speed, not fast compared to light, but crucially, they could hold it steadily for hours and hours, all the way around the planet. And second, and this is the real breakthrough, humanity had built atomic clocks, caesium clocks, of almost unbelievable precision. Clocks that keep time not with a pendulum or a spring, but with the natural, unwavering vibration of caesium atoms. When Hafele finished his scribbled calculation, he realised something remarkable. The tininess of the effect, the very thing that had hidden it for sixty five years, was finally, just barely, within reach. A caesium clock flown on an airliner should lose or gain enough time to be measured.

NARRATOR: Now, having the idea and having the means are two different things. Hafele had the calculation. What he did not have was the clocks, or the access, or the money. Then he was approached by Richard Keating, an astronomer at the United States Naval Observatory, one of the few institutions on Earth that actually worked with atomic clocks and could get its hands on them. It was a perfect pairing. The theorist with the idea, the observatory man with the hardware. Together they went looking for funding, and here is one of my favourite details in all of experimental physics. They pulled it off for eight thousand dollars.

NARRATOR: Eight thousand dollars. At the time, that made it the cheapest experiment on relativity, special or general, that had ever been attempted. And of that eight thousand, seven thousand six hundred went on plane tickets. Because they did not just buy seats for themselves. They bought seats for the clocks. Around the world flights, eastward and then westward, and the clocks needed their own paid seats to ride along. So the most famous test of Einstein's theory of time was, in its budget, mostly a stack of airline tickets for four machines and the two men minding them.

QUESTION: So what exactly did they do once they were on board?

NARRATOR: Here is the design, and it is beautifully simple. They took four atomic clocks. They made two around the world trips, the same four clocks each time. One trip headed east, in the direction the Earth spins. The other headed west, against the spin. And crucially, back home at the Naval Observatory, a set of reference clocks stayed behind on the ground. The whole experiment comes down to one comparison. Fly the clocks around the world, bring them home, and hold them up against the clocks that never left. Do the travelling clocks agree with the stay at home clocks, or not?

NARRATOR: Now, before we get to the answer, we need to think like Hafele and Keating did, because there is a subtlety here that trips almost everyone. They predicted two separate effects pulling on the flying clocks. The first comes from general relativity, from gravity. Up at cruising altitude, the clocks sit higher in Earth's gravitational field, at a greater gravitational potential, and that makes them run a little faster, gain a little time, compared to clocks down on the surface. That gravitational effect is real, and it was part of what they measured. But in the HSC we do not deal with general relativity, so beyond knowing it was in there, we are going to set it aside and focus on the effect that is ours.

NARRATOR: And the effect that is ours is the motion. Time dilation from velocity. Here is where you have to be careful, because you cannot just say the plane is moving and the ground is still. Moving relative to what? To answer honestly, you have to pick a good reference frame, and the best one available is the frame sitting at the centre of the Earth, not spinning, just looking out at the whole rotating planet from the middle.

QUESTION: Why does the centre of the Earth change the answer?

NARRATOR: Because from out there, in that central frame, nobody is standing still. Watch what happens. The clock back at the observatory, the one we call stationary, is not stationary at all. It is being carried around in a giant circle by the rotation of the Earth, moving east at the speed of the spinning surface. So even the ground clock is running a little slow.

NARRATOR: Now bring in the plane heading east, in the same direction as the spin. From the centre of the Earth, its speed is the Earth's rotation plus the plane's own speed on top. It is the fastest thing in the picture. So it should run the slowest of all. When that eastward clock comes home, we expect it to read less time than the ground clock. It aged less. Its seconds were stretched the most.

NARRATOR: And now the clever twist. The plane heading west flies against the spin. From the centre of the Earth, its own speed is subtracted from the Earth's rotation. It is moving slower through that frame than even the ground beneath it. So while it still experiences time dilation, it experiences less of it than the ground clock does. And that means, relative to the ground, the westward clock should run faster. When it comes home, we expect it to read more time than the clock that stayed behind. One clock loses time going one way, the other gains time going the other way. Same experiment, opposite signs, and that opposite sign is what makes it such a satisfying test. A sloppy error would push both clocks the same direction. Relativity says they should split.

NARRATOR: Hafele and Keating did not just wave their hands at this. They sat down before the flights and calculated hard numbers, predictions in billionths of a second, for how much each clock should gain or lose, combining the gravitational effect and the velocity effect together. They wrote those predictions down in advance. That is what turns a good story into science. You commit to the number before you look.

QUESTION: And when they finally compared the clocks, what did they find?

NARRATOR: The clocks had disagreed. And they had disagreed in the directions predicted, by amounts that matched the predictions to a very acceptable degree given the messiness of real flights, real turbulence, real flight delays, real clocks that are precise but not perfect. The eastward clock came home having lost time. The westward clock came home having gained it. Not because anything was broken. Because time had genuinely flowed at different rates for those clocks, depending on how they moved through the world.

NARRATOR: Now, I want to be honest with you, the way the student who wrote this up was honest. This is not the tightest, cleanest evidence for relativity in existence. The muons are better. The uncertainties here are larger, the effect is small and hard won, and a careful critic can pick at the error bars. If you are choosing evidence for special relativity on pure quality, this is not the one.

NARRATOR: But quality is not the only thing that matters, and this experiment did something the muons never could. It made time dilation tangible. For the first time in the history of the human race, you could stand in an ordinary room, hold up two clocks that were built the same and set the same, one of which had simply gone on a trip, and see with your own eyes that they no longer agreed. A real clock, a thing with a reading, had measurably run slow. Not a particle. Not an inference from a decay curve. A clock you could carry.

NARRATOR: That is why this experiment became famous in a way the muons never did. It escaped the physics journals. It got into newspapers, into the public imagination, because anyone could grasp the punchline. Take a clock on a plane, and it comes back out of step with the world. Einstein's strangest, most intuition breaking claim, the one that had waited sixty six years for someone to simply point at it, was finally something you could see.

NARRATOR: And there is a beautiful modesty in how it happened. Not a billion dollar accelerator. Not a mountaintop observatory. A scribbled calculation before a lecture, two men, four clocks, eight thousand dollars, and a stack of around the world plane tickets. Sometimes the deepest truths about the universe are hiding in the most ordinary places, waiting only for someone precise enough to measure them.
