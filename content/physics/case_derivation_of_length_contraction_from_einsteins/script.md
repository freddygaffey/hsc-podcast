NARRATOR: This is part of Module Seven, The Nature of Light. And I want to start with a claim that sounds like a lie. If something rushes past you fast enough, close to the speed of light, then that object literally becomes shorter. Not looks shorter. Not seems shorter through some trick of the eye. It is shorter. A metre-long rod flies by, and to you it might measure half a metre, or a tenth of a metre, or less. Squashed. And here is the part that breaks people's brains: the rod hasn't been crushed. Nothing pushed on it. No force squeezed it. Space itself, along its direction of motion, has simply shrunk.

QUESTION: So who was mad enough to suggest that?

NARRATOR: A twenty-six-year-old patent clerk in Bern, Switzerland, in the year nineteen oh five. His name was Albert Einstein. And he didn't reach this by building a giant machine or measuring flying rods in a laboratory. He reached it by sitting still and thinking very carefully about two simple ideas. Two postulates. From those two sentences alone, out fell one of the strangest predictions in all of physics.

NARRATOR: Here are the two ideas. The first: the laws of physics are the same for every observer moving at a steady speed. There is no experiment you can do on a smoothly moving train to prove you're moving at all. The second idea is the troublemaker. The speed of light in a vacuum is the same for everyone, no matter how fast they move. It's a fixed number, about three hundred million metres per second. Chase a beam of light as fast as you like, and it still races away from you at that exact same speed.

QUESTION: Why is that second idea such a problem?

NARRATOR: Because it wrecks common sense. Think about a car. If a car does one hundred kilometres an hour, and you chase it at ninety, then from your seat the car crawls away at just ten. Speeds subtract. That's how the world is supposed to work. But light refuses to play along. You can sprint towards it at nearly light speed, and it still leaves you at the full three hundred million metres per second. The one thing everyone assumed was flexible, speed, turns out to be locked. And if speed is locked, then something else has to give. That something is space and time themselves.

NARRATOR: So let's do exactly what Einstein did. Let's run a thought experiment. And we'll use a little particle called a muon as our traveller. Now, a muon here is just a convenient stand-in. Pick anything you like moving fast. The muon flies through the sky beneath two clouds, and it passes the first cloud, then the second. Two events. A start line and a finish line.

NARRATOR: Watch it first from the ground, standing still. From here, the distance between those two clouds is what we'll call the rest length. Rest, because from our point of view the clouds aren't going anywhere. Let the muon take some interval of time to cross from the first cloud to the second. Then the muon's speed is simple. It's that rest length, divided by that time. Distance over time. The most ordinary equation there is.

NARRATOR: Now here's the pivot. We climb aboard the muon and ride along with it. And from up here, everything flips. The muon feels perfectly still. It's the ground, and the two clouds, that come rushing towards it. The first cloud arrives, then the second cloud arrives. And the distance between those clouds, measured now from the muon's own frame, we'll just call the length. The time between the two clouds passing, in this frame, is a different interval. And the muon's speed is that length, divided by that new time.

QUESTION: But surely the speed has to come out the same either way?

NARRATOR: Exactly. That's the whole trick. The speed is just how fast the ground and the muon slide past each other, and both observers must agree on that one number. So we can set the two versions equal. The rest length divided by the ground's time, equals the length divided by the muon's time. Two different distances, two different times, but the same speed tying them together.

NARRATOR: And now we bring in the one extra fact we already earned, from the twin idea to this one, time dilation. When you look at a moving clock, it ticks slow. From the muon's frame, it's the ground observer's clock that's running slow, that's stretched out, dilated. The ground's time interval is longer than the muon's own time interval, by a specific stretch factor. Physicists call it the Lorentz factor, usually written as the Greek letter gamma. And gamma is always bigger than one whenever anything is actually moving. The faster you go, the bigger it grows, climbing towards infinity as you approach the speed of light.

NARRATOR: So take that relationship, the rest length over the ground's time equals the length over the muon's time, and substitute in the fact that the ground's time is gamma times the muon's time. Do the algebra, and the times cancel clean out of the equation. What you're left with is startling in how tidy it is. The length equals the rest length, divided by gamma.

QUESTION: And what does dividing by gamma actually do?

NARRATOR: It shrinks things. Because gamma is always greater than one, dividing by it always makes the answer smaller. The moving length is always less than the rest length. There it is. Length contraction, falling straight out of two postulates and a bit of algebra. You can also write it the other way round: the length equals the rest length, multiplied by the square root of one minus the speed squared over the speed of light squared. Same statement, dressed differently. As the speed climbs towards the speed of light, that square root shrinks towards zero, and the length is crushed towards nothing.

NARRATOR: Sit with how radical this is. Einstein did not measure a single flying object. He wrote down two reasonable-sounding sentences, insisted on taking them completely seriously, and out came the conclusion that a moving object is genuinely shorter along its direction of travel. Not an illusion. Not a distortion. The actual, measurable size of things depends on how fast they move relative to you. And by the same logic, the length you measure and the length someone else measures can honestly disagree, and you're both right.

NARRATOR: For centuries, space had been the stage. Fixed. Absolute. The one thing you could count on to just sit there while the drama played out on top of it. Einstein pulled the stage itself into the action. Distance is no longer something the universe hands you. It's something you and your speed decide, together. And that idea, born in a patent office from nothing but careful thinking, is the ground floor of modern physics.

NARRATOR: To go deeper on where that stretch factor comes from, and why moving clocks run slow, head to the teaching topic on time dilation and the Lorentz factor. That's the twin of this story, and the key that unlocked it.
