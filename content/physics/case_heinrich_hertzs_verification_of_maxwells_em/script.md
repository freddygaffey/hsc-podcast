NARRATOR: This is part of Module Seven, The Nature of Light. And I want to start with a confession from the man at the centre of this story. A student once asked Heinrich Hertz what his great discovery was actually good for. What could you do with it? And Hertz shrugged and said: nothing. It is of no use whatsoever. This is just an experiment that proves a professor was right. We simply have these mysterious waves that we cannot see with the naked eye. But they are there.

NARRATOR: Sit with that for a second. The man who discovered radio waves, the invisible ripples that now carry every phone call, every song, every message across the entire planet, looked at what he had made and genuinely believed it was useless. A curiosity. A footnote. He would die at thirty six, years before anyone sent a signal across an ocean, never knowing he had cracked open the twentieth century.

QUESTION: So how does a man discover something that changes the world, and completely fail to notice?

NARRATOR: To understand that, you have to understand what he was actually looking for. Because Hertz was not trying to invent radio. Radio did not exist as an idea. He was chasing a ghost. A prediction on paper that half the scientists in Europe quietly suspected was wrong.

NARRATOR: Let me take you back a couple of decades before Hertz lit his first spark. To a Scottish physicist named James Clerk Maxwell. Maxwell did something that, when you first hear it, sounds almost like a magic trick. He took everything humanity knew about electricity and everything it knew about magnetism, two forces that for centuries had seemed like completely separate things, and he wrote them down as a small set of equations. And when he looked at those equations, he noticed they contained a hidden message.

NARRATOR: The equations said that if you take an electric charge and shake it, if you make it oscillate back and forth, it should produce a wave. Not a wave in water, not a wave in air, but a wave made of electric and magnetic fields themselves, feeding off each other, leapfrogging through empty space. A changing electric field creates a magnetic field. That changing magnetic field creates an electric field. And so on, and so on, the two of them dragging each other forward through the void.

NARRATOR: Now here is the part that made Maxwell sit up. His equations also told him how fast this wave would travel. And when he calculated the speed, he got a number. Three times ten to the eight metres per second. Three hundred thousand kilometres every single second. And that number was already famous. It was the measured speed of light.

QUESTION: Wait, the speed of his imaginary electromagnetic wave was exactly the speed of light?

NARRATOR: Exactly it. And Maxwell drew the only conclusion he could. Light itself, he said, must be one of these electromagnetic waves. The glow of the sun, the flame of a candle, the colours of a rainbow, all of it was electric and magnetic fields rippling through space. It was one of the most beautiful ideas in the history of science. And here was the truly wild part of the prediction. Visible light was just one narrow slice. His equations put no limit on the frequency. There should be electromagnetic waves out there with frequencies far below anything the eye could see, and far above it too. An entire invisible spectrum. A whole universe of light we were completely blind to.

NARRATOR: It was gorgeous. It was elegant. And there was one enormous problem. Nobody had ever seen one of these invisible waves. Not one. The visible light we already knew about, sure. But the rest of the spectrum, the part that would prove Maxwell was really onto something and not just playing with pretty algebra, that was pure theory. Ink on a page.

NARRATOR: And you have to remember how radical this was. Maxwell was claiming that space could carry a wave with nothing physically waving. To a lot of very serious scientists, that felt like a step too far. Across the channel in Britain, Maxwell had followers. But in Germany, where Hertz was trained, the mood was more sceptical. A competing theory of electricity was still taken very seriously, and Maxwell's fields were treated as a foreign curiosity that might not survive contact with the real world.

NARRATOR: The stakes were high enough that one of the giants of German physics, Hermann von Helmholtz, essentially put a bounty on it. He arranged for a scientific prize, offered to anyone who could take Maxwell's predictions out of the realm of theory and demonstrate these waves in a laboratory. Find the invisible light. Prove the equations were describing reality. Helmholtz, as it happened, was Hertz's own teacher. He pointed his brilliant young student straight at the problem.

QUESTION: So how do you catch a wave that nobody has ever seen, that you cannot feel, and that might not even exist?

NARRATOR: This is where Hertz's genius really shows. Because he understood something crucial about Maxwell's prediction. If shaking an electric charge makes a wave, then he did not need anything exotic. He just needed to shake charges very, very fast. He needed to make electric current slosh back and forth millions of times a second. And he found a way to do it.

NARRATOR: The year is eighteen eighty seven. Hertz builds what we now call a spark gap transmitter. Picture two metal rods pointing at each other, with a small air gap between their tips, and a metal ball on each end. He connects this to an induction coil, a device that could ramp a modest voltage up into an enormous one. He uses that huge voltage to pile electric charge onto the conductors, more and more, until the air in the gap simply cannot hold it back.

NARRATOR: And then, crack. A spark leaps across the gap. A tiny, violent bolt of lightning in miniature.

QUESTION: But surely a single spark is just a single spark. How does that make a continuous wave?

NARRATOR: Beautiful question, and here is the subtle bit. The spark itself is not the wave. The spark is the kickstarter. When that gap breaks down, it suddenly connects the circuit, and the charge that was piled up does not just flow away calmly. It surges back and forth, back and forth, oscillating at an incredibly high frequency, ringing like a struck bell. Think of it exactly like plucking a guitar string. One pluck, but the string then vibrates at its own natural note. Hertz's circuit had a natural electrical note, a resonant frequency up in the radio range, and that spark plucked it. The exact mechanism that turns those sloshing currents into a launched wave runs through something called inductance, which sits beyond our syllabus, but the picture is what matters. Charges accelerating back and forth, millions of times a second, throwing off electromagnetic waves into the room. Precisely as Maxwell had promised.

NARRATOR: So now Hertz has, in principle, filled his laboratory with invisible waves. But invisible is the whole problem. How do you know they are there? You cannot see them. You cannot hear them. You cannot feel them on your skin.

NARRATOR: So Hertz builds a detector, and it is almost breathtakingly simple. It is a loop of wire, a ring, bent into almost a full circle, with another tiny gap left between its two ends. That is essentially the whole thing. A ring of wire with a gap. His reasoning was this. If an electromagnetic wave really is a travelling pattern of oscillating electric and magnetic fields, then when it washes over this ring, those fields should push the charges in the wire and drive a current around the loop. And if he tuned the ring to resonate at the same frequency as the transmitter, that effect would build up just enough to force a spark to jump across the ring's little gap. No wires connecting the two devices. Nothing between them but empty air and, if Maxwell was right, invisible waves.

NARRATOR: And now picture the scene. Hertz darkens the room. He stands on the far side, holding this little ring of wire, metres away from the transmitter, nothing physically connecting them. He fires the transmitter. Crack, the big spark jumps. And across the room, in the gap of the ring he is holding in his hand, a second tiny spark flickers into life. A faint blue thread of light, appearing out of nowhere.

QUESTION: Something reached across that room with no wire, no contact, nothing visible at all, and made a spark jump. What was carrying it?

NARRATOR: Maxwell's waves. There was no other explanation. Energy had leapt across empty space and made charges move on the other side of the room. The invisible light was real. And you can imagine the young man alone in that dark laboratory, watching that faint spark blink in his hand, realising he was the first human being ever to knowingly send and catch a radio wave.

NARRATOR: But here is what separates a lucky observation from a genuine proof, and here is why Hertz is remembered. He did not stop at making the spark appear. He asked a harder question. If these really are waves, and if Maxwell is right that light is the same kind of thing, then they should behave exactly like light. They should do everything light does. So Hertz set out to make his invisible waves obey every rule in the optics textbook, one by one.

NARRATOR: First, reflection. He took a flat sheet of metal and stood it in the path of the waves. And the waves bounced. They reflected off the metal exactly the way light reflects off a mirror. Tick.

NARRATOR: Next, refraction. When light passes into glass or water it bends, which is why a straw looks broken in a glass of water. So Hertz built an enormous prism, not out of glass but out of pitch, a tar like material, a block big enough to steer these long waves. And when he passed his waves through it, the beam bent. He had to physically move his detector to a new position to catch the signal again, because the prism had refracted the waves off in a new direction. Tick.

NARRATOR: Then came the most elegant experiment of all, and the one that let him measure everything. Hertz took his reflecting metal sheet and set it up facing the transmitter, so the waves would travel out, hit the sheet, and bounce straight back the way they came. Now he had waves going forwards overlapping with waves coming back. And when a wave overlaps with its own reflection, something remarkable happens. They combine into what is called a standing wave. The pattern appears to freeze in space. There are certain fixed points, called nodes, where the two waves always cancel each other out perfectly, and the field is dead still. And between them, points called antinodes, where they reinforce, and the field swings as hard as it possibly can.

NARRATOR: So Hertz walked his little ring detector slowly through the room. And it was uncanny. At certain spots, nothing. Dead silence, no spark, no matter how many times the transmitter fired. He would move it just a little further along, and the spark would leap back to life. Silence, spark, silence, spark, a regular pattern of dead zones and live zones hanging invisibly in the air of his laboratory. Those silent spots were the nodes. The bright spots were the antinodes. This was interference, the signature behaviour of a wave, made visible in flashes of blue light.

QUESTION: So why does that pattern matter so much? Why is that the crucial experiment?

NARRATOR: Because it let him measure the wavelength. In a standing wave, the distance between one node and the next is exactly half a wavelength. Hertz could pace out the distance between his silent spots with a ruler. Double it, and he had the wavelength of an invisible wave. Read that again. He physically measured the length of a ripple of light that no eye can see, by finding the quiet places in an empty room.

NARRATOR: And now he had everything he needed for the final blow. He knew the resonant frequency of his apparatus, so he knew how many times per second the wave oscillated. And he had just measured its wavelength. There is a simple relationship that governs every wave in the universe. The speed of a wave equals its frequency multiplied by its wavelength. So Hertz multiplied the two numbers together. And out came the speed of his invisible waves.

NARRATOR: Three times ten to the eight metres per second. The speed of light. The exact number Maxwell had pulled from his equations twenty years earlier, sitting at a desk, having never built a thing. Hertz's homemade sparks and rings of wire had reached across a laboratory and confirmed it. These invisible waves and visible light were the same phenomenon, travelling at the same universal speed. Maxwell was right. The equations were describing something real.

NARRATOR: There is one last detail, and it is one of the great ironies in all of physics. Hertz noticed that when he placed a sheet of glass between the transmitter and his detector, the receiving spark got dimmer. But when he used a window of quartz instead, it made no difference at all. It was a small, strange effect. He noted it, puzzled over it, and moved on. What Hertz did not know was that his spark was giving off not just radio waves but a little ultraviolet light, and that ultraviolet was helping the receiver spark along. The glass was absorbing the ultraviolet. The quartz let it through. Hertz had stumbled onto the very first hint of the photoelectric effect, the phenomenon that would, a generation later, force physicists to accept that light is also made of particles, and help launch the quantum revolution. The man who proved light was a wave had accidentally caught the first glimpse that it was also something else. He never followed it up.

NARRATOR: And that brings us back to where we began. To Hertz saying his discovery was of no use whatsoever. He genuinely could not see it. He had confirmed a theory, and to him that was the end. But within a decade of his early death, a young Italian named Guglielmo Marconi took those useless waves and sent a message across the Atlantic Ocean. Radio. Television. Radar. Wifi. Every phone in every pocket on Earth. All of it flowing out of a faint spark in a dark room, and a ring of wire held in the hand of a man who thought he had discovered nothing at all.

NARRATOR: We honour him every single day without noticing. The unit we use to count the frequency of a wave, the number of oscillations per second, is called the hertz. Every time your radio is tuned to a station, you are reading his name off the dial.
