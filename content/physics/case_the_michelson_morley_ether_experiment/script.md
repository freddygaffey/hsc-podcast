NARRATOR: This is part of Module Seven, The Nature of Light. And I want to start with a question that sounds almost too simple to be interesting. When you drop a stone into a pond, ripples spread out across the surface. When you speak, sound rolls out through the air. Every wave humanity had ever studied travelled through something. A pond needs water. A voice needs air. So here is the question that broke a generation of the best physicists alive. Light is a wave. What, then, is it waving?

QUESTION: What carries a light wave across the emptiness of space?

NARRATOR: For most of the eighteen hundreds, the answer felt obvious. There had to be something. Some invisible, weightless, frictionless substance filling all of space, right through the room you are sitting in, right through the vacuum between here and the Sun. A medium for light the way water is a medium for ripples. They gave it a beautiful name. The luminiferous ether. Light-bearing ether. And they believed in it, not out of superstition, but because their entire understanding of waves demanded it. To a physicist in eighteen eighty, saying light travelled through nothing at all was like saying you could hear a bell ringing in outer space. It simply did not make sense.

NARRATOR: But there was a genius who had, almost accidentally, set a trap for this idea. His name was James Clerk Maxwell. In the eighteen sixties, Maxwell had done something staggering. He took the scattered laws of electricity and magnetism and stitched them into a single set of equations, the fundamental laws governing electric and magnetic fields. And when he solved those equations, out fell a wave. A ripple of electric and magnetic fields, feeding into each other, racing forward at one fixed speed. That speed turned out to be the speed of light. Maxwell had discovered that light is an electromagnetic wave. It was one of the great triumphs of all physics.

NARRATOR: But hidden inside that triumph was a bombshell. Maxwell's equations described waves that needed no medium at all. The mathematics did not ask for any ether. It described light propagating through pure, empty vacuum, and it worked perfectly. And this sparked real controversy in the scientific community, because everything they knew about waves said a wave must have a medium. So most physicists made a compromise. They kept the ether. They decided Maxwell's beautiful vacuum was really filled, invisibly, with this ether, and that the ether was the true stage on which light performed. The problem was, nobody had ever seen it, touched it, or measured it. It was a substance defined entirely by the fact that you could not detect it by any conventional means.

QUESTION: If the ether is invisible and undetectable, how could anyone ever prove it is real?

NARRATOR: This is where our hero enters. Albert Michelson. And Michelson had an idea so clever it still gives me chills. If you cannot detect the ether directly, he reasoned, then detect the Earth moving through it. Think about it. The Earth is not sitting still. It is hurtling around the Sun at enormous speed, tearing through space. If space is filled with a stationary ether, then as the Earth ploughs forward, we should feel a kind of headwind. Not a wind of air, but a wind of ether. An ether wind, streaming past us as we race along our orbit. And this is the crucial part. Michelson realised that this ether wind should change the speed of light itself, depending on which direction you measured it.

NARRATOR: Let me give you the picture Michelson had in his head, because it is the whole experiment. Imagine a wide, fast-flowing river. And imagine a boat that can always travel at exactly the same speed relative to the water. Now send that boat on two journeys. First, straight across the river to the far bank and back. Second, the same distance downstream and then back upstream against the current. Here is the beautiful, non-obvious truth. Even though both trips cover the same distance, and the boat always moves at the same speed through the water, the two round trips do not take the same time. Fighting upstream against the current, then being swept back down, always costs you more time overall than the clean trip across and back. The current leaves a fingerprint in the timing.

NARRATOR: Now swap the pieces. The river is the ether. The current is the ether wind, caused by the Earth's motion. And the boat is a beam of light. Michelson's argument was this. Two beams of light sent on perpendicular journeys, one along the ether wind and one across it, should take slightly different times to return, even over identical distances. From our frame of reference here on the moving Earth, the two beams would appear to travel at slightly different speeds. And if he could catch that tiny difference in timing, he would have measured the velocity of the Earth relative to the ether. He would have found the invisible ocean at last.

QUESTION: But light is unimaginably fast, so how do you time a difference that small?

NARRATOR: You cannot, not with a stopwatch. The difference we are talking about is fantastically tiny, a fraction of a fraction of the time it takes light to cross a tabletop. No clock on Earth could catch it. And this is Michelson's true stroke of genius. He did not try to time the light. He used light to measure itself, by making the two beams interfere. He built an instrument now called the Michelson interferometer, the very same kind of device that would later be used in Froome's and Evenson's precision measurements of the speed of light. And the way it works is gorgeous.

NARRATOR: Picture a single beam of white light. It travels toward a special mirror in the centre, a half-silvered mirror, one that is only partly reflective. So when the beam hits it, the beam splits in two. Half of the light passes straight through and carries on forward. The other half reflects off at ninety degrees, shooting away at a right angle. Now you have two beams travelling perpendicular to each other, exactly the two journeys from our river. Each beam runs down its own arm, the same carefully set distance, strikes a mirror at the far end, and bounces straight back to the centre. There, the two beams are reunited and recombined, and sent on together into a viewing scope where a person can watch them.

QUESTION: And what do you see when the two beams come back together?

NARRATOR: You see a pattern. Because light is a wave, when the two beams recombine at slightly different angles, their crests and troughs overlap. Where two crests meet, the light adds up and you get a bright band. Where a crest meets a trough, they cancel and you get darkness. The result is a stable pattern of stripes, a set of light and dark fringes, called an interference pattern. You can watch them, steady and sharp, through the eyepiece. And here is why this matters so much. Those fringes are exquisitely sensitive to timing. If one beam is delayed relative to the other by even the tiniest amount, by even a fraction of a single wavelength of light, the whole striped pattern slides sideways. The fringes shift.

NARRATOR: So now you can feel the trap closing. If there really is an ether wind blowing past the Earth, then one arm of the interferometer is pointed along the wind and the other across it. The two beams take slightly different times to return. And that difference should push the fringes to one side. Then Michelson slowly rotated the entire apparatus. As he turned it, the arm that had been pointing along the ether wind would swing across to point against it, and the timing difference would reverse. And the fringes, he predicted, should visibly crawl side to side across the field of view as he rotated the instrument. That drifting of the stripes is called a fringe shift, and it was the signature of the ether. Catch the fringes moving, and you have caught the Earth swimming through the invisible sea.

NARRATOR: To even attempt this, Michelson had to fight the world itself. The instrument was so sensitive that a footstep across the room, a passing carriage in the street, the warmth of his own hand, could blur the fringes into uselessness. In the later, refined version of the experiment, the apparatus was mounted on a massive slab of stone, floated in a pool of liquid mercury so it could be turned smoothly and silently, without a single jolt. He would set it slowly rotating and press his eye to the scope, watching those delicate stripes, waiting for them to drift. He repeated the measurement at many different angles, and at many different times of day, because as the Earth spins and orbits, the direction of the ether wind should keep changing. Sooner or later, if the ether was real, the fringes had to move.

QUESTION: So after all that brilliance and all that effort, what did he actually see?

NARRATOR: Nothing. The fringes did not move. Not by the amount predicted. Not by any meaningful amount at all. Michelson turned the apparatus through every angle, watched it through the hours, and the stripes sat there, stubbornly still. He looked for a large, unmistakable shift, and he found a stillness. In science, we have a name for this. It is called a null result. The experiment worked flawlessly, and it detected exactly nothing. The most sensitive measurement ever attempted had been aimed straight at the ether wind, and the ether wind was not there.

NARRATOR: Now, stop and feel how strange this is. This is one of the most famous failures in the history of science. Michelson set out to measure the speed of the Earth through the ether, and instead he found the single most important nothing anybody has ever recorded. Because a null result is not the absence of a discovery. It is a discovery. It is the universe answering your question with a flat, unexpected no.

NARRATOR: And people scrambled to explain it. Maybe, some said, the Earth drags the ether along with it, like a ball of dough carrying flour on its surface, so that near the ground there is no wind at all. Maybe the ether was there but somehow this medium simply did not influence the speed of light, which, as the student who wrote this beautifully put it, does not make it much of a medium at all. Every rescue attempt made the ether stranger, more slippery, more like an idea that existed only to be excused. But here is a distinction that really matters, and it is easy to get wrong. This experiment did not prove that the ether does not exist. What it did was fail, completely, to measure its velocity, and fail to give any evidence that it was there at all. It did not slam a door. It quietly removed the reason the door was ever built.

QUESTION: So if there is no ether wind, what does that tell us about the speed of light itself?

NARRATOR: Here is the thread that leads out of the maze, and it is astonishing. Think again about what the null result is really saying. No matter which direction Michelson measured, along the Earth's motion or across it, the light came back in the same time. The light did not care how the Earth was moving. Its speed did not shift with our motion through space at all. And that is a clue so radical that it took a mind like Albert Einstein's to seize it and follow it to the end. Where everyone else saw a broken ether that needed patching, Einstein saw something cleaner and far more shocking. What if there is no ether, and the speed of light is simply the same for everyone, always, no matter how you are moving? Not because a stubborn medium hides its wind, but because that constant speed is a fundamental law of the universe itself.

NARRATOR: That single idea, that the speed of light is the same in every frame of reference, is one of the two postulates on which Einstein built special relativity. And out of it come all the things that make relativity sound like a dream. Moving clocks running slow. Moving lengths shrinking. Time and space themselves bending to keep the speed of light fixed. The strange, beautiful physics of the very fast. All of it traces back to a man in a darkened room, turning a stone slab on a pool of mercury, staring at a pattern of stripes that refused to move.

NARRATOR: That is why we still teach this experiment more than a century later. Michelson went hunting for an invisible ocean and came back to tell us the ocean was never there. He asked the universe how fast we were moving through the ether, and the universe answered by telling him that the question itself was wrong. The greatest failure in physics turned out to be one of its greatest gifts, because it cleared the ground for Einstein to build something new.
