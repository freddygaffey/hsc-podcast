NARRATOR: This is part of Module 7, The Nature of Light. And I want to start with a number. Two hundred ninety-nine thousand, seven hundred ninety-two point four five six kilometres per second. That is how fast light moves. Not roughly. Not about three hundred thousand. That exact string of digits, out to a fraction of a kilometre per second, on a thing that crosses the gap between here and the Moon in a little over one second. Here is the question that should stop you cold. How could anyone possibly measure something that fast, that precisely, without a clock fast enough to catch it?

QUESTION: Because surely, to measure a speed, you time how long something takes to travel a known distance?

NARRATOR: That is the obvious way, and for three hundred years it was the only way anyone had. And it kept failing them. Think about the problem. Light is so fast that in the time it takes you to blink, a beam has gone most of the way around the planet. To time it over any distance you could fit in a laboratory, you would need a stopwatch that could split a single second into billions of pieces. In the nineteenth century, no such stopwatch existed. And yet the answer, when it finally came, would be so exact that the world eventually threw away its definition of the metre and rebuilt it around the speed of light. To understand how we got there, you have to meet a man who decided he would never time the light at all.

NARRATOR: For centuries, people had genuinely wondered whether light even had a speed. Maybe it was instantaneous. You open your eyes, the whole sky is just there, all at once. It took the patient tracking of the moons of Jupiter, and later spinning toothed wheels and rotating mirrors, to prove that no, light takes time, and to pin its speed down to within a percent or so. That was extraordinary for the era. But a percent is a rounding error dressed up as an answer. If you want the true speed of light, the number woven into the fabric of the universe, a percent is nowhere near good enough. The methods of the old masters had hit a wall, and the wall was time itself. You cannot measure what you cannot time.

QUESTION: So what if you simply refused to time the light at all?

NARRATOR: That is the beautiful trick at the heart of this whole story. Instead of racing the light against a clock, you make the light race against itself. And the machine that lets you do it is one of the most elegant instruments ever built, the Michelson interferometer. Picture it. A single beam of light travels toward a special mirror in the centre, a mirror that is only half silvered. Half silvered means it is a compromise. Half of the light passes straight through it, and the other half bounces off at a right angle. So one beam becomes two, sent off down two separate arms of the apparatus. At the far end of each arm sits an ordinary mirror, and each of the two beams hits its mirror, turns around, and comes racing straight back to the half-silvered centre, where the two of them are recombined into one.

NARRATOR: Now, when those two beams meet again, something remarkable happens, and it happens because light is a wave. A wave has crests and troughs, high points and low points, marching along one after another. When you overlap two waves, they add together. And how they add depends entirely on whether they are marching in step. If the crests of one beam line up perfectly with the crests of the other, they reinforce, and you get bright light, maximum brightness. We call that constructive interference. But if the crests of one beam land exactly on the troughs of the other, they cancel. High plus low equals nothing. Complete darkness. That is destructive interference, and it is the key to everything.

QUESTION: So when do the two beams come back in step, and when do they cancel?

NARRATOR: It comes down to distance. If the two arms of the interferometer are exactly the same length, both beams travel the same distance out and back, so they arrive perfectly in step, crest on crest, and you see brightness. Now watch what happens if you take one of the far mirrors and very slowly slide it backwards, lengthening that one arm. That beam now has a little farther to travel, so it falls slightly behind its partner. Slide the mirror far enough and eventually one beam is lagging the other by exactly half a wavelength. Half a wavelength means every crest of one beam lands squarely on a trough of the other. They wipe each other out completely. The light, quite literally, goes dark. And here is the thing. You do not need a fast clock to see that. You just need eyes, or a detector, and you watch for the moment the brightness collapses to nothing. That moment is unmistakable.

NARRATOR: And now comes the piece that catches almost everyone out, so listen carefully, because this is worth marks in an exam. You might think, if the beams are half a wavelength out of step, then I must have slid the mirror back by half a wavelength. But no. Remember, the light goes down the arm and comes back. It travels the extra distance twice, once on the way out and once on the way home. So the extra path is double the distance you moved the mirror. For the beam to fall half a wavelength behind, you only have to move the mirror back by a quarter of a wavelength. The distance the mirror moves is one quarter of the wavelength of the light. Get that wrong, forget the round trip, and every number that follows is off by a factor of two.

QUESTION: But we wanted the speed of light, so how does moving a mirror a hair's breadth get us there?

NARRATOR: This is where it all pays off. You have just measured, with a precision screw and a careful hand, exactly how far the mirror moved to reach that moment of perfect darkness. Multiply that distance by four, and you have the wavelength of your light, the length of a single ripple. And you already know one more thing about your beam, because you made it. You know its frequency, how many of those ripples pass by every second. Now, for any wave in the universe, there is one simple, unbreakable relationship. Its speed equals its frequency multiplied by its wavelength. So the speed of light is just its frequency times four times the distance the mirror moved. Frequency you know. Distance you measured. And out drops the speed of light, without ever once timing a beam. You have turned an impossible question about time into a very careful question about length.

NARRATOR: In nineteen fifty-eight, a physicist named Froome took this idea and pushed it hard. But he made a choice that seems, at first, completely backwards. He did not use visible light at all. He used radio waves, microwaves, with wavelengths enormously longer than anything your eye can see. And there was a logic to it. If your ruler has some fixed sloppiness in it, some tiny unavoidable error in measuring distance, then the longer the wavelength you are measuring, the smaller that error becomes as a fraction of the whole. Bigger waves should mean a smaller relative mistake. Froome got a stunning answer. Two hundred ninety-nine thousand, seven hundred ninety-two point five kilometres per second.

QUESTION: So if longer waves are better, why is that not the end of the story?

NARRATOR: Because there was a hidden catch, and it fought back harder than Froome expected. With long radio waves, the moment of destructive interference is not sharp. The darkness does not snap on cleanly. The fringes, the bands of light and dark, spread out wide and blurry, and it becomes genuinely difficult to say exactly where the perfect cancellation lives. You gain precision on your ruler, but you lose it on the very thing you are trying to catch, the instant the light goes dark. And that loss outweighed the gain. Froome's radio waves had run into their own ceiling.

NARRATOR: The escape came from an invention that would define the rest of the century, the laser. A laser gives you visible light of one pure, exact colour, one clean wavelength, in a tight, brilliant, disciplined beam. And with short visible wavelengths, the interference fringes are razor sharp. The darkness snaps on and off with beautiful clarity, so you can pinpoint that moment of cancellation with extraordinary confidence. In nineteen seventy-two, Evansen and his colleagues did exactly that. Same elegant idea as Froome, same interferometer principle, but now with the sharp, clean light of a laser. And their answer was two hundred ninety-nine thousand, seven hundred ninety-two point four five six kilometres per second.

NARRATOR: Sit with how good that is. That value is only about two metres per second away from the number we use for the speed of light today. Two metres per second, on a thing that travels three hundred million metres every single second. It is a match of nearly one part in a hundred million, wrung out of a moving mirror and a beam that goes dark. That is the payoff of the whole three-hundred-year chase. The speed of light was no longer a rough figure with a shrug attached. It had become one of the most precisely known numbers in all of science, so trustworthy that the world eventually turned the definition around. Instead of measuring light with our metre, we now define the metre using the speed of light. The thing we spent centuries struggling to measure became the ruler we measure everything else with.

NARRATOR: And it all came from refusing to race the light, and choosing instead to let it race itself. If you want to see how this fits together with the physics of waves adding and cancelling, go back to the teaching episode on interference and the superposition of waves.
