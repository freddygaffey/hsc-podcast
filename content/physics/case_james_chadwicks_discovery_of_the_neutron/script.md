NARRATOR: This is part of Module 8, From the Universe to the Atom. And I want to start with a number that should have kept physicists awake at night. Take an atom of helium. Its charge is exactly two. But its mass is four. Take carbon. Its charge is six, but its mass is twelve. Everywhere you look, the mass of an atom is roughly double its charge. Two to one. Two to one. Two to one. The universe was practically shouting a clue at the people who studied it, and for twenty years, they got the answer wrong.

QUESTION: So what were they missing?

NARRATOR: They were missing something that had no charge at all. Something that carried mass but left no electrical fingerprint, that could pass through matter like a ghost through a wall, and that would sit undiscovered inside every atom in your body until a quiet, pipe-smoking Englishman finally cornered it in nineteen thirty-two. This is the story of how we found the neutron. And to understand why it was so hard to find, you have to understand just how wrong the smartest people in the world were about what an atom is made of.

NARRATOR: Let's go back to where we left the nucleus. Ernest Rutherford had fired alpha particles at a sheet of gold foil and discovered something staggering. Almost all of an atom's mass, and all of its positive charge, was crammed into a tiny speck at the centre. A nucleus so small that if the atom were a football stadium, the nucleus would be a grain of rice on the centre spot. But Rutherford's model left a gaping hole. He knew the nucleus was small, and he knew it was positive. He had no idea what it was actually built from.

NARRATOR: The clues started arriving. In nineteen eleven, a Dutch amateur named Antonius van den Broek suggested that an element's place in the periodic table was set by its nuclear charge, not its mass. Two years later, in nineteen thirteen, Henry Moseley proved it, using X-rays to read off each element's nuclear charge like a barcode. And then, in nineteen seventeen, Rutherford himself showed that the hydrogen nucleus turns up inside other nuclei. The hydrogen nucleus, the simplest one there is, was a building block. He would come to call it the proton.

QUESTION: So if nuclei are built from protons, problem solved, right?

NARRATOR: Not even close. Here is the trap. Atomic masses come out as whole-number multiples of the hydrogen atom's mass. So you might think, fine, a helium nucleus is just four protons stuck together. But four protons would carry a charge of four. And helium's charge is only two. Carbon's mass is twelve, so twelve protons, but its charge is six, not twelve. The masses were always about double the charge. If nuclei were nothing but protons, every element would have the same charge-to-mass ratio as hydrogen. They don't. So something inside the nucleus was adding mass without adding charge.

NARRATOR: And here the greatest minds of the age reached for the wrong answer, and they reached for it together. The idea was this. What if the nucleus is packed with extra protons, and tucked in among them are electrons, whose negative charge cancels out the surplus? An electron is nearly two thousand times lighter than a proton, so it barely adds any mass, but it subtracts a whole unit of charge. So a helium nucleus, in this picture, would be four protons and two electrons. Mass four, charge two. It fits. Everywhere you looked, wherever we would put a neutron today, they imagined a proton and an electron married together inside the nucleus.

NARRATOR: It was elegant. It used only particles they already knew about. And it was completely, spectacularly wrong. The trouble is, it took physics the better part of a decade to see why.

QUESTION: What was so wrong with electrons living in the nucleus?

NARRATOR: One by one, the cracks appeared. Consider a nucleus of nitrogen-fourteen. In the proton-electron model, you'd build it from fourteen protons and seven electrons to get a charge of seven and a mass of fourteen. Now, particles like protons and electrons carry a property called spin, and there's a rule about how spins add up. An odd number of these particles should give the whole nucleus a half-integer spin. Fourteen plus seven is twenty-one, an odd number, so nitrogen-fourteen should have half-integer spin. But in nineteen twenty-eight, physicists measured nitrogen's actual nuclear spin. It came out as a whole number. The model had failed a direct test. Something was deeply wrong with the idea of electrons in the nucleus. People began to whisper that maybe protons and electrons "do not keep their identity inside the nucleus the way they do outside it."

NARRATOR: Then quantum mechanics itself turned on the model. As physicists worked out the strange new rules of the very small, they realised, through the idea of quantum tunnelling, that you simply could not trap an electron inside the nucleus. No wall of force, no potential well, could hold it there. The nucleus was far too small a box.

NARRATOR: And that smallness leads to the killer blow, which comes straight from Heisenberg's uncertainty principle. The principle says there's a fundamental trade-off. The more precisely you pin down a particle's position, the more wildly uncertain its momentum becomes. Written out, the spread in position times the spread in momentum must be at least Planck's constant divided by two. Now, the nucleus is unimaginably tiny. So if you insist on confining an electron inside it, you've pinned its position down to an incredibly small region. And that means its momentum, and therefore its energy, has to be enormous. When physicists ran the numbers around nineteen thirty, they found that an electron trapped in the nucleus would need something like forty million electron volts of energy.

QUESTION: And why is forty million electron volts a problem?

NARRATOR: Because nobody had ever seen that energy come out. When a nucleus spits out an electron, in what we call beta decay, the electron leaves with only a few million electron volts, nowhere near forty. And the force holding the whole nucleus together, the binding energy, worked out to less than nine million electron volts for each particle in it. So the model demanded an electron carrying forty million electron volts, packed inside a bag whose glue could only muster nine. The numbers were absurd. The electron simply could not be in there. And yet, if it wasn't, what was cancelling all that charge? What was the extra mass made of? Physics was stuck.

NARRATOR: And then, as so often happens, the answer walked in wearing a disguise. In nineteen thirty, two German physicists, Walther Bothe and Herbert Becker, were playing with one of nature's most violent tools. Polonium, an intensely radioactive element, throws out fast alpha particles. Bothe and Becker aimed those alpha particles at light elements, and when they hit beryllium, something strange came flying off. A new radiation. And this radiation was a phantom. It carried no charge, so when they sent it through an electric field, it sailed straight through, undeflected, unbothered. And it was ferociously penetrating. It could plough through material that would have stopped ordinary radiation dead.

NARRATOR: Everyone's first guess was gamma rays, the most energetic form of light, which also carries no charge. But this stuff was far more penetrating than any gamma ray anyone had ever met. Then, in nineteen thirty-two, in Paris, Irène Joliot-Curie, the daughter of Marie Curie, and her husband Frédéric took the next step. They let this mystery radiation strike paraffin wax, an ordinary substance rich in hydrogen. And out of the wax came protons, fired off like billiard balls scattering from a break, some of them carrying up to five million electron volts of energy.

QUESTION: So the invisible radiation was knocking protons clean out of the wax. What could do that?

NARRATOR: The Joliot-Curies, and most everyone else, still clung to gamma rays. But here's the problem, and it's a beautiful one. Think about knocking a stationary billiard ball across a table. To send it flying, you need to hit it with something of comparable heft moving fast. A ping-pong ball, no matter how fast, barely nudges a bowling ball. Light, a gamma ray, has no mass at all. For a gamma ray to punch a proton out of that wax with five million electron volts of energy, the gamma ray itself would need a truly impossible amount of energy, far more than any nucleus could ever produce. The gamma-ray story was mathematically bankrupt. But almost nobody wanted to let go of it.

NARRATOR: Almost nobody. Because in Cambridge, at the Cavendish Laboratory, there was a man who had been waiting for this his entire career. James Chadwick had worked under Rutherford for years, and Rutherford had actually predicted, back in nineteen twenty, that a neutral particle with about the mass of a proton ought to exist. The idea had sat there, unproven, for over a decade. When Chadwick read the Paris results, he didn't see gamma rays. He saw Rutherford's neutral particle, finally leaving a trail. And by his own account, he dropped everything and worked in a fury of long days to nail it down.

NARRATOR: So how do you catch a particle you can't see, that carries no charge, that leaves no track? Chadwick's answer was elegant. If you can't see the ghost, watch what it hits. He set up polonium to rain alpha particles onto beryllium, generating the mystery radiation, just as the others had. Then he sent that radiation into different target materials and precisely measured the particles that came flying out the other side.

NARRATOR: And his observations dismantled the gamma-ray theory piece by piece. First, the sheer number of particles coming out was far too high. Gamma rays are so penetrating precisely because they interact so rarely, so weakly. This radiation was interacting far too often to be light. Second, if it really were high-energy gamma rays, it should have triggered the photoelectric effect, kicking electrons loose from atoms. It didn't. Wrong signature entirely.

NARRATOR: But the masterstroke was the momentum. This is pure physics, the kind you can do on paper. Chadwick fired his radiation into hydrogen and measured how fast the protons recoiled. Then he fired it into nitrogen and measured how fast the heavier nitrogen nuclei recoiled. He measured the protons flying off with kinetic energies around five point three million electron volts. Now he applied one of the deepest laws in all of physics, the conservation of momentum. When two things collide, the total momentum before equals the total momentum after. If you know how fast each target recoils, and you assume the same invisible bullet is doing the hitting, you can solve for the mass of the bullet.

NARRATOR: If the bullet were a gamma ray, the sums demanded it carry around fifty million electron volts of energy to explain the recoils, an energy no nucleus on Earth could supply. That answer was impossible, so the premise was false. But when Chadwick instead assumed the bullet was a particle with mass, the equations fell into place. The recoils across every target, hydrogen, nitrogen, all of them, agreed on one answer. The mystery particle had a mass almost exactly equal to that of a proton. Not lighter like an electron. Not massless like light. A heavyweight, neutral, hidden partner to the proton.

NARRATOR: Chadwick had found it. Rutherford's neutron, real at last. You can write down what was happening at the heart of the experiment in a single line. An alpha particle strikes a beryllium-nine nucleus, and out comes a carbon-twelve nucleus plus one neutron. Charge balances. Mass balances. And that lonely neutron, carrying no charge, was the ghost that had been sailing through electric fields and punching protons out of wax the whole time.

QUESTION: So what did this actually fix?

NARRATOR: Everything. Remember the two-to-one puzzle, mass always about double the charge? Solved, instantly and cleanly. A helium nucleus isn't four protons and two electrons doing some quantum juggling act. It's two protons and two neutrons. Mass four, charge two. Carbon is six protons and six neutrons. Mass twelve, charge six. No electrons in the nucleus. No forty-million-electron-volt paradox. No broken spin. The nucleus was made of two kinds of building block, protons and neutrons, both with nearly the same mass, one charged and one neutral. The whole tangled, contradictory picture dissolved and left something simple and true in its place.

NARRATOR: And it did more than tidy the bookkeeping. Because the neutron has no charge, it isn't pushed away by the positive nucleus. It can drift right up to a nucleus and slip inside. Within a few short years, that single fact would let physicists split the atom, build the first nuclear reactors, and unleash both the reactor and the bomb. The ghost particle Chadwick cornered in nineteen thirty-two would reshape the entire twentieth century. In nineteen thirty-five, he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery.

NARRATOR: And it all came down to a man who refused to accept an answer that didn't add up, who trusted the momentum equations over the fashionable theory, and who understood that if you can't see the ghost, you watch what it knocks over.
