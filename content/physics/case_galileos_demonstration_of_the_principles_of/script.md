NARRATOR: This is part of Module Five, Advanced Mechanics. And I want to start with a bet, one that almost everybody loses. Imagine you're standing on a flat, open field holding a rifle, perfectly level, pointing straight out at the horizon. In your other hand you hold a single bullet, identical to the one in the chamber. At the exact same instant, you fire the gun and you let the bullet in your hand simply drop from the same height. The fired bullet screams away across the field, hundreds of metres, maybe a kilometre before it lands. The dropped bullet just falls at your feet. Here's the question. Which one hits the ground first?

QUESTION: The fast one has so much further to travel, so surely it stays in the air longer, right?

NARRATOR: That is what almost everyone says. And it is wrong. They hit the ground at exactly the same moment. The bullet travelling three hundred metres a second and the bullet doing nothing but falling touch down together, in perfect unison. If you find that hard to believe, you are in excellent company, because for nearly two thousand years the greatest minds in the world believed something that made this result flatly impossible. And the man who proved them wrong did it with nothing more than a table, a ball, and a way of thinking that nobody had ever tried before.

NARRATOR: To understand why this was such a shock, you have to go back to the world before it, the world of Aristotle. For roughly two millennia, the ruling idea about motion came from the ancient Greeks, and it was beautifully intuitive and completely mistaken. Aristotle taught that an object could really only do one thing at a time. A thrown stone, in his picture, moved forward because something was pushing it forward, and it kept going in a straight line until that push, that violent motion as he called it, got used up and exhausted. Only then did the stone's natural motion take over and pull it straight down to the earth, its proper home. Motion was a sequence. First you go along, then you fall down. One thing, and then the other thing.

NARRATOR: You can actually see this idea drawn in old military manuals. Gunners in the fifteen hundreds sketched the path of a cannonball as a straight line shooting up out of the barrel, then a little curve at the top, and then a straight line plunging vertically to the ground. Up, over, down. Three separate phases. It looks almost childish to us now, but it was the serious science of the age, and men aimed real cannons by it.

QUESTION: So what was actually wrong with that picture?

NARRATOR: The deep error was the assumption that the horizontal motion and the vertical motion were fighting for control of the object, taking turns, that one had to finish before the other could begin. And this is where Galileo Galilei enters, in the first decade of the sixteen hundreds, in northern Italy. Galileo had a genuinely radical suspicion. What if a moving object is doing two completely separate things at once? What if the sideways motion and the falling motion don't interfere with each other at all, don't take turns, don't compete, but simply happen together, in parallel, each one utterly blind to the other?

NARRATOR: Think about how strange that idea is. It means that the instant a cannonball leaves the barrel, it begins falling toward the earth at the very same rate it would if you had just let it drop from your fingers. Its blistering horizontal speed does nothing, absolutely nothing, to hold it up. Gravity doesn't wait its turn. Gravity starts pulling immediately and pulls just as hard whether the ball is streaking sideways or hanging still. If that were true, then the fired bullet and the dropped bullet really would hit the ground together, because both are falling in exactly the same way, and the only difference between them, the horizontal speed, has no say in how fast they fall.

NARRATOR: Now, it is one thing to have a beautiful idea. It is another thing entirely to prove it, and in Galileo's time proving it was genuinely hard. A falling object moves far too fast for the human eye to track. There were no high speed cameras, no electronic timers, no way to freeze a cannonball in flight and measure it. Galileo needed to catch motion in the act, and to do that he did something clever. He slowed the whole problem down and turned it sideways.

NARRATOR: Here is the experiment, and it is elegant enough that you could run it on a kitchen table this afternoon. You take a smooth ball and you roll it along a flat surface, a table or a horizontal plank, and you let it run off the edge. From the moment it leaves the edge, it is a projectile. It is falling and moving sideways at the same time, and it traces a graceful arc down to the floor. Now the genius is in what you change and what you refuse to change. The one thing you keep absolutely fixed is the height of the table. Every single trial, the ball leaves the edge from exactly the same height above the floor. That is the controlled variable, and it matters enormously, as we'll see.

NARRATOR: What you deliberately vary is the speed of the ball as it leaves the edge. Some trials you roll it gently, so it just dribbles off. Other trials you roll it hard, so it launches off fast. That launch speed, the horizontal velocity, is your independent variable, the thing you are changing on purpose to see what it does.

QUESTION: But how do you measure how fast the ball is going if you can't see it clearly?

NARRATOR: This is the practical heart of it. You can't easily read the ball's speed straight off, so instead you time it. You mark out a fixed run along the surface before the edge, and you time how long the ball takes to cover that known distance. Speed is just distance divided by time, so distance over time gives you the horizontal velocity at launch. And there is real craft in choosing that run length. If the run is too short, then your reaction time on the stopwatch, that little human fumble of a fraction of a second, becomes a huge slice of a tiny measurement, and your percentage error balloons. But if the run is too long, friction starts to bite. The ball is dragging along the surface the whole way, quietly slowing down, so the speed it actually leaves the edge with is lower than the average speed you calculated over the run, and you end up overestimating how fast it launched. So you want a run that is comfortably long, but not so long that friction corrupts it. That balance, that judgement, is exactly the kind of experimental thinking the whole exercise rewards.

NARRATOR: And then for each launch you measure one more thing, the range. The range is how far out from the edge the ball lands, measured horizontally along the floor from the point directly below where it left the table to the point where it actually hits. So now, trial after trial, you build a table of numbers. On one side, the launch speed. On the other side, the range. And then you do the thing that turns a pile of measurements into a discovery. You plot them on a graph. Launch speed along the bottom, range up the side. Point after point after point.

QUESTION: So what shape do those points make?

NARRATOR: They fall on a straight line. A clean, straight line rising from the origin. Double the launch speed, and the ball lands twice as far out. Triple it, three times as far. The range is simply proportional to the horizontal launch velocity. And that straight line, that humble straight line, is the whole secret of projectile motion sitting right there in front of you. Because think about what a straight line through the origin means. It means the range equals some fixed number multiplied by the launch speed. Range is speed times a constant. And the question is, what on earth is that constant, that gradient, the steepness of the line?

NARRATOR: Here is where it all clicks together. Look at just the horizontal motion of the ball once it's in the air. Nothing is pushing it sideways and, in the ideal case, nothing is slowing it sideways either. So horizontally it just cruises at a steady speed. And for steady speed, distance is simply speed multiplied by time. The horizontal distance, the range, equals the launch speed multiplied by the time the ball spends in the air. Now put that next to what the graph told us. The graph says range equals the gradient multiplied by the launch speed. The physics says range equals the time of flight multiplied by the launch speed. Line those two up, side by side, and there is only one conclusion. The gradient of your graph is the time of flight. The steepness of that line literally is the number of seconds the ball spends falling.

NARRATOR: And now comes the moment the whole experiment was built for. That gradient is a constant. It's a single fixed number, the same for the slow rolls and the fast rolls, the same for every point on that straight line. Which means the time of flight is the same for every ball, no matter how fast it was launched sideways. The ball you barely nudged off the edge and the ball you fired off at speed, both of them spend precisely the same amount of time in the air before they hit the floor. The horizontal velocity, the one thing you were changing, has zero effect on how long the fall takes. It is written right there in the flatness, the constancy, of that gradient.

NARRATOR: And that is the proof. That is Aristotle overturned on a kitchen table. The time to fall depends only on the vertical part of the motion, on gravity and on the height you dropped from, and it is completely deaf to whatever the ball is doing horizontally. The two motions are independent. They don't take turns. They don't compete. They run alongside each other, each one blind to the other, exactly as Galileo suspected.

NARRATOR: You can even go one step further and pin down that time from the vertical side alone. The ball falls from rest in the vertical direction, and it falls under gravity, speeding up as it goes. From the standard relationship for falling from rest, the distance fallen is one half the acceleration multiplied by the time squared. Rearrange that for the height of the table, and the time of the fall comes out as the square root of two times the height divided by the acceleration due to gravity, that steady pull of about nine point eight metres per second squared here on Earth. Notice what is and isn't in that formula. There is the height, and there is gravity, and that is all. The launch speed appears nowhere. The fall time is decided entirely by how high the table was, which is exactly why we were so careful to keep that height fixed for every single trial.

QUESTION: So where does the exam actually test all of this?

NARRATOR: In a really neat way. Picture a question that hands you a graph of range against launch velocity for a ball rolled off some ledge, and then asks you to find the height of that ledge. At first it looks like there isn't enough information. But you now know the trick cold. The gradient of that line is the time of flight. Read the gradient off the graph, and you've got the fall time. Feed that time into the falling from rest relationship, and out drops the height of the ledge. A graph about horizontal speed quietly tells you a vertical distance, because the independence of the two motions is the hidden bridge between them.

NARRATOR: Now, one honest note, because good physics is honest about its limits. In the real world, if you kept cranking up the launch speed, that straight line would eventually start to bend and sag. At high speeds air resistance grows, dragging on the ball, shortening its range, and the relationship stops being perfectly linear. In the HSC we deliberately ignore air resistance, so we live in the clean world of the straight line, but it's worth knowing that the tidy line is an idealisation, and that reality gently curves away from it once things move fast enough.

NARRATOR: Step back, though, and look at what Galileo actually handed us. Before him, a projectile was one messy, tangled thing, and predicting where a cannonball would land was closer to art than science. After him, a projectile became two simple problems living inside one arc. A horizontal problem, where the object just cruises at constant speed. And a vertical problem, where it falls under gravity exactly as if it had been dropped. Two separate, simple stories, and because they are independent, you are allowed to solve them one at a time and then stitch the answers back together. Every projectile question you will ever meet, a ball, an arrow, a stunt car sailing off a ramp, a spacecraft arcing over the horizon, every one of them cracks open along that seam Galileo found. Split it into a horizontal part and a vertical part, handle each on its own, and the impossibly complicated becomes almost easy.

NARRATOR: That is the real payoff of a ball rolling off a table. Not just that it settled a two thousand year old argument, though it did. It handed physics a method, a way of taking something complex and cutting it cleanly into pieces that don't interfere with each other. And it means that the very first thing you should reach for, every time you see something flying through the air, is to break it apart, horizontal and vertical, and treat the two as the independent motions Galileo proved them to be.
