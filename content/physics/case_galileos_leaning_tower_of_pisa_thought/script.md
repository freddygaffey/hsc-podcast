NARRATOR: This is part of Module Five, Advanced Mechanics. Picture yourself standing at the top of a tower, an iron cannonball in one hand, a small wooden ball in the other. You let them both go at exactly the same instant. Which one hits the ground first? Almost everyone, when you ask them cold, says the heavy one. It just feels obvious. The heavy thing should win the race to the earth. And for nearly two thousand years, that answer was not just common sense. It was official, textbook, unquestioned science.

QUESTION: So why is the obvious answer wrong?

NARRATOR: To understand how wrong, we have to go back to Aristotle, the Greek philosopher who lived more than three centuries before Christ. Aristotle taught that the rate at which objects fall is proportional to their weight. Twice the weight, twice the speed. And here is the strange part. Nobody really tested it. It sat in the books for close to two thousand years as simple, unchallenged truth. Partly that is because the world seems to agree with him. Drop a feather and a stone, and the stone lands first every time. It looks like heavier really does mean faster.

NARRATOR: But that is a trick, and the trick is the air. The feather is not being slow because it is light. It is being slowed by air resistance, catching the air like a tiny sail. Take the air away and the feather and the stone fall together, side by side. Aristotle had mistaken the effect of the air for a law of gravity, and an entire civilisation had taken his word for it.

NARRATOR: Now, by the time our story begins, a few brave Italians had already claimed to have shown Aristotle was wrong. But the idea would not die, because people could not shake their gut feeling. Why on earth, they asked, would a ball of iron and a ball of wood fall at exactly the same speed? It offends the intuition. And this is where a young man in his twenties, teaching mathematics at the University of Pisa, decided to settle it. His name was Galileo Galilei.

NARRATOR: And before Galileo ever climbed a single step of any tower, he did something more powerful. He beat Aristotle with pure thought. In his early book, On Motion, he laid a trap made entirely of logic. Suppose, he said, that Aristotle is right and heavier objects really do fall faster. Now take a heavy ball and a light ball, and tie them together with a loose string.

QUESTION: What happens when you drop them tied together?

NARRATOR: Follow the logic and it eats itself alive. The light ball, being slower, should lag behind and pull back on the heavy ball through the string, dragging the pair down more gently than the heavy ball alone. So the combination should fall slower than the heavy ball by itself. But wait. Tie the two balls together and you have made a single object that is heavier than either one. And a heavier object, Aristotle insists, must fall faster. So the same system must fall both slower and faster than the heavy ball at once. It cannot do both. The idea contradicts itself. And when an assumption forces you into an impossibility, the assumption has to be false. In a few sentences, with no equipment at all, Galileo had shown that falling speed simply cannot depend on weight.

NARRATOR: That thought experiment is beautiful, but a hostile world of professors was never going to surrender to logic alone. So the story goes that Galileo took the argument to the most dramatic stage he could find. The Leaning Tower of Pisa. He is said to have carried up unequal weights of the same material, held them out over that famous tilting edge, and let them fall together, timing how long each took to strike the ground far below.

QUESTION: And the result?

NARRATOR: They landed together. The heavy weight and the light weight, more or less, hit the ground at the very same moment. Not proportional to their weight. Not a race. A tie. The time it took to fall did not care how heavy the object was, and since the time was the same, the rate of falling had to be the same too. Weight, it turned out, has nothing to do with how fast you fall.

NARRATOR: Now, I have to be honest with you, because the history here is genuinely uncertain. Galileo himself never wrote down that this drop actually happened. The tower story comes to us from one of his students, a man named Viviani, who told it decades later in a biography, his Life of Galileo. So historians still argue about whether Galileo really climbed that tower, or whether the perfect stage was added afterwards to make the legend shine. But whether the balls really fell from Pisa or only fell in the imagination, the physics stands. The conclusion is right either way.

NARRATOR: And Galileo, being a careful thinker, would have known the objections. What about the air? He specified objects of the same material, which means the heavier one has to be larger, more surface area, more air to push through. Does that not slow it down? A little, in principle. But choose dense, smooth objects, spherical wooden balls or lead cannonballs, and the air barely gets a say. For a heavy, compact ball the drag is a tiny fraction of its weight, so the fall is almost perfectly clean. And there is a deeper symmetry hiding here. The heavier ball feels more air resistance, yes, but it also feels a far stronger pull of gravity, and the two effects very nearly cancel. The heavy and the light fall together anyway.

NARRATOR: So sit with what Galileo had really found. Strip away the air, and every object, no matter its mass, falls with exactly the same acceleration. A cannonball and a pebble, a mountain and a marble, all quickening toward the earth at the identical rate. This is the seed of everything that comes next in mechanics. It is the reason a thrown ball and a dropped ball follow the same arc of motion, the foundation of projectile motion, where the vertical acceleration is treated as constant and equal for every mass.

NARRATOR: And it did not stop there. Decades later, Isaac Newton would take this stubborn little fact and explain exactly why it must be true, weaving it into his law of universal gravitation and his second law of motion, force equals mass times acceleration. But it started here, with a young mathematician, a leaning tower, and the courage to distrust two thousand years of common sense.
