NARRATOR: This is part of Module Eight, From the Universe to the Atom. And it starts with one of the strangest predictions ever made in physics. Imagine a group of theorists, sitting with pencils and paper, who announce to the world that three particles exist which nobody has ever seen. They don't just say the particles are out there somewhere. They tell you almost exactly how heavy each one will be, before a single experiment has found a trace of them. Years later, engineers build one of the largest machines on Earth, switch it on, and there the particles are, sitting at very nearly the predicted weight. It sounds like cheating. How could anyone know the mass of a particle that had never been detected?

NARRATOR: To understand why that prediction was so bold, you have to go back to a puzzle that had bothered physicists for decades. There are four fundamental forces in nature. Gravity, which holds you to the ground. Electromagnetism, which lights your screen and binds atoms together. And then two forces that only show themselves deep inside the nucleus. One is the strong force, the glue of the nucleus. The other is the odd one out. The weak force.

QUESTION: Why call a force weak?

NARRATOR: Because compared with electromagnetism, it barely reaches out at all. The weak force is the force behind a certain kind of radioactive decay, the process where one type of particle inside the nucleus quietly transforms into another and spits out a tiny fast particle. It is the reason the Sun burns slowly instead of exploding all at once. But it has two features that made no sense together. First, it is feeble. Second, its reach is astonishingly short, far smaller than the width of a single proton. Every other force physicists understood at the time seemed to reach out across all of space. This one seemed to switch off almost the instant it switched on.

NARRATOR: Now here is the thing about forces in modern physics. A force is not some invisible influence humming across empty space. Forces are carried. When two particles push or pull on each other, they do it by tossing a messenger particle back and forth between them. Electromagnetism is carried by the photon, the particle of light. And that single fact explains why electromagnetism reaches so far. The photon has no mass. A massless messenger can travel forever, so the force it carries never truly runs out.

QUESTION: So what carries the weak force?

NARRATOR: That was the whole mystery. In the nineteen seventies, three theorists, Sheldon Glashow, Abdus Salam, and Steven Weinberg, working on the deepest kind of theory, did something audacious. They argued that electromagnetism and the weak force are not really two separate forces at all. At everyday energies they look completely different, one long-ranged and familiar, the other feeble and hidden. But wind the conditions up to enormous energies, the kind that filled the universe in its first fraction of a second, and the two forces merge into a single unified force. We call this the electroweak theory, and it stands as one of the great unifications in the history of science, sitting right alongside the moment when electricity and magnetism were shown to be two faces of the same thing.

NARRATOR: The mathematics behind that unification is a long way beyond anything you will ever be asked in an exam, so don't worry about how they built it. What matters is what fell out of it. The theory insisted that the weak force must be carried by messenger particles, just as electromagnetism is carried by the photon. It predicted three of them. Two of them carry electric charge, a positive one and a negative one, and physicists labelled them the W particles. The third is electrically neutral, and they called it the Z particle.

QUESTION: And why were those messengers so heavy?

NARRATOR: This is the beautiful part. Remember that the weak force has an incredibly short reach. In this picture, the reach of a force depends on the mass of its messenger. A massless messenger, like the photon, gives you a force that stretches across the cosmos. A heavy messenger can only carry the force a tiny distance before it has to vanish again, which strangles the force down to the width of a nucleus. So the very shortness of the weak force was telling physicists something precise. Its messengers had to be enormously heavy. Not a little heavy. Monstrously heavy, close to a hundred times the mass of a proton. The theory did not just say the particles were heavy in a vague way. When you fed in what was already measured about the weak force, the equations spat out actual numbers. The W particles should weigh in at around eighty giga electron volts over c squared. The Z particle, a touch heavier, at around ninety giga electron volts over c squared. Now they had a target, and everyone knew exactly where to aim.

NARRATOR: But there was still a problem lurking underneath, and it is worth naming because it comes back at the end of this story. In the simplest, most elegant version of the theory, every one of these messenger particles should have been born massless, like the photon. The equations were most beautiful when nothing had any mass at all. Yet the W and the Z clearly had to be crushingly heavy. So where did their mass come from? The answer the theorists reached for was a new idea, worked out in the nineteen sixties, that space itself is filled with an invisible field. Particles moving through that field get dragged, and that drag is what we experience as mass. And if that field were real, it too should have its own particle. A final missing piece. Physicists called it the Higgs.

NARRATOR: So by the late nineteen seventies the theory had made its bets and laid its numbers on the table. Three heavy messengers at very specific masses, plus one more particle behind the curtain giving them that mass. All that was left was to go and find them. And that turned out to be extraordinarily hard, because to make a particle a hundred times heavier than a proton, you need to concentrate a hundred protons' worth of raw energy into a single point smaller than an atom.

QUESTION: How do you build a collision that violent?

NARRATOR: The answer came at Cern, the great European physics laboratory buried beneath the border of France and Switzerland. There, physicists took an existing machine, a ring several kilometres around that accelerated protons, and reimagined what it could do. The bold idea was to fire protons one way around the ring and antimatter versions of protons the other way, then steer the two streams into a head-on smash. When matter and antimatter collide, they annihilate completely, turning their entire being into pure energy, and that concentrated flash of energy is exactly what you need to conjure a heavy particle briefly into existence.

NARRATOR: The engineering was ferocious. Antimatter does not lie around waiting to be used. It had to be manufactured, a tiny amount at a time, then gathered, tamed, and packed into a tight beam, a feat so difficult it earned its own share of the eventual prize. Enormous detectors, machines the size of a house wrapped around the collision point, watched the debris of each smash, sifting through the spray for the faint, telltale fingerprints a W or a Z would leave as it decayed almost instantly into other particles.

NARRATOR: In nineteen eighty-three, after years of work, the announcement came. The W particles had been found. Then, within months, the Z as well. And when the teams weighed them, measuring their mass from the energy of the fragments they left behind, the numbers landed almost exactly where the theory had promised years earlier. The W particles sat at close to eighty giga electron volts over c squared. The Z particle sat at close to ninety giga electron volts over c squared. Predictions made with pencil and paper, confirmed by one of the most complex experiments ever attempted, to within a whisker.

NARRATOR: Think about what that means. Nobody had stumbled onto these particles by luck. The theory had described in advance objects nobody had ever seen, told the experimenters what mass to look for, and been proven right. That is science working at its absolute sharpest, and it is why the leaders of that experiment were awarded a Nobel Prize the very next year.

NARRATOR: The payoff reached far beyond three new particles on a list. Confirming the W and the Z confirmed the whole idea of electroweak unification. It said that electromagnetism and the weak force really are one force in disguise, splitting apart only as the universe cooled. And that gave physicists a window into the earliest moments of everything. In the first blink after the Big Bang, when the universe was hotter than any furnace, energies were so vast that the electroweak force was whole and undivided. As the cosmos cooled past a threshold, around one hundred giga electron volts of energy, that symmetry broke, and the single force fractured into the two we see today. The confirmation of these particles was, in a real sense, evidence about how the young universe changed as it cooled.

NARRATOR: And the last piece? The invisible field, and its particle, the Higgs, that gave the W and the Z their mass in the first place. That one stayed hidden far longer. It took a machine even larger, and nearly three more decades of searching, before physicists finally cornered it in twenty twelve, right where the theory said it should be. The final brick in a structure whose blueprint had been drawn, correctly, long before anyone could see the building.
