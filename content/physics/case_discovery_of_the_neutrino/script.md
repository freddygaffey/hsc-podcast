NARRATOR: This is part of Module Eight, From the Universe to the Atom. And I want to start with a crime scene. Not a murder, exactly. But a robbery. Energy is going missing. In the nineteen twenties, physicists watched a particular kind of radioactive decay, and every single time they measured it, some of the energy that went in simply did not come out. It vanished. Now, in physics, there is one rule you do not get to break. Energy is conserved. It is not created, it is not destroyed, it only changes form. Add up what you start with, add up what you end with, and the two numbers must match. Always. So when they didn't match, one of the greatest physicists alive was faced with a genuinely terrifying choice. Either the most sacred law in all of science was wrong. Or there was a ghost in the room. An invisible particle carrying the missing energy away, a particle so faint, so untouchable, that catching it might be impossible forever.

QUESTION: So how do you catch a particle that barely exists?

NARRATOR: To understand why this was such a nightmare, you have to understand what physicists expected to see, and why they were so sure of it. Let's talk about a different kind of decay first, one that behaved perfectly. Alpha decay. When a heavy nucleus spits out an alpha particle, the bookkeeping is beautifully simple. The energy of the reactants equals the energy of the products, plus the kinetic energy carried off by that alpha particle. And here is the key word. That kinetic energy is monokinetic. Mono, meaning one. Every alpha particle from a given decay comes flying out with exactly the same energy as every other one. Do the decay a thousand times, ten thousand times, and you get the same value, over and over. It makes perfect sense. The difference in energy between the start and the end is a fixed number, so the leftover, the kinetic energy, has to be a fixed number too. Clean. Predictable. Reassuring.

NARRATOR: So naturally, when physicists turned to beta decay, they expected the same story. Beta decay is when a nucleus emits a beta particle, a fast electron. And by the exact same logic, that electron should come out monokinetic. Same energy, every single time. The difference between reactants and products is fixed, so the kinetic energy of the beta particle has to be fixed. It was obvious. It was airtight. And it was completely wrong.

QUESTION: So what did they actually measure?

NARRATOR: When they measured the energy of beta particles, they did not get one value. They got a smear. A whole spectrum. Some electrons came out with a lot of energy. Some came out with almost none. And everything in between. Picture expecting every runner in a race to cross the line at exactly the same speed, and instead they arrive strung out across the whole track, from a sprint to a crawl. That is what beta decay looked like. And this was a catastrophe. Because if the electron only sometimes carries the full energy, and often carries less, then where is the rest of it going? The reactants had a fixed amount of energy. The products, at least the ones they could see, kept coming up short. Energy was disappearing. Some of the greatest minds of the era genuinely wondered whether the law of conservation of energy just did not hold inside the atomic nucleus. That is how deep the crisis went. They were ready to tear down a pillar of physics.

NARRATOR: Enter Wolfgang Pauli. Nineteen thirty. Pauli refused to give up on conservation of energy. He would rather invent a particle nobody had ever seen than let the great law fall. So in a letter, half apologetic, he proposed what he called a desperate remedy. What if, alongside the electron, beta decay produces a second particle? A particle that is electrically neutral, so it leaves no track, and carries almost no mass, so it is nearly weightless. This ghost particle shares the energy with the electron. Sometimes it takes a little, sometimes it takes a lot, and that is exactly why the electron's energy is smeared across a spectrum. Add the hidden particle's energy back in, and the books balance perfectly. Energy is conserved after all. And it wasn't just energy. The same invisible particle was needed to conserve momentum, and to conserve angular momentum, the spin of the system. Three separate accounting problems, all fixed by one ghost. Later, Enrico Fermi gave it a name that stuck. The neutrino. The little neutral one.

QUESTION: A particle that solves everything and can't be detected. Isn't that a bit convenient?

NARRATOR: That was exactly the problem. On paper, the neutrino was a masterstroke. In the laboratory, it was a monster. Because the very thing that made it so good at hiding, the fact that it is neutral and almost massless, also made it almost impossible to catch. Neutrinos barely interact with anything at all. They stream through matter as if it weren't there. Right now, as you listen to this, trillions of neutrinos from the Sun are passing through your body every second, and not one of them touches you. You could send a neutrino through a wall of solid lead many light years thick, and it would very likely sail straight out the other side. Pauli himself felt guilty. He is said to have admitted that he had done a terrible thing, he had invented a particle that could never be observed. For twenty-five years, the neutrino was exactly that. A brilliant idea that saved physics, and a ghost that could never be proven real.

NARRATOR: Until two men decided to try anyway. In the nineteen fifties, Clyde Cowan and Frederick Reines had a wild idea. If a single neutrino almost never interacts, then you don't hunt one. You flood yourself with an unimaginable number of them, and you wait for the rare, once in a blue moon collision. And where do you find the most intense man made river of neutrinos on Earth? A nuclear reactor. A reactor's core is a furnace of beta decays, pouring out neutrinos, or to be precise antineutrinos, by the countless trillions every second. Cowan and Reines set up their detector right next to one.

QUESTION: But if you can't see a neutrino, what exactly are you detecting?

NARRATOR: This is the clever part. You don't detect the neutrino. You detect the wreckage it leaves behind. They were looking for a process called inverse beta decay. Here is what happens. An antineutrino, on the incredibly rare occasion that it does interact, slams into a proton. And in that collision, the proton is transformed into a neutron, and a positron is thrown out. A positron is the antimatter twin of the electron, an electron with a positive charge instead of a negative one. And that antineutrino was there for a reason. Remember, conservation of energy demanded a hidden particle in beta decay in the first place. This experiment was designed around that very particle, sending it back in to trigger the reverse reaction. So now the question becomes, how do you know that a proton just turned into a neutron and a positron somewhere inside a tank of liquid?

NARRATOR: The signature is gorgeous. That positron, born in the collision, doesn't last long. Almost immediately it meets an ordinary electron, its exact opposite. And when matter meets antimatter, they annihilate. Both particles vanish, and their mass is converted entirely into energy, into two gamma ray photons flying apart in opposite directions. Two gamma rays, produced at the same instant, with a precise energy. That is a fingerprint. That is something a detector can actually catch. So Cowan and Reines built enormous tanks that would flash with light whenever gamma rays passed through, and they watched, and they waited, for that unmistakable double flash. The telltale sign that, deep in the liquid, a ghost had finally touched something real.

NARRATOR: And in nineteen fifty six, it happened. Again, and again. The flashes came exactly as predicted, at exactly the rate the theory said they should, and they got quieter when the reactor was turned off and louder when it was turned on. The neutrino was real. Twenty six years after Pauli scribbled his desperate remedy, his ghost had been caught. Cowan and Reines sent Pauli a telegram to tell him the particle he thought could never be seen had been detected at last. And decades later, Reines was awarded the Nobel Prize for it.

NARRATOR: But step back and see what really just happened here. Faced with energy that appeared to vanish, physicists had two choices. Abandon conservation of energy, one of the deepest truths we have. Or trust the law so completely that they were willing to predict an invisible particle, purely because the equations demanded it, and then go and find it. They chose the law. And the law was right. That is the astonishing thing about the neutrino. It was not stumbled upon. It was foretold, by nothing more than the stubborn insistence that the energy has to add up. The universe was hiding a particle, and conservation of energy pointed straight at its hiding place.
