NARRATOR: This is part of Module Seven, The Nature of Light. And I want to start with a prediction so absurd that the man who made it was certain it would end an argument forever. He thought he had found the one result that could not possibly be true. He was right that it was absurd. He was wrong about everything else.

NARRATOR: Picture a small, perfectly round ball. You shine a bright light at it, and behind it, on a screen, it casts a shadow. A dark circle. Simple. Everyone knows what a shadow looks like. Now here is the claim: right in the very centre of that shadow, in the darkest part, where absolutely no light should reach, there should be a bright spot. A pinprick of light sitting in the middle of the darkness, as bright as if the ball were not even there.

QUESTION: A bright spot in the centre of a shadow? That has to be nonsense, doesn’t it?

NARRATOR: That is exactly what one of the finest mathematicians in France thought. He worked it out on paper, and he presented it as the killing blow, the proof that a certain new theory of light was ridiculous. And then someone went into a laboratory and looked. To understand why that moment mattered so much, we have to go back to a fight that had been running for more than a hundred years.

NARRATOR: For most of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, if you wanted to know what light was, there were two answers, and they could not both be right. On one side stood Isaac Newton. Newton said light was made of tiny particles, little corpuscles, streaming out from a source like bullets from a gun. It was a beautiful idea. It explained why light travels in dead straight lines. It explained sharp shadows. It explained why light bounces off a mirror the way a ball bounces off a wall. And crucially, it had Newton’s name attached to it, and by then Newton was not just a scientist, he was practically a monument. To doubt Newton felt close to bad manners.

NARRATOR: On the other side stood a Dutchman, Christiaan Huygens. Huygens said no, light is not a stream of particles at all. Light is a wave, a disturbance spreading out through space the way ripples spread across a pond. Every point on a wavefront, he said, acts as a tiny new source, sending out its own little wavelet, and all those wavelets add together to make the wave move forward. It was elegant. But it had a problem, and the problem was Newton’s strongest card.

QUESTION: If light is a wave, why does it travel in straight lines and cast sharp shadows at all?

NARRATOR: Waves bend around obstacles. Sound is a wave, and you can hear someone talking around a corner even when you cannot see them. Water waves curl around a rock. So if light were a wave, why did it not obviously bend around corners? Why did shadows have crisp edges instead of fuzzy, smeared ones? For a century, that objection sat there, and Newton’s particles seemed to win. The wave theory was a curiosity, quietly ignored.

NARRATOR: Then, in the early eighteen hundreds, a young French engineer changed everything. His name was Augustin-Jean Fresnel. Fresnel was not even a full-time physicist. He built roads for the French government. But in his spare time he became obsessed with light, and he did something Huygens had not fully done. He took the wave idea and gave it real mathematics, and he added the crucial ingredient: interference. Where two waves meet crest to crest, they add up and get brighter. Where a crest meets a trough, they cancel out and you get darkness. Light plus light, Fresnel said, can make dark.

NARRATOR: With that one idea, the old objection melts away. Light does bend around edges. That bending is called diffraction. But the wavelength of light is astonishingly small, less than a thousandth of a millimetre, so the bending is tiny. Shadows look sharp not because light travels in perfect lines, but because the waves are so short that the fuzziness at the edge is too fine for the eye to notice. It is there. You just have to look closely enough.

NARRATOR: In eighteen eighteen, the French Academy of Sciences announced a competition. They set diffraction as the prize topic, fully expecting the winning entry to confirm Newton’s particles. Fresnel entered with a long, dense paper laying out his wave theory of diffraction in full mathematical detail. And the judges who received it were, almost to a man, committed Newtonians. Among them was a brilliant, coldly logical mathematician named Siméon Denis Poisson.

NARRATOR: Poisson did not just dislike the wave theory. He decided to destroy it using its own weapons. He took Fresnel’s equations, the very mathematics Fresnel had submitted, and he pushed them to an extreme case to expose an absurdity. Suppose, Poisson said, you put a small round obstacle in a beam of light, a disc, a ball bearing. Let us follow Fresnel’s own maths and ask what happens in the shadow directly behind it.

QUESTION: So what did Fresnel’s equations actually predict for the centre of that shadow?

NARRATOR: Here is the reasoning, and it is beautiful even though Poisson meant it as a trap. The edge of a perfectly round ball is a circle. Every single point on that circular edge is exactly the same distance from the point at the very centre of the shadow behind it. By Huygens’ principle, every point on that edge sends out its own little wavelet. Because they all travel the same distance to reach the centre, they all arrive perfectly in step, crest lined up with crest. And waves that arrive in step add together. So at that one special point, dead centre in the darkest part of the shadow, all those edge wavelets reinforce each other and produce a bright spot.

NARRATOR: Poisson was delighted. This, he declared, was obviously ridiculous. A bright spot in the middle of a shadow contradicts all common sense. Since Fresnel’s theory led to such an impossible conclusion, the theory had to be wrong, and the wave model of light with it. He had, he thought, won the argument by turning Fresnel’s own equations into a joke.

NARRATOR: But there was one more judge on that committee, and his name was François Arago. And Arago was not content to argue on paper. He said, in effect, let us not just declare it impossible. Let us do the experiment. Let us actually set up a small round object in a beam of light and look at the centre of its shadow.

NARRATOR: So he did. He took a small metal disc, mounted it in the path of a beam of light, darkened the room, and examined the shadow it cast on a screen behind. And there, in the exact centre of the shadow, in the place Poisson had sworn must be pitch black, was a tiny, unmistakable point of light. The bright spot was real. It was exactly where the wave theory said it would be. Poisson’s impossible prediction had come true.

QUESTION: How could the light get to a spot that the ball was completely blocking?

NARRATOR: It got there by bending around the edge. The waves diffracted around the entire rim of the ball, curled inward into the shadow, and met at the centre all in step, adding together through constructive interference into a bright point. The very fact that had seemed absurd, light appearing where an object should block it, was the wave nature of light showing itself in the open. Diffraction and interference, the two signatures of a wave, were on display in a single dot of light.

NARRATOR: And this is the delicious twist of the whole story. The result is called Poisson’s bright spot, named after the man who predicted it in order to prove it could never happen. He designed the argument as a weapon against the wave theory, and instead he handed it the single most dramatic piece of evidence in its favour. His attack became its monument. Some people fairly call it Arago’s spot, after the man who actually had the nerve to look.

NARRATOR: Fresnel won the prize. And more than that, the tide of the whole century-long argument turned. A bright spot at the heart of a shadow is not something Newton’s little particles can explain. Particles fired at a ball are blocked by the ball. Full stop. But waves bend, waves overlap, waves add and cancel. Only a wave can reach into the dark and light it up. This was strong, early, physical evidence that Huygens had been right all along, and that light, at least in this experiment, behaves as a wave.

NARRATOR: The wave model would go on to dominate physics for the rest of the century, and it opened the door to understanding light as an electromagnetic wave, and eventually to the strange twentieth-century discovery that light is somehow both wave and particle at once. But it started here, with a shadow, a ball bearing, and a spot of light that should not exist, predicted by a man who was certain he was proving the opposite.

NARRATOR: The lesson is one physics keeps teaching. Sometimes the prediction that sounds most impossible is the one nature was waiting to confirm. You just have to be brave enough to set up the experiment and look.
