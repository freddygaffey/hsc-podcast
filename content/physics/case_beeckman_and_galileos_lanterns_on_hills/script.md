NARRATOR: This is part of Module Seven, The Nature of Light. And I want to start with a question that sounds almost too simple to be interesting. When you flip a light switch on the far side of a dark room, and the light reaches your eyes, how long did that take? Instantly? Or did it take some tiny sliver of time to cross the room?

NARRATOR: For most of human history, the greatest minds on Earth were completely certain of the answer. And they were completely wrong.

QUESTION: So what did they believe?

NARRATOR: They believed light was instantaneous. That it took no time at all. Aristotle taught it. Kepler taught it. Descartes, one of the sharpest thinkers who ever lived, staked his reputation on it. To them, light wasn't something that travelled, like a thrown stone or a fired arrow. Light was more like a fact about the world. You open your eyes, and the whole of creation is simply there, all at once, in the same instant. The idea that a sunbeam might be running late, that the light hitting your face right now left its source a moment ago, would have struck them as absurd.

NARRATOR: But not everyone was convinced. In the early sixteen hundreds, an old man in Italy, half blind and under house arrest, refused to just take it on faith. His name was Galileo Galilei. And Galileo had a rule he lived by. Don't argue about nature. Ask her. So he asked a dangerous question. What if light isn't instant? What if it's just so fast that nobody has ever managed to catch it in the act?

QUESTION: And how do you catch light in the act?

NARRATOR: Here is the beautiful, stubborn simplicity of the plan. Galileo, and a Dutch thinker named Isaac Beeckman who wrestled with the same idea around sixteen twenty-nine, imagined two people standing on two distant hilltops at night. Each one holds a lantern. And each lantern has a cover, a shutter, that can be whipped away in a heartbeat.

NARRATOR: The first observer opens his lantern. His light streaks across the valley to the second hill. The moment the second observer sees that light arrive, he opens his own lantern in reply. And that answering light streaks all the way back. So the first observer times it. He starts counting the instant he uncovers his own flame, and he stops the instant he sees the reply come back. In that single interval, light has made a full round trip. Out to the far hill, and home again.

NARRATOR: If you know the distance between the hills, the physics is clean. The speed of light is just the total distance travelled, which is twice the gap between the hills, divided by that measured time. Out and back, over the clock. It is, honestly, a gorgeous idea. Two lanterns, two hills, and a measurement of the fastest thing in the universe.

QUESTION: So did it work?

NARRATOR: No. And the reason it failed is the whole lesson. Think about what actually happens on that second hilltop. The light arrives. But the second observer is a human being. Light hits his eye, a signal races up his optic nerve, his brain registers it, decides to act, and fires a command down to his hand to yank the cover off the lantern. All of that takes time. Call it a quarter of a second, maybe more. That reaction time is baked into the measurement, and there is no way to remove it.

NARRATOR: Now compare the two things you're adding together. On one side, you have the actual flight of the light. Even across hills a couple of kilometres apart, light makes the entire round trip in well under a hundred thousandth of a second. On the other side, you have a slow human nervous system fumbling in the dark, taking thousands of times longer to react. The thing you're trying to measure is a whisper. The thing drowning it out is a shout. The genuine travel time of the light is a vanishingly small fraction of the total delay, and the rest is just reflexes and clumsiness. And that's before you even ask what clock they were using, because in the sixteen hundreds there was no stopwatch on Earth precise enough to time a fraction of a second anyway.

NARRATOR: So Galileo, being honest, admitted defeat on the number. He could not measure the speed of light this way. But he wrote down something careful and important. He said that if light's motion is not truly instantaneous, then it must be extraordinarily fast. Not infinite. Just faster than any tool he had could ever hope to catch.

QUESTION: Surely someone tried again with better equipment?

NARRATOR: They did. Decades later, in sixteen sixty-seven, a group of Italy's finest experimenters, the Accademia del Cimento in Florence, the Academy of Experiment, took up the lanterns again. They set their observers roughly a mile apart and watched for the delay. And their report came back with a flat, deflating result. No delay. Nothing they could detect at all. Which, of course, doesn't mean light is instant. It just means light was, once again, far too fast for two people and a pair of shutters to pin down.

NARRATOR: And this is the moment that makes the whole story matter. Because after all of this effort, the best estimate humanity had for the speed of light ranged from, and I'm quoting the spirit of it, very fast, all the way up to infinity. That was the state of the art. The lanterns had proven their own point in reverse. They had shown, beyond doubt, that no experiment done here on the ground, on human hills, with human hands, would ever be big enough to trap something moving this quickly.

NARRATOR: And that failure contained the answer. If the Earth is too small a stage, you need a bigger one. You need distances so vast that even light, at its impossible pace, takes real, measurable time to cross them. You need to stop using hills, and start using the solar system. Just nine years after Florence gave up, a young Danish astronomer named Ole Rømer would look up at a moon of Jupiter, and finally, for the first time in history, put a number on the speed of light. But that is the next story.

NARRATOR: For the physics behind why light has a finite speed at all, and how we finally measured it, head to the teaching episode on the nature and speed of light in Module Seven.
