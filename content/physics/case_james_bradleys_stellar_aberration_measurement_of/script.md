NARRATOR: This is part of Module seven, The Nature of Light. And it begins with an astronomer who set out to prove one thing, failed completely, and in failing stumbled onto something far bigger than what he was chasing. He was trying to catch the Earth moving. What he caught instead was the speed of light.

NARRATOR: Picture a man on a small boat on the River Thames, some three hundred years ago. It's a breezy afternoon, the boat is tacking back and forth across the water, and he keeps glancing up at the little flag fluttering at the top of the mast. And he notices something odd. Every time the boat changes direction, the flag swings to point a different way. The wind hasn't changed. The wind is doing exactly what it was doing a moment ago. So why does the flag keep turning? The sailors tell him it's obvious. It's not just the wind that decides which way the flag points. It's the wind combined with the motion of the boat. That tiny, ordinary observation is about to unlock one of the deepest measurements in the history of science.

QUESTION: So what was the astronomer actually trying to do in the first place?

NARRATOR: His name was James Bradley, and in seventeen twenty-eight he was chasing a ghost that astronomers had hunted for two thousand years. He wanted to prove, once and for all, that the Earth moves around the Sun. Now, by Bradley's time nearly every serious astronomer already believed in the heliocentric model, the idea that the Earth orbits the Sun. But belief isn't proof. The Church still officially denied it. And there was one clean, decisive test that had never quite worked: parallax.

QUESTION: What is parallax, and why would it prove the Earth moves?

NARRATOR: Hold up a finger at arm's length and look at it with one eye, then the other. Your finger seems to jump against the background. That jump happens because your two eyes look from slightly different positions. Now think of the Earth as those two eyes. In summer the Earth sits on one side of its orbit around the Sun. Six months later, in winter, it's on the far side, hundreds of millions of kilometres away. If you look at a nearby star from those two vantage points, the star should appear to shift, just a hair, against the far more distant stars behind it. And crucially, over a full year the star should trace out a tiny ellipse in the sky, its position tied directly to where the Earth is in its orbit. If Bradley could see that ellipse, he would be holding direct, physical proof that the Earth swings around the Sun. So he built a precise telescope, aimed it at a star nearly overhead, and he watched, night after night, for a year.

NARRATOR: And he did see the star move. It traced out a neat little loop in the sky, just as predicted. Victory, surely. Except it wasn't victory at all. Because when Bradley looked carefully at the pattern, it was wrong. The timing was wrong. The direction of the shift was wrong.

QUESTION: What do you mean the direction was wrong?

NARRATOR: Here's the thing about parallax. If the star's apparent shift were caused by the Earth's changing position, then the star should be most displaced when the Earth is furthest to one side of its orbit. The shift should track the Earth's location in space. But that's not what Bradley found. The star reached its extreme positions three months out of step with the parallax prediction. The displacement didn't line up with where the Earth was. It lined up with which way the Earth was going. The star always appeared shifted in the direction the Earth was travelling at that moment. That is a completely different phenomenon. And it was bigger, far bigger, than any parallax could ever be.

NARRATOR: For a while it was a genuine mystery. And this is where that flag on the boat comes back. Bradley realised the sky was doing exactly what the flag was doing. It's not just the direction the light comes from that decides where a star appears. It's the direction of the light combined with the motion of the observer. And we, riding on the Earth, are the observer, hurtling through space at enormous speed.

QUESTION: So how does moving sideways change where a star appears to be?

NARRATOR: Think about running through vertical rain. If you stand still, the rain falls straight down, and you'd hold your umbrella straight up. But the moment you start running, the rain seems to come at you from ahead, at a slant. You have to tilt the umbrella forward into it, even though the rain itself never changed direction. It only looks tilted because you are moving through it. Starlight does the very same thing. The light from a star travels toward the Earth along a fixed path. But because the Earth is sweeping sideways through space, that light appears to arrive at a slight angle, tilted in the direction we're moving. So to catch the star in your telescope, you have to tip the telescope forward, ever so slightly, into the incoming light. This tilt is what we call stellar aberration. And as the Earth swings around its orbit and reverses its direction of travel every six months, the tilt swings with it, which is exactly why the star appeared to trace a loop that followed the Earth's velocity rather than its position.

NARRATOR: Now here is the beautiful part, the part that turns a curiosity into one of the great measurements. The size of that tilt depends on a race between two speeds: how fast the Earth moves sideways, and how fast the light itself is coming in. If light were infinitely fast, there would be no tilt at all, the light would arrive before your sideways motion mattered. The slower the light, the bigger the tilt. So if you can measure the tilt, and you already know how fast the Earth is moving, you can solve for the one thing nobody had ever cleanly measured from the heavens: the speed of light.

QUESTION: How did Bradley actually turn an angle into a speed?

NARRATOR: He reasoned it through with nothing more than triangles. Imagine the incoming starlight from the Sun's point of view. It comes in at some angle, and you can split its motion into two parts: a part along the plane of the Earth's orbit, and a part across it. Now switch to the Earth's point of view. From here, the Earth's own sideways velocity simply adds on to the sideways part of the light's motion. Bradley did what anyone before Einstein would do: he added the velocities together the ordinary, common-sense way. And yes, we know today that light doesn't truly speed up like that, special relativity forbids it, but Bradley lived long before relativity, and for something moving as slowly as the Earth, his simple approach gives an answer that's astonishingly accurate.

NARRATOR: When he worked the geometry all the way through, comparing the star's true angle to its apparent, tilted angle, the messy trigonometry collapsed into something clean and elegant. The tilt of the star depends on just two things multiplied together: the ratio of the Earth's speed to the speed of light, and the sine of the angle the starlight makes with the Earth's motion. That's it. A whole shifting sky, distilled into one small relationship. And every quantity in it was something Bradley could measure or already knew.

NARRATOR: He could measure the angles with his telescope. And he knew the Earth's speed around the Sun, somewhere between twenty-nine point three and thirty point three kilometres per second, roughly thirty kilometres per second. He plugged in his numbers. And out came the speed of light: three hundred and one thousand kilometres per second.

QUESTION: How good was that number, really?

NARRATOR: The value we accept today is just under three hundred thousand kilometres per second. Bradley, working in seventeen twenty-eight, with a telescope bolted to a friend's chimney and a pencil full of trigonometry, landed within a whisper of it, a fraction of a percent off. He had measured the speed of light not with mirrors and lamps in a laboratory, but by watching a single star wobble overhead and understanding why. Light, it turned out, was blisteringly fast, but it was not infinite. It had a speed, a real, finite, measurable speed, and you could read it right out of the sky.

NARRATOR: And there's a final irony worth sitting with. Bradley never did find the parallax he set out to prove; the stars were simply too far away for his instruments to catch it. He failed at his actual goal. But stellar aberration, the thing that got in his way, was itself undeniable proof that the Earth is moving. A star's apparent tilt can only exist if the observer is in motion. So Bradley proved the Earth moves after all, just not in the way he intended, and he brought home the speed of light as a bonus. The universe answered a question he wasn't even asking.
