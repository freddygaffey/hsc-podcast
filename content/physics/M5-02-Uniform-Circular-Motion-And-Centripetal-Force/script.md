NARRATOR: Here's today's topic, and its central, slightly counter-intuitive claim. An object in uniform circular motion, that phrase means moving in a circle at constant speed, is accelerating the entire time, even though its speed never changes. Let me convince you, because if you truly get this one idea the rest of the episode is just equations. Velocity is a vector. It has a size, the speed, and a direction, the way the object is heading. For something going in a circle, the direction of travel is always along the tangent, along the edge, changing continuously as it goes round. So even if the speedometer reads a rock-steady value, the velocity vector is swinging around constantly because its direction keeps changing. And acceleration is defined as the rate of change of velocity, any change, in size or in direction. Change the direction of the velocity and you have, by definition, an acceleration. So a car going round a roundabout at a steady forty kilometres an hour is accelerating every second it's on the curve. This is the number-one trap in this topic, and examiners set it deliberately: students equate constant speed with no acceleration. Constant speed, changing direction, means accelerating. Write that sentence on the page and you've earned the mark.

NARRATOR: So which way does this acceleration point? Here's the beautiful result, and the syllabus wants the word. If you carefully track how the velocity vector changes from one instant to the next as the object goes round, the change always points inward, toward the centre of the circle. Not forward, not backward, not outward, inward. We call this the centripetal acceleration, and "centripetal" is Latin for "centre-seeking". It is an acceleration that is always aimed at the centre of the circle, perpendicular to the velocity, at right angles to the direction of travel. That perpendicularity is important and we'll come back to it: because the acceleration is sideways to the motion, it changes the direction of the velocity without changing its size, which is exactly what going in a circle at constant speed requires. The velocity points along the tangent; the acceleration points along the radius, straight at the centre. Ninety degrees apart, the whole way round.

NARRATOR: Before the forces, let's nail the language of going round in circles, because the exam expects three precise terms and their symbols, and there's a memorisable relationship between them. First, the period, symbol capital T, the time taken for one complete revolution, one full lap, measured in seconds. Second, the frequency, symbol lower-case f, the number of revolutions per second, measured in hertz. Period and frequency are reciprocals of each other: the frequency is one divided by the period, and the period is one divided by the frequency. If a wheel takes half a second per turn, its frequency is two turns per second, two hertz. Third, and this one is new for many of you, the angular velocity, symbol omega, the Greek letter, which measures how fast the object sweeps around in terms of angle, specifically in radians per second. One full revolution is two pi radians, so the angular velocity is two pi divided by the period, which is also two pi times the frequency. Those three, period, frequency, angular velocity, and their links, are collected in Listing one.

NARRATOR: Now connect the angular velocity to the ordinary straight-line speed, because the exam uses both. In one full period, the object travels once around the circle, a distance equal to the circumference, two pi times the radius. Speed is distance over time, so the speed equals two pi times the radius, divided by the period. And since two pi over the period is just the angular velocity, that same speed equals the angular velocity times the radius. Both forms are worth knowing: speed equals two pi times radius over period, and speed equals angular velocity times radius. The second one says something physical and examinable: at the same angular velocity, a point further from the centre moves faster, because it has a bigger circle to get around in the same time. NARRATOR: And now Newton's second law does the rest for free. If there's an acceleration toward the centre, then by the second law there must be a net force toward the centre, in the same direction as the acceleration, equal to mass times that acceleration. So the net inward force equals mass times speed squared divided by radius. This is the centripetal force. Write it as mass times speed squared over radius, or equivalently mass times angular velocity squared times radius. That equation is the workhorse of the entire topic, and it's in Listing two with all its forms. But here is the single most important conceptual point in this episode, more important than the equation itself, and it's where marks are won and lost, so I'm going to give it a name and repeat it all episode.

NARRATOR: The centripetal force is not a new, separate, extra force. It is not something that appears out of nowhere just because an object is going in a circle. There is no little engine of "circular-motion force" bolted onto the object. Centripetal force is simply the name we give to the net of the real, ordinary forces, once they're resolved toward the centre. The real forces are the ones you already know: gravity, tension in a string, the normal force from a surface, friction. In any circular-motion problem, one or more of those real forces is what physically points toward the centre, or has a component that does, and their inward sum is what we're calling the centripetal force. So the method, and this is the mnemonic for the whole topic, is: net to the centre. Say it: net to the centre. You find the real forces first, draw them on a free-body diagram, work out the net force pointing toward the centre, and then, and only then, set that net inward force equal to mass times speed squared over radius. Never add a centripetal force onto your free-body diagram as an extra arrow. Centripetal is the answer, the net, not one of the ingredients.

NARRATOR: Let me show you why "net to the centre" is worth marks by contrasting a weak answer with a strong one, because a marker separates them in a heartbeat. Suppose a ball is whirled on a string in a horizontal circle and the question asks what provides the centripetal force. The strong answer names the real force, tension, states that it points to the centre, and identifies it as the centripetal force rather than an addition to it. Net to the centre. Name the real forces, sum them inward, set equal to mass times speed squared over radius.

NARRATOR: This is exactly the moment to kill the misconception the whole topic trips on, the one the plan flags: why doesn't the object fly outward? Everyone has felt it. You're in a car that turns sharply and you feel flung against the door. Swing a bucket and you feel it pull outward on your hand. Surely there's an outward force? No. There is no outward force on the object going in a circle. The feeling of being flung outward is not a force pushing you out; it is your own inertia, your Newton's-first-law tendency to keep going in a straight line, while the car door pushes you inward to make you turn with the car. You're not being thrown at the door, the door is being pulled across into you. The made-up outward force has a name, the centrifugal force, "centre-fleeing", and in this course it is treated as a fictitious force, an illusion that appears only because you're sitting in a rotating, accelerating frame. In the proper, ground-based analysis the HSC wants, there is no centrifugal force. Do not draw it on a free-body diagram. The only real forces point inward, or have inward components, and their net is the centripetal force. Listing five lays out the centripetal-versus-centrifugal distinction cleanly, because "distinguish between them" is a real exam question.

NARRATOR: And here's the proof that settles it, the single best mental image for this whole topic. What actually happens if the inward force suddenly vanishes? Cut the string on the whirling ball. Your gut says the ball flies straight outward, away from the centre. It does not. The instant the string is cut, there is no net force, and by Newton's first law the ball travels in a straight line, along the tangent, the direction it was heading at that instant. It flies off sideways, on a tangent, not radially outward. A hammer thrower releases the hammer and it leaves along the tangent to the circle, which is exactly why they time the release so carefully. If there really were an outward centrifugal force, the ball would accelerate straight out along the radius, and it simply doesn't. So the force was never outward. It was inward the whole time, the only thing bending the natural straight-line path into a circle, and when you remove it the straight line reasserts itself. Tangent, not radius.
