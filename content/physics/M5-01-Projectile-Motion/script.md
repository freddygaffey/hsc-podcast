NARRATOR: Welcome to Year Twelve. This is the first episode of Module Five, Advanced Mechanics, and it is where physics stops moving in a straight line and starts to curve. For all of Year Eleven, motion was a line: a car speeding up along a road, a ball dropped straight down, a trolley sliding down a ramp. Module Five takes everything you already know, kinematics, forces, energy, and lifts it into two dimensions and out into space. Motion that curves comes in three flavours across this module: projectiles arcing through the air, objects whirling in circles, and planets falling forever around a star. Today we do the first and the friendliest of the three, projectile motion, and the single idea that unlocks it is one you already met at the very end of Module One. So let's start there, with a recap, because today is built almost entirely out of things you already own.

NARRATOR: Cast your mind back over the last few Year Eleven lessons, because the whole of today rides on them. In Module One, kinematics, you learned the four equations of uniformly accelerated motion, the suvat equations, which relate the five quantities: initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration, time, and displacement. You chose which equation to use by spotting the missing quantity. You applied them to motion under gravity, where a dropped object accelerates downward at about nine point eight metres per second squared. Then, in the last kinematics episode, vectors in a plane, you learned the one skill today depends on completely: resolving a vector into components. Any vector pointing at an angle can be replaced by two perpendicular vectors, a horizontal one and a vertical one, that together do exactly the same job. And the reason we bothered, the reason that was worth a whole episode, is that perpendicular components are independent: they do not affect each other, so a hard two-dimensional problem splits into two easy one-dimensional problems. Hold that sentence. It is the entire secret of today.

NARRATOR: From Module Two, dynamics, hold two more things. First, Newton's second law: the net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration. Second, and this is the one that matters most today, the acceleration an object gets from gravity does not depend on its mass. A heavy object and a light object, dropped together, hit the ground at the same time, because although gravity pulls harder on the heavier one, the heavier one also has more inertia to move, and the two effects cancel exactly. We'll come back to that when we hook into Galileo. And from the work and energy episode, remember that a projectile is a closed mechanical system: as it rises it trades kinetic energy for gravitational potential energy, and as it falls it trades back, which gives us a lovely independent check on our answers.

QUESTION: Quick check before the new material, straight from the vectors episode. A vector of magnitude ten metres per second points at thirty degrees above the horizontal. Is its horizontal component ten times the cosine of thirty, or ten times the sine of thirty? Pause the player, decide, then play.

NARRATOR: The horizontal component is ten times the cosine of thirty degrees, because the angle is measured from the horizontal, so the horizontal side is the one adjacent to the angle, and adjacent takes the cosine. That gives about eight point six six metres per second horizontally. The vertical component is ten times the sine of thirty, which is five metres per second upward. Horizontal is cosine, vertical is sine, when the angle is measured from the horizontal. Keep that reflex sharp, because from here on, every projectile problem begins by resolving the launch velocity exactly like this. Now let's meet today's topic.

NARRATOR: A projectile is any object that is launched into the air and then moves under the influence of gravity alone. A kicked football, a thrown cricket ball, a bullet, a long jumper, a stream of water from a hose, a stunt car off a ramp. Once it has left the hand or the boot or the barrel, the only force acting on it, in our model, is its own weight, gravity pulling it straight down. We deliberately ignore air resistance. That is the HSC projectile model, and naming its assumptions is worth marks, so here they are: no air resistance, so the horizontal motion is undisturbed; the acceleration is constant, equal to g, about nine point eight metres per second squared, directed vertically downward; and therefore there is no horizontal force and no horizontal acceleration at all. Those assumptions are listed in Listing one. The moment a question says "ignore air resistance" or "assume a smooth projectile", it is inviting you to use exactly this model.

NARRATOR: Now the core idea, and it is the most important sentence in this entire episode. The horizontal motion and the vertical motion of a projectile are completely independent of each other. They happen at the same time, sharing one clock, but neither one influences the other. Vertically, the projectile is simply in free fall: it accelerates downward at g, exactly like the dropped-ball problems from kinematics. Horizontally, there is no force, so by Newton's first law the horizontal velocity never changes: it is constant for the entire flight. So a projectile is doing two things at once that you already know how to do separately: constant-acceleration motion vertically, and constant-velocity motion horizontally. The curved path you see, the parabola, is just what those two simple motions look like when you watch them together.

NARRATOR: This gives us the mnemonic for the whole method, and it is the marquee memory hook for this episode, so write it down and keep it: split, then suvat. Say it again: split, then suvat. Step one, split: resolve the motion into its horizontal part, which has constant velocity, and its vertical part, which has constant acceleration g. Step two, suvat: treat each part as its own one-dimensional suvat problem, the very kind you solved all through Module One. The horizontal problem and the vertical problem are linked by one thing and one thing only, the time, because both motions take exactly the same time, they share the same clock. That shared clock is the bridge between the two halves. Nine times out of ten the method is: use the vertical motion to find the time, then feed that time into the horizontal motion to find the range. Split, then suvat, joined by time. The whole recipe is in Listing one, and the suvat toolkit itself, the four equations plus the handy level-ground shortcuts for time of flight, height and range, is collected in Listing two.

NARRATOR: Let me show you why this matters by contrasting a weak answer with a strong one, because the marker can tell them apart instantly. Suppose a question shows a ball thrown horizontally off a cliff and asks you to analyse its motion. A band-four answer says something like "the ball goes forward and slows down as it curves toward the ground." That is wrong twice over and earns almost nothing: the ball does not slow down horizontally at all, and it does not speed up horizontally either. A band-six answer says: "Resolve the motion into two independent components. Horizontally there is no force, so the horizontal velocity is constant throughout the flight. Vertically the ball is in free fall, accelerating downward at g, so its vertical velocity increases uniformly from zero. The two combine to give a parabolic path." That answer names the model, states the independence, and identifies each motion correctly. The magic words the marker is hunting for are "resolve into independent horizontal and vertical components." Lead with them every time.

NARRATOR: Let's make it concrete with the classic first worked example, the horizontal launch. Picture a ball rolling off the edge of a table one point two five metres high, leaving the edge horizontally at three metres per second. Two questions the exam always asks: how long is it in the air, and how far from the base of the table does it land, the range. Split, then suvat. Take the vertical part first, because that is what gives us the time. Vertically, the ball starts with zero vertical velocity, because it left the table horizontally, and it accelerates downward at nine point eight metres per second squared through a drop of one point two five metres. Using the suvat equation "displacement equals initial vertical velocity times time plus one half times acceleration times time squared", the first term is zero, so the drop equals one half times g times the time squared. Rearranging, the time squared is two times the drop divided by g, which is two times one point two five divided by nine point eight, about zero point two five five, so the time of flight is about zero point five zero five seconds. That is the shared clock.

NARRATOR: Now feed that time into the horizontal part. Horizontally the velocity is constant at three metres per second, so the range is simply velocity times time, three times zero point five zero five, about one point five two metres. Done. Notice the structure: the vertical drop set the clock, and the horizontal motion just coasted along it. Notice too what did not appear anywhere in that calculation: the mass of the ball. It cancelled out, because g is the same for everything. The full working is in Listing three. And here is a beautiful, examinable consequence: if you rolled the ball off twice as fast, six metres per second, it would still take the same zero point five zero five seconds to fall, because the vertical motion has not changed at all, but it would travel twice as far. The height sets the time; the horizontal speed sets the range. They are independent.

QUESTION: Here is a genuine retrieval one, and it is worth pausing for. From that same table, one point two five metres high, we drop a second ball straight down at the exact instant the first ball rolls off the edge horizontally. Which ball hits the floor first? Pause the player, commit to an answer and a reason, then play.

NARRATOR: They hit the floor at exactly the same instant. This is the single most famous, most tested idea in projectile motion, so make sure you own the reasoning. The vertical motion of both balls is identical: both start with zero vertical velocity, both accelerate downward at g, both fall the same one point two five metres, so both take the same zero point five zero five seconds to land. The fact that one of them is also travelling horizontally at three metres per second makes no difference whatsoever to how fast it falls, because horizontal motion and vertical motion are independent. The marker wants that word, independent, and wants you to say the horizontal velocity has no effect on the vertical fall. A weak answer says "the dropped one lands first because it goes straight down." A strong answer says "they land together, because the vertical component of the projectile's motion is free fall identical to the dropped ball, and the horizontal motion is independent of it." This is exactly the demonstration Galileo is famous for, and it brings us to our first case-study hook.

NARRATOR: Around the early sixteen hundreds, Galileo rolled balls off a table at different horizontal speeds and carefully measured, for each launch speed, how far out they landed. He found the trajectory traced a perfect parabola, and, crucially, that the time to fall did not depend on the horizontal speed at all. That was the experimental birth of the idea that horizontal and vertical motion are independent, the very principle we have just used. There's a full case study on Galileo's projectile-motion demonstration if you want the story of how he pulled it off and why it overturned two thousand years of Aristotle, so I won't retell it here. Just tag it in your memory: Galileo, rolling balls off a table, is the origin of "split, then suvat."

NARRATOR: Now the second standard problem, and the more common one in exams: launching at an angle. Everything is the same method, split then suvat, but now the launch velocity has both a horizontal and a vertical part from the very start, so we resolve it first. Take a projectile launched from ground level at twenty metres per second, at an angle of thirty degrees above the horizontal. Resolve the launch velocity, exactly like the warm-up question. The horizontal component is twenty times the cosine of thirty, about seventeen point three metres per second, and this stays constant for the whole flight. The vertical component is twenty times the sine of thirty, which is ten metres per second upward, and this is the one that changes, because gravity acts on it. Those two numbers are the start of every angled-projectile answer, and they go straight into Listing four.

NARRATOR: With those components, the standard quantities fall out one by one. First, the time to reach maximum height. At the very top of the arc, the vertical velocity is momentarily zero, the object is neither rising nor falling for that instant, though its horizontal velocity is still a full seventeen point three metres per second, it is still moving. This is a classic trap: at the top, the velocity is not zero, only its vertical component is zero. Using "final vertical velocity equals initial vertical velocity minus g times time", we set the final vertical velocity to zero, so the time to the top is the initial vertical velocity divided by g, ten divided by nine point eight, about one point zero two seconds. Because the path is symmetric for a projectile that lands at the same height it launched from, the total time of flight is double that, about two point zero four seconds. Symmetry is your friend here: time up equals time down.

NARRATOR: Next, the maximum height. Use the vertical suvat equation "final velocity squared equals initial velocity squared minus two g times height", with the final vertical velocity zero at the top. The height is the initial vertical velocity squared divided by two g, that's ten squared, a hundred, divided by two times nine point eight, about five point one metres. And finally the range, the horizontal distance travelled before it lands. Horizontal velocity is constant, so range equals horizontal velocity times the total time of flight, seventeen point three times two point zero four, about thirty five point three metres. There it is: split into components, use the vertical motion to get the times and the height, then use the horizontal motion and the total time to get the range. Every angled-projectile question is this same sequence. It is all worked in full in Listing four.

QUESTION: Let me make you do one, because you only really learn this by driving. Pause the player, work out both, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. The initial vertical velocity is forty times the sine of thirty degrees. Sine of thirty is one half, so that is twenty metres per second upward. The time to the top comes from setting the vertical velocity to zero: time equals initial vertical velocity divided by g, twenty divided by ten, which is two seconds. So it reaches the top after two seconds, and by symmetry the whole flight lasts four seconds. If you also want the range as a bonus, the horizontal velocity is forty times the cosine of thirty, about thirty four point six metres per second, times four seconds, about a hundred and thirty eight metres. If you got the vertical component and the time to the top, you have the method cold. The pattern is always: resolve, then let the vertical motion set the clock.

NARRATOR: Now the parabola itself, because the syllabus wants you to know why the path is that specific shape, and it is a satisfying little argument. Horizontally, the displacement grows in proportion to time, at a steady rate, because the horizontal velocity is constant. Vertically, under constant acceleration, the displacement grows in proportion to time squared. So if you call the horizontal distance x and eliminate the time, the vertical position turns out to depend on x squared. A relationship where one coordinate depends on the square of the other is, by definition, a parabola. That is why every projectile, in the absence of air resistance, follows a parabolic path, and why "parabolic" is the word the marker wants, not "curved" or "arced". The algebra behind that is in Listing five if you'd like to see the time eliminated step by step.

NARRATOR: Let's talk about launch angle and range, because this is a favourite exam theme. On level ground, if you fix the launch speed and vary only the angle, the range is greatest at forty five degrees. Below forty five the projectile is too flat, it lands too soon; above forty five it goes high but not far. Forty five degrees is the sweet spot that balances hang time against horizontal speed. There's a second, prettier fact that examiners love: complementary launch angles give the same range. That is, an angle and its complement, the two that add up to ninety degrees, produce identical ranges on level ground. Fifteen degrees and seventy five degrees give the same range; thirty degrees and sixty degrees give the same range. So if a question tells you a projectile launched at thirty degrees lands a certain distance away, you can immediately say a launch at sixty degrees, same speed, lands at the same distance, just via a higher, slower arc. That result and the reasoning are in Listing six. The mnemonic here is simple: forty five for furthest, and complements match.

NARRATOR: This is a good moment to weave in the connections, because projectile motion does not stand alone. Look back first: everything today is Module One kinematics used twice at once. The vertical half is precisely the free-fall suvat problem from the equations-of-motion episode; the horizontal half is constant-velocity motion, the flat line on a velocity-time graph from the graphs episode. Projectile motion did not introduce a single new equation, it just applies the old ones along two axes. And it is Module Two dynamics underneath: the reason the vertical acceleration is g and the horizontal acceleration is zero is Newton's second law applied to the only force present, gravity. So this topic is a genuine synthesis of the two Year Eleven mechanics modules. If you were shaky on resolving vectors or on suvat, this is where it comes back to bite, and where fixing it pays off.

NARRATOR: Now look forward, because the same mathematics reappears twice more in Year Twelve, and spotting that will save you real effort. In Module Six, electromagnetism, you will fire a charged particle sideways into a uniform electric field between two charged plates. The field pushes the particle steadily to one side while it keeps coasting forward, and, exactly like a projectile, it traces a parabola. The uniform electric field plays the role gravity plays here; the algebra is identical. And later in this very module, when we get to motion in gravitational fields, you will see that g itself, the nine point eight we have taken as given, actually comes from Newton's law of universal gravitation, the gravitational field strength at Earth's surface. So the constant we have been feeding into suvat all day has a deeper origin we will uncover in a few episodes. Projectiles here, charged particles in Module Six, gravitational fields later, all the same parabola.

NARRATOR: Before we finish, the second case-study hook, and it is the mass-independence idea we flagged at the start. Legend has it that Galileo dropped two balls of different weights from the Leaning Tower of Pisa and they landed at the same time, demonstrating that, ignoring air resistance, the acceleration due to gravity is the same for all masses. Whether he truly did it from the tower is genuinely disputed by historians, and that dispute is itself examinable, but the physics is rock solid: because acceleration equals force divided by mass, and gravity's force is mass times g, the mass cancels and every object accelerates at g regardless of how heavy it is. There's a full case study on the Leaning Tower experiment with the history and the controversy, so I'll leave the story there. For today, just bind it to the physics: mass cancels, so g is universal, which is exactly why the mass never appeared in any of our projectile calculations.

NARRATOR: Let me pull today together into the takeaways, phrased the way you'd write them for marks. One: a projectile moves under gravity alone; we assume no air resistance, a constant downward acceleration g, and therefore constant horizontal velocity. Two: horizontal and vertical motion are completely independent, sharing only the time, so we split every problem into a constant-velocity horizontal part and a constant-acceleration vertical part, split then suvat. Three: the standard method is to use the vertical motion to find the time, then feed that time into the horizontal motion to find the range. Four: at maximum height the vertical velocity is zero but the horizontal velocity is not, so the object is still moving; and the flight is symmetric, time up equals time down. Five: the path is a parabola, the range is maximum at forty five degrees, and complementary angles give equal ranges. QUESTION: Question one, worth two marks. Explain why a heavy ball and a light ball, released from the same height at the same time, reach the ground together, assuming air resistance is negligible. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. The acceleration due to gravity is independent of mass. Although the gravitational force on the heavier ball is larger, its mass, and therefore its inertia, is larger in exactly the same proportion, so by Newton's second law the acceleration, force divided by mass, is the same for both, equal to g. Since both balls start from rest, fall the same distance, and have the same acceleration, they take the same time to land. The two marks are for, one, stating that gravitational acceleration does not depend on mass, and two, justifying it with force over mass, the mass cancelling. Do not just say "gravity pulls them equally", that misses the cancellation and drops a mark.

QUESTION: Question two, worth three marks. A ball is thrown horizontally at eight metres per second from the top of a building twenty metres high. The time squared is two times twenty divided by nine point eight, about four point zero eight, so the time of flight is about two point zero two seconds. Now the horizontal part: the horizontal velocity is constant at eight metres per second, so the range is eight times two point zero two, about sixteen point two metres. QUESTION: Question four, the extended one, worth five marks. A stone is launched from ground level at twenty five metres per second at an angle of fifty three degrees above the horizontal.
