NARRATOR: This is part of Module six, Electromagnetism. And I want to start with a challenge. Take the strongest magnet in your house, and take a loop of copper wire connected to something that measures electric current. Now hold that magnet inside the loop. Just hold it there, perfectly still, as close and as strong as you like. How much current flows in the wire? The honest answer, the one almost everyone gets wrong, is none. Zero. A powerful magnet, sitting right inside a coil of wire, and nothing happens. The magnetism is doing absolutely nothing to the electricity.

QUESTION: So how do you ever get a magnet to push electricity through a wire?

NARRATOR: You move it. That is the entire secret, and it is far stranger than it sounds. The magnet only matters when it is changing. And the man who dragged that fact out of nature, who turned it from a lucky accident into a law, was a bookbinder's apprentice with almost no formal schooling, working in a basement in London in the autumn of eighteen thirty-one.

NARRATOR: To feel why this was such a shock, you have to go back a decade. In eighteen twenty, a Danish physicist named Hans Christian Oersted was giving a lecture when he noticed something no one expected. He ran an electric current through a wire, and a nearby compass needle twitched. Electricity, it turned out, could make magnetism. That single twitching needle set the scientific world on fire. A brilliant Frenchman, Andre-Marie Ampere, worked out the mathematics within weeks, describing exactly how a current flowing through a wire wraps a magnetic field around itself. Electricity makes magnetism. That much was now certain.

NARRATOR: And that raised an irresistible question, one that hung over physics like a dare.

QUESTION: If electricity can create magnetism, can magnetism create electricity?

NARRATOR: It seems obvious that it should. Nature loves symmetry. Surely the door swings both ways. So the greatest experimenters in Europe tried, and tried, and failed. They wrapped wires around magnets. They laid coils next to powerful iron bars. They connected everything to the most sensitive current detectors they had, and they waited. The needles stayed dead. A few people caught fleeting hints, a weak flicker that came and went before they could pin it down, and then vanished. Faraday himself noticed these frustrating, temporary flickers, and they gnawed at him. He wrote of the way one electrical state could cause an opposite state in its neighbourhood, a phenomenon people were already calling induction. He was convinced there was something real hiding underneath, something everyone else was missing.

NARRATOR: Now, who was this man to succeed where the mathematicians had stalled? Michael Faraday was the son of a blacksmith. As a teenager he was apprenticed to a bookbinder, and he educated himself by reading the very books he was binding. He could barely handle algebra. But he had a gift that no equation can teach, a physical intuition so vivid he could almost see the invisible forces threading through space. And he had a hunch about why everyone was failing. Everyone was looking at steady magnets and steady currents. Faraday's suspicion was that steadiness itself was the enemy.

NARRATOR: So let me put you in that laboratory at the Royal Institution. Faraday takes a large block of wood and winds around it about two hundred and three feet of copper wire in one continuous length, coil after coil. Then, threaded between the turns of that first coil, he winds a second wire, another two hundred and three feet of it, so the two coils are woven right through each other, almost touching but never quite, kept apart only by twine. Two separate wires, deeply intimate, but electrically strangers. One of these coils he connects to a battery. The other he connects to a galvanometer, a device whose needle swings when current passes through it.

NARRATOR: Think about what he has built. When he closes the battery circuit, a steady current pours through the first coil, and by Ampere's law that current wraps it in a magnetic field. The second coil sits bathed in that magnetism. According to the dream of symmetry, that magnetic field should now drive a current in the second coil, and the galvanometer needle should lean over and stay there for as long as the battery is connected.

QUESTION: So he connects the battery, and the needle swings across and holds. Right?

NARRATOR: No. And this is the moment everything turns. He connects the battery, and the needle gives a sudden kick, and then falls straight back to zero. The current is still flowing through the first coil. The magnetic field is still there, strong and steady. But the galvanometer reads nothing. The magnetism, just sitting there, does nothing at all. For anyone chasing the symmetry dream, this looks like failure all over again. The needle is dead.

NARRATOR: But then Faraday does the thing the others may not have watched carefully enough for. He disconnects the battery. And the needle kicks again, only this time it swings the other way, and then settles back to zero. Connect the circuit, one flick. Disconnect it, an equal and opposite flick. In between, while everything is steady, nothing.

QUESTION: What is the needle actually responding to?

NARRATOR: Not the magnetic field. The change in the magnetic field. When he connects the battery, the field in the first coil leaps from nothing up to full strength, and during that fleeting build-up, that instant of change, a current is induced in the second coil. Once the field is steady, the induction stops dead. When he disconnects, the field collapses back to nothing, another change, and the current is induced again, in the reverse direction. That was the piece everyone had missed. They had been searching for electricity born from magnetism, and it was really being born from moving magnetism, from magnetism in the act of changing.

NARRATOR: Faraday chased this down like a hunter. He found the effect was maddeningly small at first, so he swapped the wooden core for a core of soft iron, which concentrates the magnetism, and the needle's swing grew far larger. He piled on more and more turns of wire, and found the induced current grew with them, the more coils, the stronger the effect. And he noticed something with a beautiful logic to it. The induced current in the second coil always flowed in the direction that opposed the change that created it. When the field grew, the induced current pushed back against the growth. When the field collapsed, it tried to prop it up. Nature, it seemed, resists having its magnetism meddled with. Years later this stubbornness would be written down as Lenz's law, but Faraday saw it first, right there in the swing of a needle.

NARRATOR: Then he stripped the idea down to its purest form. He wound many layers of copper wire around a hollow cylinder, building a coil so dense with turns it could catch the faintest whisper of induction. No battery this time. No second circuit. Just this coil, connected to the galvanometer, and in his hand an ordinary bar magnet, a cylinder of steel about eight and a half inches long. The kind of magnet that had failed everyone before.

NARRATOR: He holds the magnet near the coil. Nothing. He slides one end of it toward the mouth of the coil, and the needle begins to move. He thrusts the magnet suddenly into the coil, and the needle jumps hard. He holds it there, deep inside, motionless, and the needle drifts back to zero, even though the magnet is now closer and more present than ever. He yanks it out, and the needle jumps the other way.

QUESTION: So the current does not care where the magnet is. What does it care about?

NARRATOR: Whether the magnet is moving. A stationary magnet, however powerful, however deep inside the coil, induces nothing. It is only the motion, the change, that matters. And in his most elegant version, Faraday pushed the magnet in one smooth pass all the way through the coil, and watched the needle swing first one way, as the magnet plunged in and the field through the coil grew, then swing back the other way as the magnet emerged and that field faded. One motion, two opposite kicks, the whole story of induction told in a single sweep of a needle.

NARRATOR: And now step back and see what he had actually proved. It worked with an electromagnet and it worked with a plain bar magnet, so the specific source did not matter. It scaled with the iron in the core and with the current in the coil, both of which set the strength of the magnetic field, so the magnetic field was clearly the agent. And above all, it appeared only when things were changing, at connection, at disconnection, at the moment of motion, and never in the steady state. There was only one conclusion left standing. Electricity is not induced by a magnetic field. It is induced by a changing magnetic field.

NARRATOR: That sentence is one of the quiet turning points in the history of the world. Because a changing magnetic field is something you can manufacture on demand, all day, forever, just by spinning a magnet past a coil. Faraday had found the principle behind every electrical generator that would ever be built, the power station, the dynamo, the transformer sharing energy between two coils that never touch. Nearly every watt of electricity flowing through the walls around you right now was born the way that needle first flickered, from a magnet in motion and a wire that felt it change. A self-taught bookbinder, in a basement, had reached into the invisible and pulled out the engine of the modern age.
