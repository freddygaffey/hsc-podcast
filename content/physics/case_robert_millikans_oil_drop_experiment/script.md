NARRATOR: This is part of Module 8, From the Universe to the Atom. And I want to start with a number. A single number, so small it almost doesn't feel real. Somewhere inside every wire in your house, every nerve in your body, every bolt of lightning, there is a tiny fundamental amount of electric charge that everything else is built from. A smallest possible piece. And its value is about one point six times ten to the minus nineteen coulombs. Write that out and it's a decimal point followed by eighteen zeros before you even reach the six. It is unimaginably small. And here is the strange part. Just over a century ago, a man measured it. Not estimated it, not guessed at it. He pinned it down, digit by digit, by watching tiny beads of oil hang motionless in the air. How on earth do you weigh something you cannot see, and then count a charge smaller than anything anyone had ever counted before?

QUESTION: To understand why that was so hard, you have to understand what nobody knew yet.

NARRATOR: Picture the world of physics at the turn of the twentieth century. It's a confident time. Steam and steel and electricity are remaking civilisation. Engineers are stringing power lines across cities. And yet, for all that mastery over electricity, one embarrassing fact remained. Nobody actually knew what electricity was. They could measure currents, they could build motors, they could light up whole streets, and they still could not tell you what the stuff flowing through the wires was made of. Was charge smooth, like water, something you could divide forever into smaller and smaller amounts with no bottom? Or was it grainy, made of tiny indivisible lumps, like sand?

NARRATOR: In eighteen ninety-seven, a physicist named J.J. Thomson had cracked open part of the mystery. Working with cathode rays, those eerie glowing beams inside vacuum tubes, he discovered a particle far smaller than any atom, a particle carrying negative charge. The electron. It was a genuine bombshell. For the first time, the atom, that word which literally means "uncuttable," turned out to have pieces inside it. But Thomson could only measure one thing about his new particle, and it was a strange, tangled thing. He measured the ratio of the electron's charge to its mass. The charge divided by the mass. One number, welding two unknowns together.

QUESTION: And why is a ratio not good enough?

NARRATOR: Because a ratio is a locked door with no key. Imagine I tell you that two numbers divide to give exactly three. Is that six over two? Thirty over ten? Three hundred over one hundred? You have no idea. Knowing the charge-to-mass ratio of the electron told physicists the shape of the relationship, but not the size of either piece. If you could just find the charge on its own, the actual charge of a single electron, then you could unlock everything. You could divide it back out of Thomson's ratio and finally get the mass of the electron too. That one measurement was the key to the whole lock. And it sat there, unmeasured, taunting the finest laboratories in the world for more than a decade.

NARRATOR: People tried. Thomson's own students tried. They worked with clouds. They would create a fine mist of charged water droplets inside a chamber and watch the whole cloud drift and settle under gravity and electric fields, and from the behaviour of the cloud they tried to back out an average charge. But water evaporates. The droplets shrank and vanished while you watched, the cloud shifted and blurred, and the numbers that came out were rough, wobbly averages. Averages of what, exactly? Nobody was sure. It was like trying to weigh a single grain of sand by weighing the whole beach and dividing. The answer that came out was a smear, not a number.

NARRATOR: Enter Robert Millikan. An American physicist, ambitious, meticulous, and by his own admission a little frustrated that at forty years old he hadn't yet made his great discovery. He took over this problem, and at first he did the same thing everyone else did. He worked with clouds of water. And he got the same disappointing smear. But then he did what the very best experimenters do. Instead of fighting the messiness of the whole cloud, he asked a different question. What if I stop looking at the crowd, and follow just one droplet? What if I could isolate a single, tiny bead, and watch it, alone, for as long as I liked?

QUESTION: But water keeps evaporating. So how do you make a droplet that lasts?

NARRATOR: This is the beautiful, almost accidental leap. Millikan swapped the water for oil. Oil doesn't evaporate away in seconds. A droplet of oil, once made, just hangs there, stable, patient, keeping its size and its mass for as long as you need. And to make those droplets, he borrowed a perfectly ordinary piece of technology from the age, a common perfume atomiser, the little rubber bulb you squeeze to spray scent. Squeeze it, and it bursts the oil into a mist of astonishingly fine droplets, some of them a thousandth of a millimetre across. He sprayed this mist into a chamber. And crucially, the very act of spraying, the friction of the oil tearing apart into droplets, left many of them electrically charged. Now he had exactly what he needed. Single, stable, charged specks of oil, drifting in the dark.

NARRATOR: Let me describe the apparatus, because its simplicity is the whole point. Two horizontal metal plates, one above the other, with a gap of air between them. Above the top plate, the atomiser puffs its mist of oil, and a few of the droplets drift down through a tiny hole into the space between the plates. Millikan shines a bright light in from the side, and peers through a small telescope. And there, against the darkness, the droplets glitter like tiny stars, each one catching the light. He can watch a single one, all by itself.

NARRATOR: Now comes the first act, and it's pure, clean physics. Millikan leaves the plates switched off. No electric field. So a chosen droplet simply falls under gravity. But it does not speed up forever. As it falls through the air, the air pushes back, a drag force, air resistance. And the faster the droplet moves, the harder the air resists. So the drop accelerates, the drag grows, until the upward drag exactly balances the downward pull of gravity. At that instant the forces cancel, the acceleration stops, and the droplet falls at a steady, unchanging speed. Physicists call this the terminal velocity. Skydivers reach it. Raindrops reach it. And this microscopic bead of oil reaches it too, gliding downward at a constant crawl.

QUESTION: And why would Millikan care about that falling speed?

NARRATOR: Because that falling speed is a scale. It weighs the droplet. There's a law, worked out by a physicist named Stokes, that connects the drag force on a tiny sphere to its size and its speed and the thickness, the viscosity, of the air it's moving through. You don't need the equation for the exam. What matters is the logic. Millikan had already carefully measured the viscosity of his air before the experiment even began. He could time the droplet falling a known distance through his telescope and get its terminal velocity. And from those, Stokes's law handed him the radius of the droplet, its size. And once you know the size of a sphere of oil, and you know the density of oil, you know its volume, and so you know its mass. Follow that chain. He just weighed a single, invisible droplet of oil, one he could never touch, simply by timing how fast it drifted down through still air. That is the first triumph, and he had to do it individually, droplet by droplet, because no two were the same size.

NARRATOR: Now the second act. Now he switches on the plates. He connects a high voltage across them, making the bottom plate positive and the top plate negative, so there is now a strong electric field filling the gap. And remember, his droplet is carrying a charge. So the field grabs it. Suddenly there is a new force in play, an electric force, pulling the droplet upward against gravity. And Millikan, watching through his telescope, delicately turns a dial and adjusts the voltage. Up a little, down a little. Until the droplet, which had been drifting down, slows, and stops, and hangs there. Perfectly still. Suspended in mid-air. Motionless. A single bead of oil, held in the void by nothing but the tug of war between gravity pulling it down and electricity pushing it up.

NARRATOR: And that stillness is everything. Because when the droplet hangs perfectly motionless, the physics becomes almost childishly simple. It's not moving, so there's no air resistance to worry about, no drag to untangle. There are only two forces, and they must be exactly equal and opposite. Gravity down. The electric force up. Balanced. The weight of the droplet, its mass times gravity, equals the electric force, which is its charge times the strength of the field. And Millikan already knew the mass, he'd measured it in act one. He knew the field, because he'd set the voltage and measured the gap between the plates himself. Every quantity in that balance was known, except one. The charge on the droplet. So he simply rearranged and solved for it. For the first time, the charge on a single, specific object was sitting there as a hard number.

QUESTION: But wait. That's the charge on a whole droplet, not on one electron. So how does that help?

NARRATOR: This is the crux, and it's where patience becomes genius. When Millikan sprayed his oil, each droplet had picked up some charge, but he had no idea how much, or how many electrons' worth. One droplet might carry the charge of three extra electrons, another eight, another fifteen. He had no way to know. So he didn't try to know. Instead, he did the experiment again. And again. And again. Hundreds of times. Different droplets, different sizes, different charges. And to shake things up further, he'd blast the chamber with high-energy X-rays, which knocked electrons on and off the droplets mid-flight, letting him watch a single droplet suddenly jump to a new charge as its cargo of electrons changed. And every single time, he calculated the charge, and he wrote it down.

NARRATOR: Now imagine laying out that whole list of charges, hundreds of them, and staring at it. If charge were smooth, like water, those numbers should be scattered all over the place, any value at all, a continuous mush. But that is not what Millikan saw. The numbers were not random. They clustered. Every charge he ever measured turned out to be a whole-number multiple of one particular tiny amount. Never a value in between. It was as if every droplet's charge could only ever be one lump, or two lumps, or three lumps, or seven lumps, of the same fundamental piece, but never one and a half lumps. Never two and a bit. The charges came in steps. And the size of the step, the smallest brick that every single measurement was built from, was about one point six times ten to the minus nineteen coulombs.

NARRATOR: Sit with what that means. Charge is not a smooth fluid. Charge is grainy. It comes in indivisible packets, and there is a smallest possible amount, and you can never, ever have less than that or any fraction of it. Charge is quantised. And that smallest brick, that fundamental unit, that has to be the charge on a single electron. Because the electron is the thing being added and removed, one at a time. Millikan hadn't just measured a number. He had caught nature counting on its fingers. He had proven that at the deepest level, electricity is not a flow of something continuous, it is a procession of individual, identical particles, each carrying exactly the same tiny charge.

NARRATOR: And now, remember the locked door. Thomson's ratio, charge divided by mass, that maddening tangle nobody could pull apart. Millikan had just found the charge, on its own, standing alone. So you take Thomson's ratio, and you divide the charge back out of it, and out drops the other piece. The mass of the electron. A number so small it's about nine times ten to the minus thirty-one kilograms, a mass so tiny you would need to gather more than a thousand billion billion billion electrons just to make a single kilogram. Two experiments, two men, and finally the electron stood fully revealed, its charge and its mass both known. The key had turned in the lock.

NARRATOR: Millikan's first published value was close, but not perfect, and over the following decades others refined it, chasing the digits further and further, settling toward one point six zero two times ten to the minus nineteen coulombs, one of the most precisely known numbers in all of science. But the story that mattered was already told. In nineteen twenty-three, Robert Millikan was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics, in large part for those patient hours hunched over a telescope, watching flecks of oil hang in the dark. He had taken the most abstract question imaginable, what is the smallest piece of electric charge in the universe, and answered it with a perfume sprayer, two metal plates, and the stubborn refusal to look away from a single droplet.

NARRATOR: And that is the quiet lesson underneath the whole thing. Sometimes the deepest secret of the universe isn't hiding in the biggest, most expensive machine. Sometimes it's hanging right in front of you, in a single bead of oil, waiting for someone willing to hold still and count.
