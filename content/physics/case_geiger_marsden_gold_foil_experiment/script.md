NARRATOR: This is part of Module 8, From the Universe to the Atom. And it begins with the most astonishing thing that ever happened to a man who fired bullets at a sheet of gold. Imagine you take a rifle, and you aim it at a single sheet of tissue paper. You pull the trigger. And a bullet comes back and hits you in the face. That is not a metaphor. That is very nearly what happened, at the atomic scale, in a small darkened room in Manchester, England, more than a hundred years ago. And when it happened, one of the greatest physicists who ever lived said it was quite the most incredible event of his entire life.

QUESTION: How could a bullet bounce straight back off a sheet of tissue paper?

NARRATOR: It can't. Not if you understand what the tissue paper is made of. And that is exactly the point. For that bullet to come back, everything scientists thought they knew about the inside of an atom had to be wrong. Not slightly wrong. Catastrophically, beautifully wrong. So let me take you back to the world before that moment, so you can feel how strange it was.

NARRATOR: At the start of the twentieth century, everybody agreed on one thing: atoms exist, and atoms are tiny. What nobody could see was what was inside one. You cannot put an atom under a microscope. It is far, far too small. So physicists had to do something cleverer. They had to reason about the inside of a thing they could never look at. And the best mind of the age, a man named J. J. Thomson, had already made a spectacular discovery. He had found the electron, a tiny particle of negative charge, thousands of times lighter than the atom itself. That won him a place in history. But it also created a puzzle.

QUESTION: If atoms contain little negative electrons, where is all the positive charge?

NARRATOR: Because atoms, overall, are neutral. They have no net charge. So if you pull out these little negative electrons, something in there must be positive to balance them. Thomson pictured it like this. Imagine a soft ball of positive charge, spread out smoothly and evenly through the whole volume of the atom, like a warm sphere of dough. And stuck inside that dough, scattered through it, are the tiny negative electrons, like currants in a pudding. The British physicists of the day actually called it the plum pudding model. A gentle, spongy, uniform blob, with the positive charge smeared thinly everywhere and the electrons dotted about inside it. And for a while, that picture seemed perfectly reasonable. There was no reason to doubt it.

NARRATOR: But over in Manchester, there was a New Zealand farm boy turned physicist named Ernest Rutherford, and Rutherford had a new kind of tool. He had discovered that certain radioactive materials fire out tiny projectiles, which he called alpha particles. An alpha particle is small, it is positively charged, and above all it is fast and it is heavy, at least as atomic things go. Rutherford had a genius insight. If you want to know what is inside something you cannot see, throw something at it and watch how it bounces. The alpha particle was his bullet. The atom was his target. And how the bullets scattered would betray the secret of what the atom was made of.

NARRATOR: This wasn't the famous experiment yet. There were years of build-up. Back in nineteen oh six, and again in nineteen oh eight, Rutherford and his assistant Hans Geiger had been firing alpha particles at thin foils and watching what happened. Geiger built a beautiful little apparatus for it. Picture a long tube. At one end sits the radioactive source, spitting out alpha particles. In the middle there is a narrow slit, to shape the particles into a fine beam. And at the far end there is a screen coated in a phosphorescent material, so that every time a single alpha particle smacks into it, the screen gives off a tiny flash of light, a scintillation, at the exact spot it hit. Then you sit in the dark with a microscope and you count the flashes, one by one, by eye.

QUESTION: What did the beam do when there was nothing in its way?

NARRATOR: When Geiger pumped all the air out of the tube, the alpha particles flew dead straight through the slit and made a tight, sharp little patch of flashes on the screen, exactly where you would expect. Clean. Predictable. But then he slid a thin sheet of gold foil into the beam's path, and something shifted. The sharp patch of flashes went soft. It spread out. It became diffuse. The gold was nudging the alpha particles slightly off course, scattering them by small angles. So matter, it turned out, could deflect these particles a little. Rutherford, thinking about it, wrote a line that turns out to be prophetic. He said such a result brings out clearly the fact that the atoms of matter must be the seat of very intense electrical forces.

NARRATOR: Intense electrical forces. Hold onto that phrase. Because now we arrive at nineteen oh nine, and the experiment that matters, the one the whole story has been building toward. Rutherford had a hunch, a strange, almost throwaway idea, and he handed it to a young undergraduate student working under Geiger, a twenty year old named Ernest Marsden. Rutherford suggested something that, by the theory of the day, was almost a waste of an afternoon. He said, go and look for alpha particles that bounce backwards. Look for particles that come off the gold foil on the same side they went in.

QUESTION: Why was that request almost absurd?

NARRATOR: Because in the plum pudding model, it should have been impossible. Think it through. In Thomson's atom, the positive charge is smeared out thin and even across the whole volume. There is no concentrated lump of it anywhere. So when a heavy, fast alpha particle comes barrelling through, the gentle, spread-out positive dough can only give it the tiniest of nudges. The forces are weak and they point in all directions and they mostly cancel out. Firing an alpha particle at a plum pudding atom should be like firing a cannonball through a cloud of mist. It sails straight on through, barely wavering. The idea that a whole cloud of mist could throw the cannonball back where it came from was, frankly, ridiculous. Which is exactly why Rutherford wanted someone to check.

NARRATOR: So Geiger and Marsden built the apparatus. A source of alpha particles, a thin sheet of gold foil, and a movable phosphorescent screen with a microscope, arranged so they could hunt for flashes not just behind the foil but off to the sides and even back in front of it, on the source's side. Then they turned out the lights. And here is a detail that I never want you to forget, because it is the human heart of this whole story. There was no digital counter. There was no photograph. To gather this data, Marsden had to sit in a completely dark room for long stretches, letting his eyes adjust, and then stare through a microscope watching for individual pinpricks of light, each one a single atomic particle striking the screen, and count them, by hand, one flash at a time. It was slow, exhausting, eye-straining work in the pitch black. And that is how one of the deepest truths about matter was uncovered. In the dark, counting flickers of light.

QUESTION: So what did Marsden actually see?

NARRATOR: Most of the alpha particles did exactly what everyone expected. They went straight through the gold foil, barely deflected, and lit up the screen behind. That part was no surprise. But then, every so often, Marsden saw a flash where a flash should never be. On the wrong side. In front of the foil. An alpha particle had gone in, and come straight back out again, deflected by more than ninety degrees, sometimes flung almost directly backwards. It was rare, breathtakingly rare, roughly one particle in every eight thousand. But it happened. Again and again. And one in eight thousand is not zero. One in eight thousand is a scream.

NARRATOR: When they told Rutherford, he was thunderstruck. His own words are the most famous in all of atomic physics. He said it was quite the most incredible event that had ever happened to him in his life. It was, he said, almost as incredible as if you had fired a fifteen inch naval shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you. Sit with that image. The most massive artillery shell of the age, fired at flimsy paper, rebounding into your face. That is the scale of the impossibility. Something inside that thin gold foil was hitting back with staggering force.

QUESTION: What could possibly turn an alpha particle completely around?

NARRATOR: Rutherford spent weeks, by his own account, chewing on it. And slowly the answer assembled itself, and it demanded that the plum pudding be thrown away entirely. To reverse a fast, heavy alpha particle, you need an enormous force. Now recall your mechanics: force equals mass times acceleration, so a violent change in motion means a violent force. And the only force available here is the electrostatic repulsion between the positive alpha particle and positive charge in the atom. But Coulomb's law tells you something crucial about that repulsion. The force gets stronger the closer the two charges come, and it grows as an inverse square, so it blows up dramatically at very small distances. In the plum pudding model the positive charge is spread out, so an alpha particle can never get close to very much of it at once, and the force stays feeble. The pudding simply cannot push hard enough.

NARRATOR: So Rutherford made a radical leap. What if the positive charge is not spread out at all? What if all of it, every last bit of the atom's positive charge, and almost all of its mass, is crammed into a single, unimaginably tiny speck at the very centre? He called that speck the nucleus. And around it, far out in what is otherwise empty space, the light electrons orbit, the way the planets orbit the Sun. This one idea explains everything Marsden saw, and it explains it perfectly.

QUESTION: How does a tiny central nucleus explain both the misses and the rare hits?

NARRATOR: Think about what this new atom is really like. It is overwhelmingly empty space, with a minuscule dense core at its heart. So when you fire alpha particles at it, the vast majority sail right through the emptiness and never come near the nucleus, and those are the particles that pass almost straight through the foil, exactly as observed. But once in a great while, once in about eight thousand times, an alpha particle happens to be aimed almost dead centre at a nucleus. Now, because the positive charge is packed so tightly, the particle can get extraordinarily close to it before being turned away, and Coulomb's inverse square law means that at such tiny distances the repulsive force becomes titanic. That single concentrated punch is enough to stop the alpha particle in its tracks and hurl it straight back. The rarity of the reflections tells you how small the nucleus is. The violence of them tells you how concentrated its charge is. The plum pudding could give you neither. Rutherford's nucleus gives you both, at once.

NARRATOR: And that is the moment the modern atom was born. Not as a soft, uniform pudding, but as a vast, echoing emptiness with a fierce, dense, positive heart. Almost all the mass of everything around you, of your hand, of this table, of the whole Earth, is locked inside these vanishingly small nuclei, with the rest of the atom being essentially nothing at all. The solidity of the world is, at the deepest level, an illusion held up by force fields across empty space. And we know that, we truly know it, because a young man sat alone in a dark room in Manchester and patiently counted tiny flashes of light, and found a handful of them where none should ever have been.

NARRATOR: Rutherford, the man who split reality open with an alpha particle, once joked that all of science is either physics or stamp collecting. Whatever you make of that, this was physics at its purest. A simple question, throw something and watch it bounce, and an answer that rewrote what matter is.
