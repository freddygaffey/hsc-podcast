NARRATOR: This is part of Module Seven, The Nature of Light. And I want to start with a question that sounds almost too simple to be dangerous. You are sitting on a train. In front of your face, bolted to the wall, is an ordinary mirror. You look into it, and you see yourself, exactly as you always do. Now the train speeds up. Faster. Faster than any train has ever gone. It climbs until it is racing along at very nearly the speed of light. My question is this. Do you still see your reflection? Or does the mirror suddenly go dark, and your own face vanish from in front of you?

QUESTION: Why would a mirror stop working just because you are moving fast?

NARRATOR: That is exactly the trap. Most people, when they first hear this, feel certain the mirror keeps working. Of course you see yourself, they say. You are sitting still relative to the mirror. Nothing between you and the glass has changed. And yet, if you take the physics of the eighteen hundreds seriously, the physics that every scientist alive trusted completely, you are forced into the opposite answer. The glass goes dark. You disappear. And the fact that this cannot possibly be true is what cracked open the whole of physics.

NARRATOR: A teenager noticed it first. In the eighteen nineties, a restless, unimpressive student named Albert Einstein was drifting through school in Germany and Switzerland, disliked by teachers who found him insolent. He was sixteen years old when a daydream lodged in his mind and refused to leave. He imagined chasing a beam of light. If he could run fast enough to travel alongside it, what would he see? The light beside him would seem frozen. A wave of light standing perfectly still in space, going nowhere. And something about that frozen wave felt deeply, physically wrong. He would carry that unease for the next ten years.

QUESTION: What was so wrong about a frozen beam of light?

NARRATOR: To feel it, you have to understand what people believed about motion, and it is something you already believe too. It is called Galilean relativity, and it is just plain common sense. Velocities add up. If you are on a train moving at thirty metres per second, and you throw a ball forward at ten metres per second, then someone standing on the platform sees that ball fly past at forty metres per second. Your speed plus the ball's speed. We do this arithmetic without thinking. Now do it with light. You are on the fast train. You switch on a torch pointing forward. In your frame, the light leaves at the speed of light. But to the person on the platform, surely the light should travel at the speed of light plus the speed of your train. Faster than light itself.

NARRATOR: And that is where the mirror comes in, because it makes the contradiction personal. Go back to your seat on the near light-speed train, mirror in front of your face. For you to see your reflection, light has to leave your face, travel forward to the mirror, bounce off, and come back to your eyes. Simple. But now look at the whole scene from the platform, from someone watching the train scream past. In their frame, your face is already moving forward at almost the speed of light. And the light leaving your face, by the old common-sense rule, moves at the speed of light too. So the light and the mirror are travelling forward at almost the same speed. The light is barely gaining on the glass. It creeps toward the mirror agonisingly slowly, and by this reasoning it might take an eternity to close the gap. It can never quite catch up. The reflection never forms. To that outside observer, the mirror has gone blank.

QUESTION: But hang on, which answer is real?

NARRATOR: That is the knife edge. Because you, sitting on the train, are in a perfectly good, perfectly ordinary reference frame. You are not accelerating, you are not spinning, you are gliding smoothly. And Galileo himself had laid down a rule three centuries earlier that this frame must be just as valid as any other. The principle of relativity. It says the laws of physics are exactly the same in every inertial reference frame, in every frame moving at constant velocity. There is no experiment you can do, sealed inside a smoothly moving cabin, that will tell you how fast you are going. That is why you can pour a coffee on a cruising plane and it behaves completely normally. If that principle holds, then in your frame the mirror simply must work, the same as it does when the train is standing in the station. Physics cannot depend on how fast the train happens to be moving.

NARRATOR: So we have two answers to the same physical event, and they cannot both be true. In your frame, you see your face. In the platform's frame, the mirror is dark and your face has vanished. A reflection cannot both exist and not exist. Something in this picture is broken.

QUESTION: So where exactly is the flaw?

NARRATOR: Here Einstein reached for the deepest physics of his century, the electromagnetism of James Clerk Maxwell. In the eighteen sixties Maxwell had written down a handful of equations that unified electricity and magnetism, and buried inside them was a stunning prediction. Light is an electromagnetic wave, and it travels at a speed set by two fixed properties of empty space. The permeability of free space, and the permittivity of free space, the numbers that govern how vacuum responds to magnetism and to electric fields. The speed of light comes out as one divided by the square root of the permeability of free space times the permittivity of free space. And notice what is missing from that formula. There is no mention of who is looking. No mention of how fast anyone is moving. The equations simply hand you a speed. About three hundred million metres per second, full stop.

NARRATOR: Now the contradiction becomes sharp and unavoidable. If the platform observer measures the light crawling slowly toward the mirror, then in their frame light is not travelling at the speed Maxwell demands. Which means Maxwell's equations, the laws of electromagnetism themselves, would have to be different inside the moving train than outside it. And that shatters Galileo's principle. It would mean the laws of physics are not the same in every inertial frame after all. It would mean that by carefully watching a beam of light inside your sealed cabin, you could work out how fast you were really moving. The whole edifice of relativity, three hundred years old, comes crashing down.

NARRATOR: Einstein stared at this trap for years, and then in nineteen oh five, working nights as a clerk in a Swiss patent office, he found the only way out. And it is breathtaking, because it demands you sacrifice something you were certain of. If you insist, absolutely insist, that the laws of physics are the same in every inertial frame, if you refuse to give up Galileo's principle, then there is exactly one thing you can surrender instead. You must give up the common-sense adding of velocities. You must accept that the speed of light is not a speed that anything adds to. Every observer, no matter how they are moving, no matter how fast, measures light travelling at exactly that same speed, one divided by the square root of the permeability times the permittivity of free space.

QUESTION: So the platform observer sees the light going the full speed of light too?

NARRATOR: Yes. That is the resolution, and it is the second postulate of special relativity. The person on the platform does not see the light creeping slowly after the mirror. They see it leave your face at the full speed of light, the same speed you see, the same speed Maxwell always insisted on. The light does catch the mirror. The reflection does form. In every frame, your face appears in the glass, and the mirror works exactly as it does at rest in the station. The paradox dissolves. But the price is enormous, because if the speed of light is fixed for everyone, then the things you thought were fixed, the flow of time itself and the lengths of moving objects, are the things that must bend instead. Moving clocks run slow. Moving rulers shrink. Reality rearranges itself to keep light constant.

NARRATOR: And this is the quiet power of the whole thought experiment. It is not an experiment you run in a laboratory. There is no apparatus, no reading on a dial. It is a machine built purely out of ideas, designed to force two trusted principles into open war, Galileo's relativity against Maxwell's electromagnetism, and then to show that only one escape route survives. If you keep the first postulate, that physics is the same in all inertial frames, the second postulate is not a separate guess you bolt on. It follows inevitably. The constancy of the speed of light is the price of keeping the universe consistent for everyone.

NARRATOR: So the next time someone tells you that you could never outrun a beam of light, remember why. It is not that light is merely fast. It is that light does not play the game of adding speeds at all. However hard you chase it, it retreats from you at exactly the same speed, and the sixteen year old who first felt that a frozen light wave was impossible turned out to be exactly right.
