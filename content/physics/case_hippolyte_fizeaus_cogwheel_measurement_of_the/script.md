NARRATOR: This is part of Module Seven, The Nature of Light. And I want to start with a number that, for more than two hundred years, nobody on Earth could actually measure. Light is fast. That much everyone could see. Open your eyes at dawn and the whole valley is lit in an instant. Snuff a candle and the darkness arrives with no delay you could ever catch. So here is the question that had haunted natural philosophers since Galileo: does light take any time at all to travel? Or is it, as some of the greatest minds insisted, literally instantaneous, moving from here to there in exactly zero seconds?

QUESTION: How do you measure the speed of something that seems to arrive the very moment it leaves?

NARRATOR: That was the trap. To time something, you need it to be somewhere at one moment and somewhere else at a later moment, and you need a clock fast enough to tell the two moments apart. But light is so quick that in the time it takes a beam to cross an entire city, the fastest mechanical clock of the eighteen hundreds would not have twitched. Galileo himself had tried the obvious thing. Two men, two lanterns, two distant hilltops. One man uncovers his lantern, the second man uncovers his the instant he sees the first light, and Galileo tried to time the round trip. He got nothing. The delay he was hunting for was thousands of times shorter than human reaction time. All he measured was how slowly people flinch. And so he wrote, with real honesty, that if light was not instantaneous, it was at the very least extraordinarily fast. He couldn't pin it down.

NARRATOR: Now, astronomers had actually done better than any earthbound experimenter. Back in the sixteen seventies, a Danish astronomer named Ole Roemer had been watching the moons of Jupiter, and he noticed something strange. The little moon Io ducked in and out of Jupiter's shadow like clockwork, but the clock seemed to run late when Earth was far from Jupiter and early when Earth was close. Roemer understood why. When Earth is on the far side of its orbit, the light carrying the news of that eclipse has farther to travel, so the eclipse looks delayed. From that delay he concluded light had a finite speed, and put a rough figure on it. Later, using the whole width of Earth's orbit as a giant measuring stick, astronomers refined it. But here is the thing that gnawed at people. Every one of those measurements leaned on the heavens. On the size of planetary orbits, on the motions of distant moons, on numbers no one could check by hand. Nobody had ever measured the speed of light using nothing but apparatus you could build in a workshop and set up on a hillside. Nobody had done it on Earth.

QUESTION: Could you really catch light in the act, right here, with gears and mirrors and a candle?

NARRATOR: Enter Hippolyte Fizeau. A young Frenchman, born into wealth, trained partly in medicine before ill health turned him toward physics, working in Paris in the eighteen forties. And Fizeau had an idea so clean, so mechanical, that once you hear it you wonder why it took two hundred years. The whole problem, he saw, was the clock. Human reflexes were hopeless. Mechanical clocks were far too slow. So what if you didn't use a clock at all? What if you used the light itself, and a spinning wheel, to slice time into pieces so thin that the tiny delay of a light beam suddenly became something you could see with your own eyes?

NARRATOR: Here is the reasoning. Light travels fast, yes, but not infinitely fast. So if you send a beam out to a mirror several kilometres away and let it bounce back, there must be some delay, however small, between the light leaving and the light returning. You will never catch that delay with a stopwatch. But what if, in that sliver of time, you could make something physically get in the way? What if you could arrange for the road home to be open when the light sets out, and closed by the time it gets back? Then the light would simply vanish. It would leave, and never return to your eye. And the exact conditions under which it vanished would tell you how long the trip took.

NARRATOR: So this is what Fizeau built. He took a wheel. Not just any wheel, a precisely machined cog wheel with seven hundred and twenty teeth cut around its rim, and, crucially, the gaps between the teeth were exactly as wide as the teeth themselves. Think of it as seven hundred and twenty teeth and seven hundred and twenty gaps, alternating all the way around, tooth, gap, tooth, gap. He shone a beam of light so that it passed through one of those gaps, out into the night, all the way to a mirror on a distant hill, and back again toward the same wheel. If the wheel was standing still, the returning light would come straight back through the same gap and into his eye. Simple.

QUESTION: But what happens when you start spinning the wheel?

NARRATOR: Everything. Because now the wheel is turning while the light is away on its journey. The light darts out through a gap, races to the far mirror and back, and in that time the wheel has rotated a little. If it has rotated just slightly, the returning light comes back through a gap that has shifted a bit, and you still see it, though dimmer. But now speed the wheel up, faster and faster, until you hit the magic rotation rate. At exactly the right speed, in the time it takes the light to make the round trip, the wheel turns by precisely half a step, so that where there was an open gap when the light left, there is now a solid tooth when the light comes back. The tooth slams the door. The returning beam smashes into the back of a tooth and is blocked completely. And to Fizeau, sitting at the eyepiece, the light simply goes dark. It disappears.

NARRATOR: Let that sink in, because it is genuinely beautiful. The wheel is the clock. Not a clock with hands, but a clock made of teeth and gaps flickering past faster than any eye could follow, and Fizeau has tuned it so that one single tick of that clock is exactly as long as a beam of light's journey to a hilltop and back. When the light goes out, he has caught the speed of light in a machine you could turn with a crank.

NARRATOR: Now let's put the real numbers to it, because this is where a curious idea becomes hard physics. The mirror sat on the hill of Montmartre, and Fizeau's wheel and light source stood at his family's house in Suresnes, on the outskirts of Paris. The distance between them was eight thousand six hundred and thirty metres. So the light's round trip, out and back, was twice that. The wheel had seven hundred and twenty teeth. And Fizeau turned the crank, faster and faster, until the returning light first vanished completely, and at that moment the wheel was spinning at twelve point six turns every second.

QUESTION: How do you get the speed of light out of just those three numbers, a distance, a tooth count, and a spin rate?

NARRATOR: Follow it through, and it's lovely and simple. The speed of anything is just distance divided by time. The distance here is the round trip, out to the mirror and back, which is two times eight thousand six hundred and thirty metres. Now we need the time, and this is the clever part. The time we want is the tiny interval it takes the wheel to turn from an open gap to a covering tooth, that is, to rotate by half of one tooth-and-gap step. Around the whole wheel there are seven hundred and twenty teeth and seven hundred and twenty gaps, so one full turn is seven hundred and twenty of those tooth-plus-gap units. Half a tooth is a tiny fraction of one turn. Work out how long that takes when the wheel is spinning at twelve point six turns per second, and the time comes out to one divided by the quantity two times seven hundred and twenty times twelve point six.

NARRATOR: Now put the pieces together. Speed of light equals the round-trip distance divided by that time. Do the algebra and the whole thing collapses into something you could write on the back of your hand. The speed of light equals four times the distance, times the number of teeth, times the frequency of rotation. Four times eight thousand six hundred and thirty metres, times seven hundred and twenty teeth, times twelve point six turns per second.

NARRATOR: And the answer that fell out of that spinning wheel was about three hundred and thirteen thousand kilometres per second. Roughly three hundred and fifteen thousand, in round terms. Think about what that means. A young man in a Paris garden, with a cog wheel, a lamp, and a mirror on a hill, had just measured how fast light crosses the universe, and he had done it without ever leaving the ground. No planets. No moons of Jupiter. No borrowing numbers from the astronomers. Just gears and geometry and a little cleverness about time.

QUESTION: But wait, three hundred and thirteen thousand. Isn't the real answer closer to three hundred thousand? Did he get it wrong?

NARRATOR: He was off, yes, by about four or five per cent. The true speed of light in a vacuum is a hair under three hundred thousand kilometres per second, and Fizeau overshot. But hold on before you judge him. This was the very first time in human history that anyone had measured the speed of light on Earth, with a terrestrial apparatus, in a repeatable experiment you could set up and check. Every attempt before his, from Galileo's lanterns onward, had failed outright. Compared to two centuries of complete failure, landing within a few per cent on your first try, with a hand-cranked wheel, is not an error. It is a triumph. And it left obvious room to improve, which is exactly what happened next.

NARRATOR: Because sitting in the wings, watching this, was Fizeau's one-time collaborator and eventual rival, Léon Foucault. Where Fizeau used a toothed wheel, Foucault would soon swap it for a rapidly spinning mirror, a refinement that let him shorten the distances, tighten the measurement, and push the number down closer to the truth. And within a few years Foucault would do something even Fizeau's method couldn't easily manage. He would send the light through water instead of air and show that it slowed down, which was a killing blow for the old idea that light was a stream of particles and a victory for the theory that light is a wave. So Fizeau's cog wheel wasn't just a clever measurement. It was the starting gun. It proved that the speed of light was a real, finite, measurable thing you could pin down in a laboratory, and it opened the door to everything that followed.

NARRATOR: And this is why the experiment matters far beyond a single number in a textbook. That finite speed of light is not a footnote. It is one of the deepest facts in all of physics. A few decades later, James Clerk Maxwell would write down his equations for electricity and magnetism, and out of the pure mathematics a speed would appear, the speed of an electromagnetic wave, and it would match the speed Fizeau and his successors had measured. That is how we learned that light is an electromagnetic wave. And half a century after that, a patent clerk named Albert Einstein would take that speed, that fixed, unchanging speed of light, and build his entire theory of relativity on top of it, reshaping our ideas of space and time themselves. All of it rests on the simple, radical claim that light has a speed. A speed you can catch. A speed a Frenchman first cornered in a garden with a spinning wheel of seven hundred and twenty teeth.

NARRATOR: So the next time you flick on a light and the room fills instantly, remember, it isn't instant. The light took a real, countable slice of time to reach your eyes, and the first person to ever prove that on Earth did it by racing a beam of light against the teeth of a wheel, and winning.
