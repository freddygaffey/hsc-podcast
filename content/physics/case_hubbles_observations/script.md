NARRATOR: This is part of Module 8, From the Universe to the Atom. And it opens with a question that sounds almost childish until you actually try to answer it. How big is the universe? Because a little over a century ago, if you had put that question to the finest astronomers alive, the best-trained, best-equipped minds on the planet, most of them would have handed you an answer that was wrong. Not wrong by a little. Wrong by more than a hundred billion times.

NARRATOR: In the early nineteen hundreds, the universe had an edge, and that edge was the Milky Way. Our galaxy, this enormous disc of stars, was thought to be everything there was. The whole of creation was a single island of light drifting in an infinite, empty dark, and outside it there was simply nothing. No other galaxies. No other islands. Just us.

QUESTION: So how did anyone even start to doubt that?

NARRATOR: Because of some faint, fuzzy smudges. When astronomers pointed their telescopes at the night sky, alongside the sharp points of stars they kept finding little clouds of light. They called them spiral nebulae, spiral because many of them had a beautiful whirlpool shape. And nobody could agree on what they were. Were they clouds of glowing gas floating inside our own Milky Way, just part of the furniture of our galaxy? Or were they something far stranger, entire other galaxies, so absurdly far away that all their billions of stars blurred into a single smudge?

NARRATOR: This argument got so heated it earned a name. Historians call it the Great Debate. In nineteen twenty, two respected astronomers stood up in front of a hall in Washington and argued opposite sides of it, and at the end of the night nobody had won, because nobody had the one thing that could settle it. Nobody knew how far away those smudges were. Distance was the whole game. If the spiral nebulae were near, the Milky Way was the universe. If they were far, the universe was almost unimaginably bigger than anyone dared to think. And measuring the distance to a faint smudge of light is one of the hardest things in all of astronomy.

QUESTION: Why is distance so hard to measure in space?

NARRATOR: Think about looking at a light on a dark night. A dim light close to you and a brilliant light far away can look exactly the same to your eye. Brightness alone tells you nothing, because you can't separate how much light something truly gives off from how far that light has had to travel. To get distance, you need a special kind of object, one where you already know the true brightness. Astronomers call these standard candles. If you know how bright something really is, and you measure how dim it looks, the dimming tells you the distance. And it just so happened that a discovery had already quietly handed the world exactly such a candle.

NARRATOR: The discovery came from a woman named Henrietta Leavitt, working at Harvard, painstakingly measuring the brightness of stars on photographic plates. She was studying a class of star called a Cepheid variable. These are stars that pulse, that swell and shrink, brightening and dimming on a steady rhythm, like a lighthouse turning. And Leavitt noticed something extraordinary. The slower a Cepheid pulsed, the more light it truly gave off. The rhythm told you the true brightness. Which meant a Cepheid was a perfect standard candle. Time how long it takes to pulse, and you know exactly how much light it pours out. Compare that to how faint it looks, and you have your distance, a cosmic tape measure written into the flicker of a star.

NARRATOR: Now enter the man whose name is on this episode. Edwin Hubble. A tall, pipe-smoking American who had trained as a lawyer and boxed as an amateur before deciding the law was too dull and the sky was more interesting. In the nineteen twenties he was given the keys to the most powerful instrument humanity had ever built, the Hooker telescope on Mount Wilson in California, with a mirror one hundred inches across. It was the largest telescope in the world, and it could gather enough light to pick out individual stars inside those fuzzy smudges.

NARRATOR: On a cold night, Hubble aimed that giant eye at the largest and nearest of the spiral nebulae, the one in the constellation Andromeda, and he hunted, night after night, plate after plate, for a single Cepheid variable buried in the haze. And he found one. He watched it pulse. He timed its rhythm. He worked out its true brightness from Leavitt's law, compared it to how heartbreakingly faint it looked, and calculated the distance. The number that came out changed everything.

QUESTION: How far away was it?

NARRATOR: Something on the order of a million light years. By modern measurements Andromeda sits about two and a half million light years away. Either way, the answer was catastrophic for the old picture, because the entire Milky Way is only about a hundred thousand light years across. Andromeda wasn't inside our galaxy. It wasn't near the edge of it. It was far, far outside it, a separate island of hundreds of billions of stars, a whole other galaxy in its own right. In a single stroke, the universe stopped being one galaxy and became a place scattered with countless galaxies, stretching out further than anyone had imagined. Hubble had blown the walls off the cosmos.

NARRATOR: And if the story ended there, it would already be one of the great moments in the history of science. But Hubble was only getting started. Because now that he knew these were distant galaxies, he could ask a second question, and the answer to that one would rewrite our understanding of time itself.

NARRATOR: The second clue came from light, and specifically from a trick of light called the spectrum. When you spread the light from a star or galaxy out into its colours, like a rainbow through a prism, you don't get a smooth band. You get a pattern of dark lines, gaps at very specific colours where particular atoms, hydrogen, calcium, and the rest, have absorbed their own signature wavelengths. That pattern of lines is a fingerprint. And crucially, it's a fingerprint we can reproduce in a laboratory here on Earth, so we know exactly where each line is supposed to sit.

QUESTION: So what happens when a galaxy is moving?

NARRATOR: The whole fingerprint shifts. If a source of light is racing away from you, the waves get stretched out on their journey, and stretching light means shifting it towards longer wavelengths, towards the red end of the spectrum. We call that redshift. If instead the source were rushing towards you, the light would be squeezed towards the blue. It's the same reason an ambulance siren drops in pitch as it screams past you, the sound waves bunching up as it approaches and stretching out as it recedes. Light does the same thing. The size of the shift tells you the speed. A big redshift means a galaxy tearing away from us at tremendous speed.

NARRATOR: An astronomer named Vesto Slipher had already been measuring these shifts, and he'd noticed something odd, almost every galaxy he looked at was redshifted. Almost every galaxy was moving away from us. That alone was strange. But Slipher didn't know the distances. Hubble did. And Hubble did the one thing that turns a curiosity into a law. He took his distances, worked out from Cepheids and other standard candles, and he plotted them against the speeds worked out from the redshifts. Distance on one axis. Speed on the other. And he looked at the pattern.

QUESTION: What did the pattern show?

NARRATOR: A straight line. The dots didn't scatter randomly. They marched up and to the right in a clean, unmistakable trend. The further away a galaxy was, the faster it was flying away from us. Twice as far, twice as fast. Ten times as far, ten times as fast. The speed of recession was simply proportional to the distance. Today we write that relationship as a single elegant statement, the velocity of a galaxy equals a constant multiplied by its distance, and we call that constant the Hubble constant, and the whole relationship Hubble's law. He published it in nineteen twenty-nine.

NARRATOR: Now, take a breath, because you have to sit with what this means, and it is genuinely one of the most mind-bending conclusions in physics. Everything is rushing away from us, and the further it is, the faster it flees. Your first instinct is that we must be at the centre of some giant explosion, with everything blasting outward from us. But that's not it at all. That's the wrong picture.

QUESTION: If everything's running from us, aren't we the centre?

NARRATOR: No, and here's the beautiful part. Imagine a loaf of raisin bread rising in the oven. As the dough expands, every raisin gets further from every other raisin. Pick any raisin and look around, and you'll see all the other raisins moving away from you, and the distant ones moving away fastest, because there's more expanding dough between you and them. Every raisin sees exactly the same thing. There is no centre raisin. It isn't that the galaxies are flying through space away from a central point. It's that space itself is expanding, swelling everywhere at once, carrying the galaxies along with it like raisins in the dough. That is what Hubble's straight line was really telling us. The universe is not static. The universe is growing.

NARRATOR: And once you know that, you can do something no scientist had ever been able to do with any authority. You can run the film backwards. If the universe is expanding, if everything is flying apart, then yesterday everything was a little closer together, and a million years ago closer still, and if you keep winding the clock back, all the galaxies, all the matter, everything, comes crashing together into a single unimaginably hot, dense point. A beginning. There was a moment when it all started. We call it the Big Bang, and it is Hubble's expanding universe, run in reverse, that first pointed straight at it.

NARRATOR: You can even squeeze an age out of the numbers. The Hubble constant tells you how fast the universe is expanding, and if you effectively divide one by that rate, you get a rough estimate of how long the expansion has been going on, how old the universe is. Do it with the best modern value, around seventy kilometres per second for every megaparsec of distance, and you land on something close to fourteen billion years. For the first time in human history, someone could put a number, a real, measured number, on the age of everything.

NARRATOR: Now, a note of honesty, because good science is honest. Hubble's very first value for his constant was wildly too big, off by roughly a factor of seven, which would have made the universe younger than the Earth, an obvious contradiction. His distances were rough, his sample was small, and the calibration of those standard candles has been refined again and again in the century since. But that doesn't diminish what he did. The exact slope of the line took decades to pin down. The existence of the line, the fact that recession speed climbs steadily with distance, that was Hubble's, and it has stood ever since.

NARRATOR: So think about the distance travelled here, in every sense. In just a handful of years, one man with a telescope took us from a universe that was a single galaxy, static and eternal, to a universe of countless galaxies, expanding, and with a birthday. He did it with two simple, powerful tools, a pulsing star that told him how far, and a shifted fingerprint of light that told him how fast. Distance and speed, plotted against each other, and the answer was the origin of the cosmos.

NARRATOR: The next time you look up at that faint smudge in Andromeda, remember that its light left on its journey towards you around two and a half million years ago, before our own species really existed, and that it is, very slowly, moving away, carried on the expanding tide of space. That smudge is the reason we know the universe had a beginning.
