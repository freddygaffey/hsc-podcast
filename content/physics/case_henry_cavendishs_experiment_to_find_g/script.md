---

NARRATOR: This is part of Module 5, Advanced Mechanics. And I want to start with a question that sounds absurd, because for most of human history it was absurd. How much does the Earth weigh? Not how big it is. Not how far away the Sun is. How much does the entire planet, the ground under your feet, the oceans, the molten heart of it, actually weigh? In the seventeen hundreds, nobody on Earth could tell you. Not the best astronomers, not the greatest mathematicians. And here is the strange part. They had known Isaac Newton's law of universal gravitation for over a century. They had the equation that governs the falling apple and the orbiting Moon. And they still could not weigh the world they were standing on.

QUESTION: How can you have the law of gravity and still not know the mass of the Earth?

NARRATOR: Because Newton's law hides a trap. It says the force of gravity between two objects is the gravitational constant, times the first mass, times the second mass, divided by the distance between them squared. Look at that equation and count the unknowns. There is the constant, which everyone just called big G. And there is the mass of the Earth. Two mysteries, tangled together in a single equation. When you drop a stone, gravity pulls it down, sure. But that pull depends on big G multiplied by the mass of the Earth, as one lump. You can measure how fast the stone falls all day long, and all you ever learn is that product, the two unknowns stuck together. You can never pull them apart. It was like knowing that two numbers multiply to give twelve, and being asked, which two? Three and four? Two and six? The falling stone simply refuses to say.

NARRATOR: So astronomers were stuck. They could describe the entire clockwork of the heavens, predict eclipses, chart the planets, and yet the most basic number of all, the strength of gravity itself, big G, was a blank. And without it, the mass of the Earth was a blank. The mass of the Sun, a blank. Every planet, every moon, all unknown, because they all leaned on that one missing constant. If you could find big G by itself, just once, on a tabletop, the whole cosmos would suddenly have a bathroom scale.

QUESTION: So how do you measure the pull of gravity between two things you can actually hold?

NARRATOR: That is the impossible-sounding part. Gravity is staggeringly weak. We only feel it at all because the Earth is enormous. The gravitational pull between two lead balls sitting on a table is so faint it is almost a joke, thousands of millions of times weaker than the tug of a small magnet. To measure it, you would need to detect a force gentler than the weight of a falling eyelash. And in seventeen ninety eight, a painfully shy English aristocrat named Henry Cavendish decided he was going to do exactly that.

NARRATOR: Cavendish was one of the strangest figures in the history of science. Fabulously wealthy, so terrified of people that he had a separate staircase built just to avoid meeting his own servants, and communicated with his cooks by leaving notes. But behind those closed doors he was one of the most meticulous experimenters who ever lived. And the machine he built to catch gravity in the act was a thing of beautiful patience. It was called a torsion balance.

NARRATOR: Picture a light wooden rod, like a slender dumbbell, hanging horizontally from a single thin fibre. On each end of the rod sits a small lead sphere. Now the trick is in that fibre. A torsion balance works because the fibre resists being twisted. Twist it a little, and it pushes back a little. Twist it more, and it pushes back harder, exactly in proportion. The restoring torque equals a constant of the wire, times the angle you have twisted it through. That wire constant, Cavendish could work out beforehand, by watching how the balance slowly swung back and forth and timing its rhythm. So the fibre becomes a measuring stick for the tiniest of forces. The faintest sideways nudge on those spheres, and the whole rod slowly, silently rotates.

NARRATOR: Then comes the gravity. Cavendish brought two much larger lead balls, each one a heavy weight, and positioned them close beside the small spheres, one on each side, arranged to pull the dumbbell around. And gravity did what gravity does. The big masses attracted the small masses. The rod turned, a whisper of a rotation, twisting the fibre until the gravitational torque exactly balanced the restoring torque of the wire. At that stalled point, the two were equal. The pull of gravity, on one side of the equation. The known stiffness of the wire times the measured angle, on the other. Set them equal, and rearrange, and everything falls out except the one thing he was hunting. Big G, standing alone at last.

QUESTION: But the rod barely moved. How could he even see a twist that small?

NARRATOR: With light. Cavendish fixed a tiny mirror to the balance, shone a beam onto it, and let the reflection land on a scale far across the room. Rotate the mirror by a hair, and the far end of the light beam sweeps across a wide arc. The apparatus multiplied a movement too small to see into a visible glide of light along a ruler. And to keep the faint breath of a draught, or the warmth of his own body, from swamping the measurement, he sealed the whole thing in a case and observed it from another room entirely, through a telescope. He was that careful. He had to be. He was measuring a force barely stronger than nothing.

NARRATOR: And it worked. From the twist of a wire and a trembling spot of light, Henry Cavendish extracted the value of big G. Converted into the units we use today, he got about six point seven four times ten to the minus eleven, in metres cubed per kilogram per second squared. Now hold that number up against the value your syllabus uses today, six point six seven times ten to the minus eleven. Cavendish, in seventeen ninety eight, with wood and wire and candlelight, landed within about one percent of the answer we accept over two hundred years later.

NARRATOR: And the moment he had big G, the trap sprang open. Remember, gravity had always handed us big G multiplied by the mass of the Earth as one inseparable lump. Now Cavendish knew big G on its own. He could divide it out. And out came the mass of the Earth. For the first time in history, a human being had weighed the planet. He had, quite literally, weighed the world, and he had done it on a table in a sealed room, by watching two lead balls lean ever so slightly toward two others.

NARRATOR: Cavendish himself was actually chasing the density of the Earth, and what he found was telling. The planet was far denser than its rocks and crust, dense enough to hint that something heavy, like iron, was hiding at its core. But the real prize was that single constant. Big G is the number that turns Newton's beautiful law from a shape into a scale. With it, we can weigh the Sun, weigh distant planets, weigh things we will never touch, just by watching how their gravity moves other objects. One shy man, one twisting wire, and suddenly the whole universe could be put on the scales.

NARRATOR: When you meet Newton's law of universal gravitation in Advanced Mechanics, remember that the little constant G in front of it is not a given. It was caught, once, in a sealed room, by the faint twist of a wire.
