NARRATOR: This is part of Module 8, From the Universe to the Atom. Picture a darkened laboratory in the eighteen seventies. On the bench sits a sealed glass tube, most of the air sucked out of it, with a wire sealed into each end and a monstrous voltage strung across them. You throw the switch, and the far end of the glass blooms with an eerie green light. Something is streaming across the empty space inside that tube, from one wire to the other. But here is the question that split the greatest scientists in Europe for nearly thirty years, a question people got confidently, embarrassingly wrong. What, exactly, is that something? Is it a wave, like light itself? Or is it a hail of tiny flying particles? And how could anyone possibly tell the difference by staring at a glowing tube?

NARRATOR: To feel why this was so hard, you have to understand what people already believed. By the middle of the nineteenth century, physicists thought they had the universe more or less mapped. There were two great categories of stuff. There was matter, made of atoms, which everyone agreed were the smallest, indivisible, uncuttable building blocks of everything. And there were waves, like light, ripples in some invisible medium. Everything was one or the other. So when this strange glow appeared inside an evacuated tube, the German physicists mostly said, waves, some new kind of light in the ether. The British physicists mostly said, particles, some kind of charged projectile. Both camps were staring at the exact same green glow, and both were certain the other side was foolish.

QUESTION: So how do you even get a beam to appear inside a sealed glass tube in the first place?

NARRATOR: It comes down to two clever tricks, and a lot of voltage. The first trick is how you launch anything at all. You take a piece of metal, the cathode, and you heat it, sometimes to between six hundred and two thousand degrees Celsius. Heat is just jiggling, and if you jiggle the electrons in that metal hard enough, some of them gain enough energy to tear free of the surface entirely and escape into the space around the metal. That process has a name. It is called thermionic emission. Now you have loose charges hanging in the void, waiting.

NARRATOR: The second trick is how you get them moving fast. You place a second metal plate at the far end, the anode, and you connect a colossal voltage across the two. And where did that voltage come from in the eighteen hundreds, long before the wall socket? From an induction coil, which is really just a transformer. A primary coil with only a few turns of wire sits wrapped inside a secondary coil with up to ten thousand times as many turns. You rapidly switch the current in the primary on and off with a mechanical interrupter, and that flickering magnetic field induces an enormous voltage in the secondary. Rectify that into a steady one-directional push, and you have a huge potential difference across your tube.

QUESTION: And what does that enormous voltage actually do to one of those freed electrons?

NARRATOR: It hurls it. A voltage across two plates sets up an electric field between them, and the strength of that field is just the voltage divided by the gap, the letter vee over the letter dee. The force on a charge sitting in that field is the charge times the field, so the force is the charge times the voltage, divided by the gap. And Newton tells us force equals mass times acceleration, so the acceleration of that little charge is its charge times the voltage, divided by its mass times the gap. Here is the punchline hidden in that expression. The charge-to-mass ratio of these things is fantastically large, and the voltage-to-gap ratio is fantastically large too. Multiply two enormous numbers and you get a truly ferocious acceleration. The freed charge is flung from the cathode toward the anode at a staggering speed.

NARRATOR: But there is one more thing you need, or the whole show collapses. Air. In an ordinary room the space between the plates is packed with air molecules, and a fast charge would smash into them almost immediately, losing most of its energy in the collisions before it got anywhere. So you have to empty the tube. This is exactly why the discovery had to wait. It waited on a pump. When Heinrich Geissler perfected a vacuum pump that could evacuate a tube down to around one hundredth of one percent of normal atmospheric pressure, suddenly the charges could fly the length of the tube almost unobstructed. The space began to conduct, and the beam properly formed. Because these rays streamed out of the cathode, people gave them the plainest possible name. Cathode rays.

QUESTION: If the tube is nearly empty, where does that beautiful glow even come from?

NARRATOR: From the tiny amount of gas that is left. The tube is not perfectly empty, and the flying charges are moving so fast that when one does slam into a leftover gas molecule, it carries enough kinetic energy to knock one of that molecule's own electrons up to a higher energy level, or to rip it off the atom entirely. That stolen kinetic energy is now stored as electrical potential energy in the disturbed atom. But atoms do not like sitting in that excited state. A moment later the electron falls back down to where it belongs, and as it falls it dumps the borrowed energy back out as a flash of light, a photon. Multiply that by countless collisions every second and you get the steady, ghostly glow. If that sounds familiar, it should. It is almost exactly the electron jumping between energy levels in Bohr's model of the atom. The only difference is that here the kick that lifts the electron does not come from an incoming photon. It comes from the raw kinetic energy of a cathode ray slamming into the atom.

QUESTION: So back to the great argument. Wave or particle?

NARRATOR: This is where the tube gives up its secret. Bring a magnet near the beam, and the glowing streak bends. Change the strength of the magnet, and the amount it bends changes right along with it. Waves of light do not care about magnets. Charged particles do. And crucially, the direction the beam swerved was exactly the direction you would expect for a negative charge. Whatever cathode rays were made of, they were not some mysterious new light. They were streams of something carrying negative electric charge, something that could be pushed around by fields, something with mass and momentum. The particle camp was right.

NARRATOR: And that is the quiet earthquake buried in this glowing tube. Remember the belief we started with, that the atom was the smallest, indivisible thing there was. Here was a beam of negatively charged pieces, and where were they coming from? They were being boiled straight out of a lump of solid metal. Something smaller than the atom, hiding inside the atom, and it could be pulled out and made to fly across a room. The uncuttable had been cut. These little negative pieces are what we now call electrons, and this humble glowing tube is where humanity first laid eyes on a piece of the inside of the atom.

NARRATOR: To pin down exactly what these particles were, how heavy, how charged, someone would soon have to measure them properly, and that is where J. J. Thomson and his charge-to-mass experiment take the stage. That is the story to follow next.
