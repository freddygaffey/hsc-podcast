NARRATOR: This is part of Module 8, From the Universe to the Atom. And I want to start with a confession that most physicists in the nineteen sixties would have made, quietly, if you'd pressed them. They believed the proton was a dot. A perfect, featureless point of positive charge. The smallest, simplest brick in the universe. If you'd asked one of them what a proton is made of, the honest answer was: nothing. It isn't made of anything. It just is.

NARRATOR: They were wrong. And the strange thing is, the machine that proved them wrong was built to do something else entirely, and the men running it spent months arguing about whether their own results were a mistake.

QUESTION: So how do you find the inside of something everyone insists has no inside?

NARRATOR: You throw things at it. That is genuinely the whole trick, and it is one of the oldest tricks in physics. If you cannot open something up, you fire something small and fast at it, and you watch how the pieces bounce. The way they scatter tells you the shape of what you cannot see. It is like rolling marbles under a curtain and figuring out, from the clicks and the ricochets, exactly what furniture is hiding behind it.

NARRATOR: Half a century earlier, a physicist named Ernest Rutherford had done exactly this to the atom. In the Geiger and Marsden experiment, they fired heavy, positively charged alpha particles at a whisper-thin sheet of gold foil. They expected the alphas to sail almost straight through, barely nudged, because the going theory said the atom was a soft, spread-out pudding of charge. Instead, every so often, an alpha particle came screaming back almost the way it came, as if it had hit a wall. Rutherford said it was as incredible as firing a shell at tissue paper and having it bounce back and hit you. That single violent bounce told him the atom was almost entirely empty space, with a tiny, dense, hard core at its centre. The nucleus. That is how the nucleus was discovered: not by seeing it, but by watching things bounce off it.

NARRATOR: Now hold that image, because the story I want to tell you is the exact same experiment, one level deeper. This time the wall isn't the atom. It's the proton itself.

NARRATOR: To understand why anyone would even try, you have to feel how confident physics was about the proton. By the middle of the nineteen sixties, it was one of the tidiest particles they had. It had a definite mass, a definite charge, it was stable, it sat in the heart of every atom. There was no reason to think it had parts. And there was a second, quieter thing going on. A physicist named Murray Gell-Mann had proposed, on paper, that particles like the proton might be built from three even smaller things. He gave them a deliberately silly name. Quarks. But even Gell-Mann treated them, at first, as more of a bookkeeping trick than real objects. A mathematical convenience. Nobody had ever seen one. Nobody expected to, because, and this is the part that made quarks feel like a cheat, no matter how hard anyone smashed protons together, a single quark never came flying out on its own. Ever. So most physicists filed quarks under "useful fiction" and got on with their lives.

QUESTION: So what changed?

NARRATOR: A machine got built. In the golden hills south of San Francisco, at Stanford, they laid down the longest, straightest structure in the world. A linear accelerator two miles long. Three point two kilometres of dead-straight tunnel, pointing electrons down its length like a rifle barrel aimed at the future. This was the Stanford Linear Accelerator Centre. And it did one thing with terrifying precision: it took electrons and it fed them energy, again and again and again, all the way down those two miles, until they were travelling at very nearly the speed of light, carrying energies of many billions of electron volts.

NARRATOR: Now, why electrons? This is the clever part, and it is why this experiment could see something Rutherford's never could. An electron, as far as anyone can tell, really is a point. It has no size, no internal parts, nothing. So it makes a perfect probe. It is a clean, sharp needle with no messy structure of its own to confuse the picture. And because it was moving so fast, carrying so much energy, it had another magic property. In quantum physics, the faster and more energetic a particle is, the finer the detail it can resolve. A fast electron behaves like a wave with a tiny, tiny wavelength, and the tinier the wavelength, the smaller the thing you can probe. These electrons were sharp enough to see structure inside the proton, if there was any structure there to see.

NARRATOR: So here was the plan. Take these near-light-speed electrons and slam them into a small tank of liquid hydrogen. And hydrogen is the perfect target, because a hydrogen nucleus is just a single, lonely proton. Nothing else to get in the way. Fire your electron needles at the protons, and watch, very carefully, where they go afterwards. This is what physicists call deep inelastic scattering. "Deep" because the electrons were probing deep inside the proton. "Inelastic" because they were hitting it so hard the proton would shatter and spray out other particles, rather than just gently bouncing. And the team that built the experiment and pored over the results were three physicists: Jerome Friedman, Henry Kendall, and Richard Taylor. Between nineteen sixty-seven and nineteen seventy-three they ran this experiment over and over, catching the scattered electrons in a giant, swinging spectrometer, the size of a house, that could pivot to measure exactly how far each electron had been knocked off course.

QUESTION: And what did everyone expect to see?

NARRATOR: Almost nothing. If the proton really was a soft, smeared-out ball of charge, a little fuzzy pudding, then the electrons should mostly skim through it, barely deflected. Gentle little nudges. A few degrees here, a few degrees there. That was the prediction almost everyone held. A big deflection, an electron bouncing back hard and wide, should have been vanishingly rare, because there would be nothing hard and small inside to bounce off. The whole community expected the proton to be squishy.

NARRATOR: It was not squishy. As the data came in, Friedman, Kendall, and Taylor kept finding electrons that had been scattered through large angles, kicked violently sideways, far more often than a soft proton could ever explain. And here is the detail that should have made everyone sit up: the harder they hit, the more of these violent bounces they saw. A soft, spread-out proton should have looked softer and blurrier the deeper you probed. Instead the opposite happened. The deeper the electrons went, the more they behaved as though they were rattling around inside a nearly empty space, only to smack, now and then, into something tiny and hard. Electrons were coming in and, every so often, ricocheting hard, as though deep inside that proton they had struck something small, and dense, and solid. Something almost like a point.

NARRATOR: If this is giving you a feeling of déjà vu, good. It is Rutherford all over again. It is the alpha particle bouncing back off the nucleus. It is the marble hitting the hidden furniture. The proton, which everyone had sworn was a featureless dot, was behaving instead like a bag containing several tiny, hard, point-like lumps. The electrons were bouncing off the lumps.

NARRATOR: But, and I love this, the physicists did not immediately shout "quarks." Because that is not how good science works, and because the result was genuinely confusing. It took real theoretical firepower to read what the data was saying. A physicist named James Bjorken worked out a subtle pattern the scattering should follow if the proton contained hard point-like pieces, a pattern called scaling, and the data obeyed it beautifully. And then Richard Feynman, one of the sharpest minds of the century, visited Stanford, looked at the results, and gave the mysterious lumps a name of his own. He called them partons. Just: "the parts." Bits inside the proton, whatever they turned out to be. And slowly, carefully, over months of argument and cross-checking, the physics community realised that Feynman's partons and Gell-Mann's quarks were the same thing. The mathematical bookkeeping trick was real. It was in there. It was solid enough to bounce an electron off.

QUESTION: So how many lumps were inside?

NARRATOR: When they measured carefully how the electrons scattered, and how the energy was shared out, the numbers pointed to a specific answer. The proton behaved as though it contained three of these point-like objects. Three quarks. And that was exactly the number Gell-Mann's theory had demanded, from pure symmetry, years before, when quarks were still supposed to be imaginary. The scattering pattern was consistent with three tiny charged pieces sitting inside a proton. The neutron, when they got to it, told the same story. Three quarks each. The two particles at the heart of every atom in your body, in this room, in every star you have ever seen, are not fundamental. They are not the bottom. They have an inside, and the inside is quarks.

NARRATOR: And now we can close the loop on that puzzle from the beginning, the reason quarks felt like a cheat. Remember, no matter how hard you smash protons, a lone quark never comes flying out. So how can we say they're real if you can never catch one on its own? Here is the beautiful resolution. The force that binds quarks together behaves like nothing else in nature. It is unbelievably strong, and it gets stronger the further apart you try to pull the quarks. Pulling two quarks apart is like stretching a spring that only pushes back harder the more you stretch it. Try to yank one quark out of a proton, and you pour in so much energy that, before it can ever escape, that energy converts straight into brand-new particles, snapping the connection and hiding the quark inside fresh clumps of matter. You can never hold a bare quark in your hand. But you can do what Stanford did: fire an electron in fast enough to bounce off one, and feel it in there. Not see it. Feel the ricochet. Exactly the way Rutherford felt the nucleus.

NARRATOR: In nineteen ninety, Jerome Friedman, Henry Kendall, and Richard Taylor were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics for this work. And the citation was, in effect, for opening up a whole new layer of the world. Because think about what had just happened to the ladder of matter. Everything is made of molecules. Molecules are made of atoms. Atoms are made of a nucleus and electrons. The nucleus is made of protons and neutrons. And now, at last, protons and neutrons were made of quarks. Rung by rung, physics had climbed down into matter, and here, at Stanford, in a two-mile tunnel, it had found what may be one of the final rungs. As far as we can tell today, the quark, like the electron, really is a point. Really has no inside. Really is fundamental.

NARRATOR: But we thought that about the proton, too. And that is maybe the deepest lesson hiding in this story. Every time we have declared something the smallest, simplest, final brick of reality, someone has eventually built a bigger machine, fired something faster, and found a whole world hiding inside. The proton looked like a dot for decades. It took the longest, straightest machine on Earth, and three stubborn physicists willing to trust a result that everyone expected to be boring, to crack it open and find three quarks rattling around inside.

NARRATOR: So the proton in your fingertip is not a dot. It is a tiny, roaring cage of quarks, held together by the strongest force in the universe, and we only know because we watched electrons bounce off the things inside it.
