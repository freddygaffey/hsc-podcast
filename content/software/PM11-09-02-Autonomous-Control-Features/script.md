NARRATOR: Last episode was the fork at the heart of control: open loop versus closed loop. The marquee line was closed loops listen; open loops are blind. A closed loop runs the cycle Measure, Compare, Correct, M-C-C: measure the output, compare it to the setpoint to get the error — error equals setpoint minus measured — and correct in proportion to that error. You learned about negative feedback keeping things stable, and how gain that's too high causes overshoot and oscillation. That self-correcting loop is the foundation for today. Before that, the whole module spine: Sense-Think-Act.

NARRATOR: Here's the thread. A closed loop self-corrects — but a human still switches it on, sets the target, and watches over it. Today we ask: what does it take for the system to run that loop entirely by itself, making its own decisions, with no human in the loop? That's autonomous control, and it's everywhere — robot vacuums, self-parking cars, delivery drones. The dot-point: "Outline the features of an algorithm and program code used for autonomous control." QUESTION: First, the definition — and a trap. Is a powerful remote-controlled drone, the kind a pilot flies, an autonomous system?

NARRATOR: No — and this is the misconception that costs marks. Autonomous control means the system makes its own decisions and acts without a human in the loop. A remote-controlled drone has a motor, sensors, the works — but a human is deciding every move; the machine just obeys. The motor can be identical between the two; the difference is who decides. So the trap to avoid is thinking autonomous means "has a motor" or "is automatic" — what makes it autonomous is the decide-for-itself step. Manual or remote control: the human is the brain. Autonomous control: the algorithm is the brain.

NARRATOR: So what does that algorithm need? Here's your hook for the features — S-D-A-F. S, continuous Sensing: it's always watching the world, taking many readings a second, because it can't ask a human what's happening. D, Decision logic: rules, or more often a state machine, that turn those readings into actions — the actual thinking. A, Adapt, or self-correct: it uses the closed-loop feedback from last episode to measure the result of its action and correct on the next loop. And F, Fail-safe: because no human is watching, it must handle uncertainty and edge cases, and when something dangerous or unexpected happens, it must fail safe — stop, enter a safe state, or fall back to a backup plan. Sensing, Decision, Adapt, Fail-safe — S-D-A-F. Those four are the examinable features.

NARRATOR: Listing 1 makes it concrete and runnable — an autonomous line-following robot, a classic. It reads two light sensors that tell it whether each side is over the line — that's the continuous Sensing. Its decision logic is a little state machine: both sensors on the line, go straight; only the left on, it's drifting right, so steer left; only the right on, steer right. That steering to stay centred is the Adapt, the self-correction — a closed loop with no human touching it. And the crucial last branch is the Fail-safe: if it loses the line entirely, both sensors off, it doesn't barrel forward blindly — it stops and searches. In the listing, a run of sensor readings produces exactly that sequence of decisions, and the line-lost case always returns search, never forward. That's S-D-A-F in a dozen lines. Listing 2 writes the general autonomous loop as pseudocode — sense, check the safety interlock, then decide and act — with the remote-control contrast underneath: remote control just waits for a human command and does it.

QUESTION: Pause the player and try this. Think about a delivery drone that flies a parcel to an address by itself. First, what makes it autonomous rather than remote-controlled? Second, name two features its control algorithm must have. Work it out, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's a strong answer. It's autonomous because it decides its own actions — its route, its adjustments, when to descend — from sensor data, without a human piloting it; a remote-controlled drone would have a person making those decisions. Two features it needs: continuous sensing of its position and surroundings to navigate and avoid obstacles; and fail-safes — for example, if it loses signal or a sensor fails or the battery runs low, it must do something safe like return home or land, not just drop. You could also name decision logic for routing, or self-correction to hold its course in wind. The marks come from nailing the decides-without-a-human point and naming genuine algorithmic features, each tied to why the drone needs it.

NARRATOR: Autonomy isn't all-or-nothing, though — it's a spectrum, and the levels are worth knowing. Listing 3 lays out the scale from Level Zero to Level Five. Level Zero is fully manual — a human controls everything. Level One is basic assistance, like an automatic door, with a human able to override. Level Two is partial autonomy — it handles routine work while a human supervises, like a robot lawnmower. Level Three is conditional — it decides from sensor data but expects a human ready to take over, like a self-parking car. Level Four is high autonomy, handling most situations alone, needing a human only for unusual edge cases. And Level Five is full autonomy — completely independent, like a spacecraft navigating itself. Most real systems live in the middle, with a human supervising — and a key design principle is to keep a human in the loop for the critical decisions.

NARRATOR: That leads straight to safety, which matters far more for autonomous systems precisely because no one's watching moment to moment. Two mechanisms to name. A safety interlock is an automatic check that blocks a dangerous action before it can happen — like a robot that refuses to move while a person is detected in its zone. A fallback is a backup plan when the primary action fails, so the system degrades gracefully instead of collapsing — try a slower approach, an alternate route, and only then ask for help. Interlocks prevent harm; fallbacks keep it running safely when things go wrong. An autonomous system without these isn't clever, it's dangerous.

QUESTION: Question one. Outline three features of an algorithm used for autonomous control. Three marks.

NARRATOR: One: continuous sensing — the algorithm repeatedly reads its sensors to stay aware of its environment without human input. Two: decision-making logic — typically rules or a state machine that converts sensor readings into actions, the autonomous "thinking." Three: fail-safe handling — it detects unexpected or dangerous conditions and responds safely, for example stopping or entering a safe state, since no human is supervising. A fourth valid feature is self-correction through feedback, a closed loop. For three marks, name three distinct features, each with a phrase on what it does — not just one-word labels.

QUESTION: Question two. Distinguish autonomous control from remote control, using an example of each. Pause, write, then play.

NARRATOR: In remote control, a human makes the decisions and the machine carries out their commands — for example a drone flown by a pilot with a handset. In autonomous control, the system itself makes the decisions from its sensor data and acts without human input — for example a robot vacuum that senses obstacles and chooses its own path. The distinction for the mark: it's about who decides — the human in remote control, the algorithm in autonomous control — not about whether the device has motors or sensors. Give a clear example of each to secure full marks.

QUESTION: Question three. Explain why fail-safes and safety interlocks are especially important in autonomous systems.

NARRATOR: Because an autonomous system operates without a human continuously supervising it, there is no one to notice a hazard and intervene in time, so the system must protect itself and others. A safety interlock automatically blocks a dangerous action before it occurs — for example preventing movement when a person is detected nearby — and a fail-safe ensures that when something goes wrong, such as a sensor failure or lost signal, the system enters a safe state or falls back to a backup plan rather than continuing blindly. The mark-earning point is linking the absence of a human supervisor to the need for automatic, built-in safety that prevents harm and degrades gracefully.

QUESTION: Question four. What do "levels of autonomy" describe? Illustrate with two contrasting levels.

NARRATOR: Levels of autonomy describe how much a system can operate independently of human control, on a scale from fully manual to fully autonomous. For example, Level Zero is manual control, where a human operates everything directly, such as a remote-controlled arm; Level Five is full autonomy, where the system operates completely independently with no human intervention under normal conditions, such as a spacecraft navigating itself. In between, levels describe increasing independence with decreasing human supervision. The mark is defining autonomy levels as a scale of independence and contrasting two levels correctly.
