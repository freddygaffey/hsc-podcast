NARRATOR: It is the eighth of September, two thousand twenty-five, a little after one in the afternoon, Coordinated Universal Time. Somewhere in the world a developer runs a command they have run ten thousand times before. Three words. N-P-M, space, install. It is the most ordinary act in modern software — the digital equivalent of turning on a tap. Their computer reaches out to a giant public warehouse of free code, pulls down the little building blocks their project depends on, and stitches them in. Nothing flickers. No warning appears. The command finishes in a few seconds, exactly as it always has. And in those few seconds, that developer has just pulled onto their machine a piece of code that, a few hours earlier, quietly turned poisonous — code that sits inside packages downloaded more than two billion times every single week. They did nothing wrong. They typed the same three words they always type. That is the whole problem we are going to talk about today.

NARRATOR: To understand what happened that afternoon, and the far stranger thing that happened one week later, you need to hold one idea in your head: almost none of the software you use was written by the people whose name is on it. A modern app is not a building someone designed and built brick by brick. It is more like a tower assembled out of thousands of pre-made parts, ordered in from strangers all over the world, most of them given away for free by volunteers. When a developer types install, they are trusting all of those strangers at once. The whole structure rests on a long, invisible chain of trust — author, to package, to the package that depends on it, to the package that depends on that, down and down, until it reaches your machine. We call that chain the software supply chain. And for years now, attackers have understood something the rest of us were slow to learn: you do not have to break into a fortress if you can poison what the fortress orders for lunch.

QUESTION: If you wanted to reach a million computers at once, would you attack a million computers — or would you attack the one package all million of them are about to download?

NARRATOR: That September afternoon, someone chose the package. The way in was almost insultingly simple. A well-known maintainer who goes by the handle Qix — a man named Josh Junon, responsible for a family of tiny, ubiquitous utilities — received an email. It looked exactly like an official message from N-P-M. It said his two-factor authentication needed updating, and it gave him a link. The link went to a site that looked exactly like the real login page, sitting on a web address built to fool a tired eye: N-P-M-J-S dot help. Not the real npmjs dot com. Just close enough. He clicked. He typed in his username. He typed in his password. And then he typed in the live, six-digit one-time code from his authenticator app — the very code that is supposed to be the unbreakable second lock. On the other end, in real time, the attacker scooped up all three and walked straight into his account before that code expired. No bug was exploited. No encryption was cracked. A human being was simply, expertly, lied to.

NARRATOR: And once they were inside his account, they owned everything he could publish. Within sixteen minutes, poisoned versions of around eighteen packages went out under his trusted name — packages with dull, invisible names like chalk and debug and ansi-styles and strip-ansi. You have never heard of them. You have almost certainly run them. Chalk alone is pulled down roughly three hundred million times a week; it does something as unglamorous as putting colour into text in a terminal. Debug is another forty-seven million. Stacked together, the compromised set reached past two billion downloads every week, buried so deep in the foundations of the web that the developers relying on them had no idea they were even there. One stolen login. Two billion weekly downloads now carrying a payload.

NARRATOR: And what was the payload? Something patient and quiet, built for the age of digital money. It was what is called a crypto-clipper, or a wallet-drainer. When the poisoned code ended up running in a web page, it would sit silently and watch for one thing: a person about to send cryptocurrency. At the precise moment the transaction was being prepared, it would reach in and rewrite the destination — swapping the address the money was meant to go to for an address belonging to the attacker — while the screen still showed the victim the correct, expected details. You think you are sending your money to your friend. The numbers on the screen agree with you. And the money quietly leaves for a stranger. It did not smash a window. It picked a pocket, and it did it inside the most trusted software on the internet.

NARRATOR: So this should have been a catastrophe for the ages. The single largest reach of any supply-chain attack ever attempted. And here is the surprise: the actual money stolen came to a few hundred dollars. That is not a typo. A weapon loaded into two billion weekly downloads netted its makers roughly the price of a second-hand phone. Why? Because a human did it, and humans get caught. Within about two hours, other developers noticed the code looked wrong, raised the alarm, and a mortified Josh Junon — who had realised his mistake — pulled the poisoned versions and pushed clean ones. The window slammed shut. The defences had not been strong. The attacker had simply been spotted, fast, by people paying attention. It was a near-miss told as a comedy. And almost everyone missed the actual lesson buried inside it. Because exactly one week later, someone removed the human from the equation entirely.

QUESTION: What is the one thing a human attacker can do that an automatic program cannot — and what happens when you take it away?

NARRATOR: The fifteenth of September, two thousand twenty-five. A package called at-ctrl-tinycolor — another tiny, harmless-looking utility, this one for handling colours — published a new version. On the surface, nothing remarkable. Maintainers publish new versions thousands of times a day. But this version carried something new in the long, grim history of these attacks. Hidden inside it was a small script set to run automatically the moment the package was installed — a feature N-P-M genuinely offers, called a post-install script, meant for honest setup work. And what this script did, on every machine that pulled the package down, changed the game. It did not wait for a human to aim it. It aimed itself.

NARRATOR: Here is what it did, step by step, on each machine it landed on. First, it went looking for secrets. It ran an off-the-shelf tool — a credential scanner called TruffleHog, the kind of thing security teams use to find leaked passwords — and it turned that tool against the very machine it was running on. It hunted through files, through environment variables, through the special hidden services that cloud servers use to hand out keys, and it scooped up everything valuable it could find: N-P-M publishing tokens, code-hosting tokens for places like GitHub, keys to Amazon and Microsoft and Google cloud accounts. Second, it stole them — it bundled up everything it found and shipped it off to the attacker. And third — this is the part that matters, the part that had never been done at this scale — it took the N-P-M publishing token it had just stolen, logged in as that victim, found every single package that victim was allowed to publish, injected a copy of itself into each one, and published them. Poisoned. Automatically. With no human anywhere in the loop.

NARRATOR: Sit with what that means. Every developer who installed the first poisoned package became, without knowing it, a launch pad for the next wave. Their stolen keys were used to poison their packages, which were then installed by other developers, whose keys were stolen to poison their packages, and on, and on. It did not need a person to pick the next target. It did not need a command centre sending orders. It carried its own instructions, and it made copies of itself out of its victims. That is the definition of a worm — code that reproduces and spreads on its own — and this was the first time the world had seen a true one loose in the software supply chain. The people who built it gave it a name, and you could not ask for a more fitting one. They called it Shai-Hulud, after the great sandworm from the novel Dune — the vast creature that moves unseen beneath the surface and surfaces without warning. It burrowed through the ecosystem exactly like its namesake burrowed through sand.

NARRATOR: And it carried a final, vicious flourish. Remember all those secrets it stole — the tokens, the cloud keys, the private credentials? It did not just quietly mail them to the attacker. It created a brand-new, public repository on the victim's own GitHub account, gave it the name Shai-Hulud, and dumped the stolen secrets there, in the open, for anyone on Earth to read. The theft and the public humiliation were the same act. A company that got hit did not just lose its keys to a thief; it found its most sensitive credentials published to the open internet under its own name. The worm did not only steal from you. It exposed you while it did it.

NARRATOR: Now put the two attacks side by side, because the contrast is the entire point of this story. The phishing attack, one week earlier, was loaded into vastly more downloads — two billion a week — and it stole a few hundred dollars, because a human was driving it and a human can be caught in two hours. The worm reached fewer packages and did immeasurably more damage, because there was no human to catch. You can train a developer not to click a bad link. You cannot train a thousand machines not to run an install script. You can spot a human attacker getting greedy and shut their account. A worm does not get greedy and does not get tired; it just runs, silently, on every machine that touches it, while the whole world is asleep. The danger was never the size of the reach. The danger was that the attack no longer needed a hand on the wheel.

NARRATOR: The first wave of Shai-Hulud poisoned something on the order of two hundred packages, with the count climbing past five hundred as researchers pulled the thread. The registry's operators and the wider community scrambled — pulling poisoned versions, revoking stolen tokens, forcing maintainers to re-secure their accounts. America's own Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency put out a formal alert. It was contained, eventually, the way a fire is contained: not because the building was fireproof, but because enough people grabbed buckets. And then, just over two months later, it came back — bigger, faster, and meaner.

NARRATOR: The twenty-fourth of November, two thousand twenty-five. The sequel. The attackers themselves named it, with a flat sort of menace, in the description of the repositories it created: Shai-Hulud, the Second Coming. This version was the same self-replicating idea, sharpened. It poisoned nearly eight hundred packages in a single coordinated burst — most of them published in the space of a single morning — together pulling over twenty million downloads a week. It hid its working parts in innocent-looking files named after a popular new tool, so they would not draw the eye. It got cleverer about stealing: when it landed on a machine where it could not find a useful key of its own, it would reach for a token it had stolen from a previous victim and use that one instead — exfiltrating one company's data out through another company's stolen credentials. By the time it was beaten back, something like five hundred separate developers had had their secrets stolen. The worm had learned, between its first coming and its second, to be more efficient at the one thing worms do: spread.

NARRATOR: So what is the lesson, the thing you carry out of this? It is not "N-P-M is dangerous" or "open source is broken." The lesson is about a line that got crossed in the autumn of two thousand twenty-five. For a decade, supply-chain attacks were events — a person made a choice, struck once, and either got away or got caught. They had a beginning and an end because they had a human attached to them. Shai-Hulud removed the human. It turned a one-time act of theft into a self-sustaining process, a fire that lights the next fire, a thing that keeps working long after its maker has walked away. The attack learned to reproduce. And the moment an attack can reproduce on its own, the cost of our blind trust stops being a single bill and starts compounding, automatically, with every install.

NARRATOR: The defences are real, and they are mostly unglamorous, and you can practise every one of them on your own HSC project. Use the strongest form of two-factor authentication you can — the phishing-resistant kind that a fake login page cannot capture, so a Qix-style con simply fails. Pin your dependencies to specific, known versions in a lock file, and use the integrity checks built into that file — the cryptographic fingerprints that refuse a package whose contents do not match what you locked — so a poisoned new release is not pulled in automatically and a swapped-out one is caught at the door. Turn off automatic install scripts unless you actually need them, so a post-install script cannot run before you have even looked at it. Check the provenance of what you add — where it really came from. And above all, internalise the one idea this whole story is built on: your dependencies are not someone else's software that you happen to use. They are part of your software. Choosing them, locking them, and checking them is not an optional extra. It is the job.

NARRATOR: The door, in the end, was the same door it has always been — a single trusted maintainer's account, the human at the top of the chain holding the keys that everyone downstream depends on. That has not changed since the very first of these attacks. What changed in two thousand twenty-five is what walked through it. For the first time, the thing coming through the door did not just take what it came for and leave. It stopped, on the way out, and made a copy of itself — and then it went looking for the next door, and the next, and the next, with no one driving and no one to stop. Build your systems, and choose what you build them on, as if the next worm is already being written. Because it almost certainly is.
