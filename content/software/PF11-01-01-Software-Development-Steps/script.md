NARRATOR: So let's get the eight. In order, they are: requirements definition, determining specifications, design, development, integration, testing and debugging, installation, and maintenance. Eight steps. That's the spine of this entire episode, and honestly the spine of the entire course, because almost everything else you'll learn lives inside one of these steps.

QUESTION: Eight steps is a lot to hold in your head at five times speed. Is there a hook?

NARRATOR: There is, and you're going to hear it again and again, so lock it in now. The acronym is R-S-D-D-I-T-I-M. That's the first letter of each step in order: Requirements, Specifications, Design, Development, Integration, Testing, Installation, Maintenance. To remember the order, use this sentence: "Real Software Demands Discipline: Integrate, Test, Install, Maintain." Real for Requirements, Software for Specifications, Demands for Design, Discipline for Development, and then the last four words are just the step names themselves: Integrate, Test, Install, Maintain. When you sit down in the exam, the very first thing you should dump in the margin is R-S-D-D-I-T-I-M. Offload it onto paper in second one, and then any "list the steps in order" question is just reading it back. We'll come back to that acronym in every recap and every review from here on.

NARRATOR: Now let me walk each step, because knowing the names isn't enough. The marker wants you to know what each step actually produces and why it's there. I'll anchor every step to one running example, a Grade Calculator, because a concrete thread is far easier to remember than eight abstract definitions. The Grade Calculator is a small tool that takes three exam scores and returns an average. Trivial as a program. Perfect as a lifecycle example, because you can see all eight steps clearly without drowning in detail.

NARRATOR: Step one, requirements definition. This is where you figure out what problem you're actually solving, and for whom. Not how you'll build it. What it needs to do, in the language of the people who'll use it. For the Grade Calculator, the requirement is: a teacher needs to turn three student scores into an average quickly and without arithmetic mistakes. Notice there's no code in that sentence, no mention of Python, no mention of functions. Requirements are about the problem, stated from the user's side. The key syllabus idea hiding here is the stakeholder: anyone affected by the software. The teacher who enters grades, the student who wants to know their standing, the administrator who needs the numbers to be auditable. A strong requirements step talks to those people and writes down what they need before anyone opens an editor.

NARRATOR: Step two, determining specifications. Requirements say what the user needs in their words. Specifications translate that into precise, testable statements a developer can build against. This is the step students most often blur into the previous one, so hear the difference clearly. The requirement is "calculate an average quickly and accurately". The specification is "accept three numeric inputs in the range zero to one hundred; reject anything outside that range with an error message; calculate the arithmetic mean; round the result to one decimal place". See how each of those is something you could write a test for? That's the test: a good specification is measurable. The syllabus splits these into functional specifications, what the system must do, and non-functional specifications, how well it must do it, things like speed, reliability, and usability. "Calculate the average" is functional. "Respond within one hundred milliseconds" is non-functional. Naming that distinction in an answer is worth a mark.

QUESTION: Why separate requirements from specifications at all? In practice don't they bleed together?

NARRATOR: They do bleed together, and that's exactly why the syllabus separates them, because the bleed is where projects die. If you jump from a vague requirement straight to code, you build something, show the user, and they say "that's not what I meant". The specification step forces you to pin down the fuzzy bits before they cost you a rewrite. "Accurate" becomes "rounded to one decimal place". "Quick" becomes "under a hundred milliseconds". The specification is the contract. When you later test, you test against the specification, not against a vibe. So in an exam, if you're asked why specifications matter, the mark-earning answer is: they turn an ambiguous user need into precise, testable criteria that the solution can be verified against. That word "testable" is what the marker is hunting for.

NARRATOR: Step three, design. Now, and only now, do you decide how it'll be built. Design is the blueprint: the structure of the solution before you commit to writing it all. For the Grade Calculator, design is the decision to split the job into three separate pieces: one piece that validates a grade, one that calculates the average, and one that formats the result for display. That's a real design principle called separation of concerns, each part has one job, and you'll meet it formally later in the course. Design also includes choosing your data: three numbers in, one number out. The point of designing before developing is that it's cheap to move a box around on a diagram and expensive to restructure code you've already written. Design is where you catch structural mistakes while they're still free.

NARRATOR: Step four, development. This is the part you already know how to do: writing the actual code. The teacher's three pieces from the design become three real functions. The validate-grade routine checks that a score is numeric and between zero and one hundred. The calculate-average routine adds the validated scores and divides. The format-result routine turns the number into a tidy string. I've put the full Python in Listing one if you want to read it later, and the same algorithm written in the exam's own NESA pseudocode style is in Listing two, so you can rehearse the exact form you'll be marked on. But the spoken point is this: development is implementation, and the syllabus wants it done a particular way. Build incrementally, in small units. Write one function, check it works, then write the next. And commit your changes frequently in small, focused commits, which is a habit we'll properly unpack next lesson when we talk about collaboration tools and version control. The exam-relevant phrase here is "incremental development": small, tested pieces, not one giant heroic push.

NARRATOR: Step five, integration. You've built the pieces separately. Integration is making them work together as one system. This is its own named step for a reason that surprises people: individual parts can each pass their own tests and still fail when connected, because the problem lives in the interfaces between them. Maybe the validate-grade routine returns a float, but the calculate-average routine was written expecting an integer. Each works alone. Together they break. For the Grade Calculator, integration is wiring the three functions into one flow, validate, then calculate, then format, and confirming data passes cleanly from one to the next. In a bigger system, integration is where your module meets someone else's module, and that's exactly why we commit small and often, so the integration points are easy to find when something snaps.

NARRATOR: Step six, testing and debugging. Now you check it actually does what the specification said. And here's the thing the specification step bought you: you already know what to test against. You test with typical inputs, eighty, seventy-two, ninety should give eighty-point-seven. You test boundary inputs, a score of exactly zero, a score of exactly one hundred, the edges where bugs love to hide. And you test faulty or abnormal inputs, a score of negative five, a score of one hundred and fifty, a word instead of a number, to confirm it rejects them gracefully instead of crashing or, worse, silently returning nonsense. That trio, typical, boundary, and faulty data, is a whole lesson of its own later in the module, and it's heavily examined, so I'm planting the seed now. I've written that exact set of typical, boundary, and faulty tests out as a small runnable test suite in Listing three, if you want to see what testing against a specification actually looks like. Debugging is the other half: when a test fails, you locate the cause and fix it, using tools we'll cover, breakpoints, stepping through line by line, watching a variable's value change. The mark-earning idea: testing finds that there's a fault; debugging finds where it is and removes it.

NARRATOR: Step seven, installation. Also called deployment. This is getting the finished software out of your development environment and into the place where real users actually run it. It sounds trivial and it is famously not. Software that runs perfectly on your machine can fall over on the user's machine because a dependency is missing, the operating system is different, or the setup steps weren't written down. So installation includes packaging the software for distribution and documenting how to run it and what it needs. For the Grade Calculator, that might be as simple as "it needs Python three, here's the command to run it". For a real product it's installers, configuration, and environment setup. The phrase markers like is "deploying to the production environment", as opposed to the development environment where you built it.

NARRATOR: Step eight, maintenance. The software is live and being used, and this is where it spends most of its actual life. Maintenance is everything that happens after release: fixing defects that users discover, updating dependencies so they don't go stale or insecure, and adding improvements as needs change. People underestimate this step badly. The majority of a real system's total cost is spent here, in maintenance, not in the original build. For the Grade Calculator, maintenance might be a teacher saying "actually we now weight the final exam at fifty percent", and you going back in to change the calculation. Which, and this is the crucial bit, sends you right back to the start: a new requirement, a new specification, a design tweak, more development, more testing. Which brings us to the single most important idea in this whole episode.

NARRATOR: The eight steps are not a straight line. They are a cycle. The textbook word, and the word you want in your answer, is "iterative". You loop back. You build a small version, get feedback, and revisit earlier steps to improve it. Maintenance feeds new requirements. A failed test sends you back to development, or even back to design. A misunderstanding caught during design sends you back to requirements. The whole thing spirals forward through repeated passes, not down a one-way staircase. This name, the Software Development Lifecycle, the SDLC, has the word "cycle" in it for exactly this reason.

QUESTION: Okay, this is the one I want you to actually stop for. Pause the player. From memory, list the eight software development steps in order, and then tell me in one sentence why the process is iterative rather than linear. Work it out, say it out loud, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's the answer. The eight, in order: requirements definition, determining specifications, design, development, integration, testing and debugging, installation, maintenance. R-S-D-D-I-T-I-M. And it's iterative rather than linear because feedback at any stage, a failed test, a changed user need, a flaw found in maintenance, sends you back to revisit and refine earlier steps, so the process loops and improves with each pass rather than running once top to bottom. If you got the eight in order and used the word "iterative", you've got the two highest-value things in this lesson.

NARRATOR: Let me make that iterative point concrete with a contrast, because the HSC loves a contrast, and this one shows up directly in a lesson coming very soon. There's a project management model called Waterfall that treats these steps as a strict one-way sequence, finish each phase completely, then move on, no going back. And there's a model called Agile that treats them as a tight repeating loop, do a little of each, get feedback, go round again. We've got a whole lesson on Waterfall versus Agile coming up at one-point-four, so I won't teach it here, but plant this now: the "iterative versus linear" distinction you just learned is the seed of that entire comparison. The hook for that lesson, which you'll hear me repeat, is "Waterfall falls once; Agile loops". Today's iterative lifecycle is the Agile mindset in embryo.

NARRATOR: One more forward link, because connecting today's topic to the rest of the syllabus is how it sticks. The design step I mentioned, where you sketch the structure before building, becomes a serious skill set later. So when you hear "design" as one of eight quick steps today, know that it expands into multiple lessons later. The lifecycle is the table of contents for a big chunk of the course.

NARRATOR: A weak answer says: "First you plan it, then you write the code, then you test it to make sure it works, then you give it to the user." That's not wrong, exactly. Maybe two marks if the marker is generous.

NARRATOR: A strong answer says: "The Grade Calculator follows the software development lifecycle. In requirements definition, the need for fast, accurate grade averaging is identified with the teacher as the key stakeholder. In determining specifications, this becomes testable criteria: accept three scores from zero to one hundred, reject invalid input, return the mean to one decimal place. Design separates the solution into validation, calculation, and formatting components. Development implements these incrementally as functions. Integration connects them into one flow and checks the interfaces. Testing and debugging verifies the result against the specification using typical, boundary, and faulty data. Installation deploys the tool to the teacher's environment with run instructions. Maintenance handles later defect fixes and changes, such as new grade weightings. The process is iterative: maintenance and failed tests feed back into earlier steps." That answer names every step, uses the exact terms, ties them to the example, and lands the iterative point. That's full marks. Hear the difference? Same knowledge. The marks are in the vocabulary and the structure.

NARRATOR: Let me flag the two traps that cost the most marks on this topic, by name, so you can sidestep them. Trap one: presenting the lifecycle as strictly linear. If your answer reads like a one-way to-do list with no looping back, you've missed the single most examined idea here. Always get the word "iterative" in, and ideally give one example of a loop-back, like maintenance generating a new requirement. Trap two: skipping problem identification and jumping straight to code. A surprising number of students, when asked to describe building software, start at "write the program". The first real work, requirements and specifications, happens before any code exists, and forgetting that is exactly the mistake that makes real projects build the wrong thing. Markers know this, and they reward the answer that starts where the work actually starts: understanding the problem.

NARRATOR: There's a small bit of redundancy I'm doing on purpose, so let me say the core point one more time in different words, because at five times speed a single mention slips past. The software you'll build in your life is not "design it once, build it once, ship it once, done". It is a loop: understand, specify, design, build, connect, test, deploy, maintain, and then maintenance throws you a new need and you go round again. Eight steps, R-S-D-D-I-T-I-M, run iteratively. That's the whole lesson in one breath.

NARRATOR: Quick recap of what to carry out of here, phrased the way you'd write it. One: the software development lifecycle has eight steps, requirements definition, determining specifications, design, development, integration, testing and debugging, installation, maintenance, remembered as R-S-D-D-I-T-I-M, "Real Software Demands Discipline: Integrate, Test, Install, Maintain". Two: requirements are the user's need in plain language; specifications are the precise, testable version a developer builds against. QUESTION: Question one. "List the fundamental software development steps in order." Four marks. Pause now, write all eight from memory, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer: requirements definition, determining specifications, design, development, integration, testing and debugging, installation, maintenance. If you wrote your acronym, R-S-D-D-I-T-I-M, in the margin first, this is a guaranteed four marks. The marker wants all eight, in order, with the correct names, not paraphrases like "make it" for development.

QUESTION: Question two. "Distinguish between requirements definition and determining specifications." Three marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer: requirements definition identifies what the user needs in general, real-world terms from the stakeholder's perspective, for example "calculate grades quickly and accurately". Determining specifications translates those needs into precise, measurable, testable statements that a developer can build and verify against, for example "accept three scores from zero to one hundred and return the mean to one decimal place". The distinction the marker rewards: requirements are the problem in the user's language; specifications are the precise, testable contract for the solution.

QUESTION: Question three. "Explain why the software development lifecycle is described as iterative rather than linear." Four marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer: the lifecycle is iterative because feedback at any stage causes developers to return to and refine earlier steps, rather than completing each step once and never revisiting it. For example, a fault found during testing sends work back to development; a change requested during maintenance generates a new requirement, restarting the cycle. This looping allows the software to be built in small increments, validated with users, and improved over repeated passes, which manages risk and accommodates changing needs, unlike a linear process that runs once from start to finish.

QUESTION: Question four. A scenario one. "A school wants a program that converts exam marks into letter grades. The team writes the code immediately, then shows it to teachers, who say it doesn't handle marks on the grade boundaries correctly. Identify which development steps the team skipped, and explain how following them would have prevented this." Six marks. This is the hard one. Pause, plan a full answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer: the team skipped requirements definition and determining specifications, jumping straight to development. Had they done requirements definition, they would have consulted the teachers, the key stakeholders, and learned that boundary marks are a critical case. Had they then determined specifications, they would have written a precise, testable rule for how a mark exactly on a boundary is graded, for example "a mark of ninety or above is an A". Testing and debugging would then have included boundary values as deliberate test data, catching the error before release rather than after. The underlying lesson is that the early steps exist to understand the problem fully before building; skipping them led the team to build a solution that didn't match the real requirement, forcing expensive rework, exactly the failure the lifecycle is designed to prevent. A top answer also notes that because the lifecycle is iterative, the team can now feed this discovered fault back into requirements and specifications and loop forward to a corrected solution.
