NARRATOR: Let's start with the single most important idea of the whole lesson, and then everything else hangs off it. The reason there are three diagrams and not one is that a software system is too rich to capture in a single picture. Each diagram is a different lens, and each answers a different question. Here is the mnemonic — write this on your page the second the exam starts — Class equals things, Chart equals jobs, DFD equals data. Say it back: Class equals things, Chart equals jobs, DFD equals data.

NARRATOR: Let me unpack that. A class diagram shows the things — the static structure. What classes exist, what attributes and methods each one has, and how they relate to each other. It answers the question: what objects exist, and how are they connected? A structure chart shows the jobs — the functional decomposition. It breaks a big complex task down into smaller and smaller sub-tasks in a hierarchy. It answers: how does this complex job break down into simpler pieces? And a data flow diagram — DFD for short, say "dee-eff-dee" — shows the data. It tracks how information moves through the system and gets transformed along the way. It answers: where does data come from, where does it go, and what happens to it on the way? Three lenses. Things, jobs, data.

NARRATOR: The house-building analogy the textbook uses is genuinely good, so hold it. When you design a house you don't make one drawing. You make a floor plan that shows the rooms and walls — that's your structure, the static layout. You make an electrical diagram showing how the wiring connects up. And you make a plumbing diagram showing how the water flows through. Same house, three drawings, each essential, none redundant. The class diagram is your floor plan of objects, the structure chart is your breakdown of rooms into smaller spaces, and the data flow diagram is your plumbing — where the information flows.

QUESTION: Hang on — if I can describe the whole system in a class diagram, the things and how they connect, why do I even need the other two?

NARRATOR: Because the class diagram deliberately leaves things out. That's not a weakness — it's the point of having three. A class diagram tells you a Customer relates to an Order, but it doesn't tell you the step-by-step jobs the system performs, and it doesn't tell you that a payment request flows out to a payment gateway and a confirmation flows back. Each diagram is powerful precisely because it ignores what the others handle. If you tried to cram movement of data and the job hierarchy into the class diagram, you'd get an unreadable mess. The skill the syllabus is testing is matching the question to the diagram — and we'll hammer that, because the number one trap in this whole topic is using the wrong diagram for the wrong question.

NARRATOR: Right. Let's go deep on each one, starting with the class diagram, because it leans hardest on everything you already know about objects.

NARRATOR: A class diagram represents each class as a box divided into three stacked compartments. Top compartment: the class name. Middle compartment: the attributes — the data. Bottom compartment: the methods — the behaviour. So if you picture a single class, say a Student, you'd draw a rectangle, write "Student" across the top, then a line, then in the middle list the attributes — name, student number, a list of grades — then another line, then in the bottom list the methods — add a grade, get the average, get the student's info. Notice that's exactly the object split from episode one: data on top, behaviour on the bottom, inside one box. A class diagram is just that, drawn for every class, with lines showing how the boxes relate.

NARRATOR: There's a small notation convention worth knowing for marks. In front of each attribute or method you put a visibility marker. A minus sign means private — internal state, not for outside code. A plus sign means public — part of the interface. So the attributes usually get minus signs and the methods that form the interface get plus signs. And there it is again: that's encapsulation from episode two, drawn as a picture. The minus-sign attributes are the wiring, the plus-sign methods are the buttons. If an exam stimulus shows you a class box, the minus and plus signs are telling you what's hidden state and what's public interface.

NARRATOR: Now the part that earns the most marks and trips the most students: the relationship lines between classes. There are a few kinds, and the difference is real, so let's do them carefully. The full written-out version of the example I'm about to describe is Listing 1 in the supplementary materials — a School system with four classes — but stay with the words.

NARRATOR: First relationship: composition. This is drawn as a line with a filled-in diamond — a solid black diamond — at one end. The diamond sits against the "whole", the thing that owns the parts. Composition means the parts belong to the whole and don't exist without it. If the whole is destroyed, the parts are destroyed too. Here's the mnemonic, and it's the one that survives the exam: filled diamond equals composition equals the part dies with the whole. Say it back — filled diamond, composition, the part dies with the whole. So in our School example, picture the School class up top, and a line running down from it to the Student class with a filled diamond sitting against the School end. That says: the School is composed of Students; the students belong to the school and, in this model, don't exist as part of the system without it. Same again from School down to Course — filled diamond at the School end — the school is composed of its courses.

NARRATOR: Second relationship: association. This is just a plain line, no diamond. It means two classes are connected, they know about each other and interact, but neither owns the other and neither dies with the other. In our School diagram, picture a plain line between Course and Teacher, labelled "taught by". A course is taught by a teacher. They're associated — the course needs to know its teacher — but the teacher isn't a part of the course the way a student is a part of the school. Kill the course, the teacher still exists. Plain line, association, a looser connection than composition.

NARRATOR: Now there's a halfway house you should be able to name, called aggregation, drawn with a hollow, unfilled diamond. Aggregation is a "has-a" where the part can live on its own — a team has-a player, but the player still exists if the team folds. The exam mostly cares that you can contrast the filled diamond, where the part dies with the whole, against the looser relationships. So lock in the filled diamond first; it's the high-value one.

NARRATOR: And here's a lovely link backwards — two episodes ago, talking about inheritance, we set up "is-a versus has-a". Inheritance is an is-a relationship: a Car is-a Vehicle. Composition is a has-a relationship: a School has-a Student, a Car has-an Engine. On a class diagram those look different. Inheritance — the is-a — is drawn with a line ending in a hollow triangle arrowhead pointing at the parent class. Composition and aggregation — the has-a — are drawn with diamonds. So when you read a class diagram: triangle pointing up at a parent means "is-a, inheritance", and a diamond means "has-a, composition or aggregation". That single visual distinction is worth knowing cold.

QUESTION: Okay, those little numbers and symbols on the ends of the lines — the ones that look like "one" and a star — what are those actually telling me?

NARRATOR: That's multiplicity, and it's where a lot of marks quietly live, because students read it backwards. Multiplicity is the numbers written at each end of a relationship line that tell you how many objects of one class connect to how many of the other. The notation: a plain number like "one" means exactly one. A star, which you read as "many", means zero or more. So a relationship labelled "one" at the School end and "many" at the Student end means: one School connects to many Students. That's a one-to-many relationship. Read it like a sentence pointing across the line — one school has many students; each student belongs to one school.

NARRATOR: The trap, and mark it, is reading multiplicity in the wrong direction or mixing up which end is which. You read toward the far class. The number sitting next to the Student box tells you how many Students participate, not how many Schools. So if you see "one" next to School and "star, meaning many" next to Student, the correct spoken reading is: each one school is associated with many students. Get the direction right and you've got the mark; flip it and you've described the opposite system.

NARRATOR: And there's one more pattern: many-to-many. Picture our School example again, the relationship between Course and Student, labelled "enrolled in". A star at both ends — many at the Course end, many at the Student end. That reads: one student can enrol in many courses, and one course can hold many students. Many-to-many. The real world is full of these — students and courses, actors and films, authors and books. When you see a star on both ends, that's the signature of a many-to-many relationship, and naming it as such is exactly what a marker is looking for.

NARRATOR: Let me give you the worked reading of the whole School class diagram in one breath, because hearing it assembled is the thing that makes you able to reproduce it. Four boxes: School, Student, Course, Teacher. From School, a line down to Student with a filled diamond at the School end and "one" at School, "many" at Student — the school is composed of many students, one-to-many composition, the students die with the school. From School, a line down to Course, filled diamond at School, "one-to-many" again — the school is composed of its courses. Between Course and Teacher, a plain line labelled "taught by", with "many" courses to "one" teacher — association, one teacher can teach many courses but isn't part of any of them. And between Course and Student, a plain line labelled "enrolled in", "many" at both ends — many-to-many association, students enrol in many courses and courses hold many students. If you can say that paragraph, you can draw the diagram, and you can answer a "draw a class diagram for this described system" stimulus question. That's the whole skill.

NARRATOR: Let me model the marking boundary, weak answer versus strong, because the exam loves a "describe this relationship" question. Suppose the stimulus shows a filled diamond between Library and Book, with one at the Library end and many at the Book end. A weak, band-four answer says: "the library and the book are connected." True but worthless — it names nothing. A strong answer says: "This is a composition relationship, shown by the filled diamond at the Library end, with a multiplicity of one-to-many — one library is composed of many books, and the books are part of the library and don't exist in the model without it." Hear the difference? The strong answer names the relationship type, points to the symbol that proves it, reads the multiplicity in the correct direction, and states the consequence — part dies with the whole. Four ideas, probably four marks.

NARRATOR: Now let's move to the second diagram, the structure chart — Chart equals jobs. This one's a complete change of mindset, and it actually reaches back before OOP entirely.

NARRATOR: A structure chart is a tree. At the very top sits the whole system as one box — the big job. Below it, branching down, are the major sub-tasks that make up that job. Below each of those, the smaller tasks they break into. It's pure functional decomposition — taking one complicated job and splitting it, top-down, into progressively simpler jobs, until each leaf at the bottom is a single, do-able thing. It does not care about objects, or data movement. It cares about: what are the jobs, and how do they nest inside each other.

NARRATOR: Listing 2 in the supplementary materials writes one out as an indented tree for a Library Management System, but let me speak it. Top box: "Library Management System" — the whole thing. Branching off it, three major sub-tasks side by side: "User Management", "Book Management", and "Lending Operations". Now each of those breaks down further. Under User Management hang three leaves: add a member, remove a member, update a member's info. Under Book Management hang three: add a book, remove a book, search for books. Under Lending Operations hang three: borrow a book, return a book, calculate fines. So it's three levels — the system at the top, three job-groups in the middle, and the concrete little tasks as leaves at the bottom. If you picture an org chart, or a family tree, that's the shape exactly: one root, branching down into more and more specific jobs.

NARRATOR: When is the structure chart the right lens? When the question is "how does this big task break down?" It's brilliant for planning before you build — you decompose the problem into manageable chunks. It's how you'd document a system at a high level, or make sense of an old procedural codebase by mapping out its call hierarchy. And notice the deep link here: this is exactly the top-down decomposition idea you met back in Programming Fundamentals in Year 11, where you took a big problem and broke it into subprograms. A structure chart is the picture of that. So when last episode we said procedural programming uses top-down decomposition — the structure chart is literally the diagram of top-down decomposition. The two ideas are the same idea, one in code, one in a picture.

NARRATOR: And there's a forward link worth planting. Next episode, 05-06, is all about the design process — task definition, top-down versus bottom-up, the facade pattern. The structure chart is the tool you'll reach for during that design process to capture the top-down breakdown. So today you learn to draw it; next episode you learn the process that uses it. We're not teaching the design process now — that's its own lesson — but know they connect.

QUESTION: So a structure chart breaks a job into smaller jobs in a tree. Isn't that basically the same thing as a class diagram? Both have boxes and lines.

NARRATOR: Good — that's exactly the confusion the exam is built to catch, so let's kill it. Both have boxes and lines, yes, but the boxes mean completely different things. In a class diagram, every box is a thing — a class, a type of object, with data and methods inside it. The lines are relationships between objects: owns, is-a, associated-with. In a structure chart, every box is a job — a task or function — and the lines mean "this job is made up of these smaller jobs". One is a map of nouns and how they relate; the other is a breakdown of verbs and how they nest. Class equals things, Chart equals jobs. If the boxes are types of objects, you're reading a class diagram. If the boxes are tasks decomposing downward, you're reading a structure chart. Same shapes on the page, totally different meaning — and the marker wants you to know which is which.

NARRATOR: Now the third diagram, and the one with the most specific notation to memorise — the data flow diagram, the DFD. DFD equals data. This one tracks information moving and being transformed as it travels through the system.

NARRATOR: A DFD has exactly four kinds of element, and you need every symbol nailed, because exams test the symbols directly. Let me give you all four, then a mnemonic to hold them. Element one: a process. A process transforms data — it takes data in, does something to it, and pushes different data out. It's drawn as a circle, and you number your processes one, two, three, and so on, and write a short verb-phrase inside, like "validate PIN" or "process order". Circle, numbered, that's a process. Element two: an external entity. This is a source or destination of data that sits outside the system — a person, another system, anything the data comes from or goes to but that you don't build. It's drawn as a rectangle, a plain square-cornered box, like "Customer" or "Payment Gateway". Rectangle, external entity. Element three: a data store. This is where data sits at rest — a database, a file, a log. It's drawn as an open-ended rectangle — picture a rectangle missing its right-hand end, or two parallel lines — and labelled, like "Account Database". Open rectangle, data store. And element four: the data flows themselves — the arrows. Every arrow is labelled with the actual data travelling along it, like "enrolment request" or "current balance".

NARRATOR: Here's your memory hook for the three shapes, because mixing them up is the commonest DFD mistake. Process is a circle — think of a wheel spinning, doing work, transforming. External entity is a closed rectangle — a solid box for an outsider standing fully outside your system. Data store is an open rectangle — open, because data slides in and out of it like a drawer. Circle works, closed box waits outside, open box stores. Or compress it: circle does, box is outside, open box keeps. Lock those three down and the symbol question is free marks.

NARRATOR: Let me walk you through a DFD in words — an ATM, since it's a classic. Listing 3 has the full thing written out, but listen. On the left, an external entity rectangle labelled "Customer". The customer sends an arrow labelled "card and PIN" into process circle one, labelled "Validate PIN". Process one reaches out to a data store — an open rectangle labelled "Account Database" — with an arrow labelled "account lookup", and the database sends back an arrow labelled "account details". Process one then sends an arrow back to the Customer labelled "validation result". Then the customer sends "withdrawal request" into process circle two, "Process Withdrawal", which checks the balance against the Account Database, updates it, sends "cash and receipt" back to the customer, and pushes "transaction details" along to process circle five, "Log Transaction", which writes a "transaction record" into a second data store, the "Transaction Log". I've traced just the withdrawal path to keep it clear; the full diagram in Listing 3 also has process three, "Process Deposit", and process four, "Check Balance", hanging off the same customer and the same Account Database — same shape, different transaction. Every arrow is labelled with the data on it. You can hear the whole system: who sends what, what each process does to it, where it's stored. That's a DFD doing its job — showing the movement and transformation of data.

NARRATOR: Now there's a structural idea in DFDs you must know by name: levels. You don't draw the whole detailed thing at once. You start with Level zero, also called the context diagram — and "context diagram" is the term markers reward. The Level-zero context diagram shows your entire system as a single process circle in the middle, with all the external entities around it and the data flowing in and out. It's the ten-thousand-foot view: the system as one bubble, and everything it talks to. Then you zoom in. Level one explodes that single bubble into the major processes inside it — process one, process two, process three — and shows the data stores and the flows between them. So the progression is: Level zero, the context diagram, system as one circle; then Level one, the system broken into its main processes. Listing 4 shows a Level-zero then a Level-one DFD for a library, side by side, so you can see the zoom.

NARRATOR: And now the single most important trap in this entire topic, the one I flagged at the start. A data flow diagram is not a flowchart. They look superficially similar — boxes and arrows — but they show fundamentally different things, and confusing them is a guaranteed lost mark. Here it is, plainly: a DFD shows data flow. A flowchart shows control flow. A DFD's arrows say "this piece of data moves from here to here". A flowchart's arrows say "do this step, then do this step, and if this condition is true, branch here". A flowchart has decision diamonds and a strict sequence — it's the order of execution. A DFD has no concept of "first this happens, then that"; it just shows where data comes from, what transforms it, and where it ends up. Say it as a one-liner: a DFD shows where the data goes; a flowchart shows what the program does, in order. Data flow, not control flow. If an exam asks you to distinguish a DFD from a flowchart, that contrast — data movement versus order-of-execution — is the answer.

QUESTION: First, quick recall. What does each of a class diagram, a structure chart, and a data flow diagram show? Give one use of each. Pause and answer, then play.

QUESTION: Second. In a data flow diagram, what symbol represents a process, an external entity, and a data store? And what is a Level zero DFD also called? Pause, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A process is shown as a circle, numbered, containing a verb phrase describing the transformation. An external entity is shown as a closed rectangle. A data store is shown as an open-ended rectangle. And a Level zero DFD is also called a context diagram — it shows the whole system as a single process with its external entities around it. Markers want the exact symbols and the term "context diagram"; vague answers like "a box" lose the mark because there are two kinds of box and they mean different things.

QUESTION: Third. A class diagram shows a filled diamond on a line between a Playlist class and a Song class, with the diamond at the Playlist end, a "one" beside Playlist and a star beside Song. Interpret this relationship fully. Pause and write it, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer, and this is the multiplicity-reading skill. This is a composition relationship — shown by the filled diamond — between Playlist as the whole and Song as the part. The multiplicity is one-to-many: one Playlist is composed of many Songs. Because it's composition, the songs, in this model, belong to the playlist and don't exist as part of the system without it — the part dies with the whole. The trap to avoid: don't read it backwards as "many playlists to one song", and don't call it mere association — the filled diamond specifically means composition. Naming the relationship type, citing the symbol, reading the multiplicity in the right direction, and stating the consequence is the full-mark answer.

QUESTION: Fourth, a distinguish question — these are worth real marks. Distinguish a data flow diagram from a flowchart. Pause, give a proper answer with the key contrast, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer. A data flow diagram and a flowchart are not the same and confusing them is a classic error. A data flow diagram shows the flow of data — it tracks where information comes from, which processes transform it, and where it is stored or sent; it has no notion of execution order. A flowchart shows the flow of control — the step-by-step sequence in which a program executes, including decisions and branches, in strict order. In short: a DFD shows where the data goes; a flowchart shows what the program does, in what order. Data flow versus control flow — that is the distinction the marker is looking for, and stating both halves explicitly is what earns full marks.

QUESTION: Fifth, the big one — a design question, the kind that carries the most marks. You're given this scenario: a clinic appointment system where patients book appointments with doctors, each appointment has a date, time and status, and reception staff manage the schedule. Describe how you would use all three diagram types to model this system, and say what each would capture. Pause, plan a full answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer — and the structure of this answer is itself the skill. I'd use a class diagram to capture the static structure: classes for Patient, Doctor, Appointment, and Receptionist, each with its attributes and methods — an Appointment with date, time and status, and methods to book and cancel. The relationships: a Patient has many Appointments and a Doctor has many Appointments — one-to-many associations — and I'd use multiplicity to show one patient to many appointments. That answers "what objects exist and how do they relate". I'd use a structure chart to capture the functional decomposition: the Clinic Appointment System at the top, breaking down into Patient Management, Appointment Booking, Schedule Management and Reporting, each splitting into concrete tasks like check availability, create appointment, send reminder. That answers "how does the work break down". And I'd use a data flow diagram to capture the data movement: external entities for Patient and Receptionist, a process to book an appointment that reads from and writes to an Appointment data store, with labelled flows like "booking request" and "confirmation". That answers "where does the data come from and go to". The top-band move is to tie each diagram explicitly to the question it answers — Class equals things, Chart equals jobs, DFD equals data — rather than just describing three drawings. Name the lens, name what it captures, and you've shown you understand why there are three.
