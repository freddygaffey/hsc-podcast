NARRATOR: Last episode we followed the data. A mechatronic system handles four streams — M-C-D-O: Measurement, Control, Diagnostic, Optimisation; and the examinable pair was diagnostic data, which asks "is it healthy?", versus optimisation data, which asks "can it do better?" — the purpose decides, not the numbers. Data is obtained by polling or interrupts — polling asks, interrupts tell — then processed by calibrating, filtering, and validating, and stored safely, every entry wearing a Timestamp, Identity, and Units, T-I-U. Before that: the devices, the S-A-E trio — Sensor, Actuator, End-effector — all hanging off the microcontroller's pins, and the whole thing running on Sense-Think-Act.

NARRATOR: Here's the thread. We've chosen the devices and we've handled their data — but none of it runs until it's physically powered and connected. Every one of those components needs the right voltage and enough current, and they all need to be wired together correctly and safely. That's today: working out the power and battery a system needs, and drawing the wiring diagram that documents how it all connects. The dot-points: "Determine power, battery and material requirements for components of a mechatronic system," and "Develop wiring diagrams for a mechatronic system, considering data and power supply requirements." Outcomes S-E-eleven-oh-one — planning and engineering a solution; S-E-eleven-oh-six — applying tools and resources to design; and S-E-eleven-oh-three — hardware shaping the solution.

NARRATOR: Objectives. By the end you'll be able to: use the basic electrical quantities and the power equation; build a power budget and size a battery for a system; state material requirements; and read and draw a wiring diagram that separates data lines from power lines — including the one safety rule about actuators that examiners love.

NARRATOR: Start with the electricity, just enough to be dangerous on paper. Three quantities, and here's your hook — V-I-P: Voltage, measured in volts, is the electrical push; current, the letter I, measured in amps, is the rate of charge flowing; and power, measured in watts, is energy used per second. The one equation you must know: power equals voltage times current. Watts equals volts times amps. There's also Ohm's law — voltage equals current times resistance — but the power equation is the workhorse for budgeting. That's it: power equals voltage times current.

QUESTION: So if I know what each part needs, how do I work out the battery for the whole robot?

NARRATOR: You build a power budget, and Listing 1 does it for our recurring thermostat-fan example, runnably. Step one: every component's power is its voltage times its current, in watts — the controller sips about a watt, the temperature sensor barely a tenth of a watt, and the fan motor pulls nearly five watts. Notice that pattern: the actuator dominates the budget. Motors and other movers are almost always the hungry components, far more than the controller or the sensors — that's a theme worth carrying into every estimate. Step two: add those up for the total system power. Step three: add headroom, around twenty percent, so you're never running at the ragged edge, and allow for inefficiency. Step four: the current the battery must supply is the total power divided by the battery's voltage. For our little system that's well under one amp from a twelve-volt battery.

NARRATOR: Then you size the battery for runtime, and here's the memory hook — amp-hours over amps gives hours. A battery's capacity is measured in amp-hours; divide that by the current draw in amps and you get hours of runtime. With one catch: you only safely use about eighty percent of a battery's rated capacity, so multiply by that factor first. In the listing, a two amp-hour battery against our draw lasts a bit over two and a half hours. That whole chain — watts per part, total, margin, current draw, runtime — is the examinable calculation.

QUESTION: Your turn — pause the player. A mobile robot draws one-and-a-half amps from its twelve-volt battery. You fit a six amp-hour battery, and you can safely use eighty percent of it. Estimate the runtime in hours. Work it out, then play.

NARRATOR: Usable capacity is six amp-hours times zero-point-eight, which is four-point-eight amp-hours. Divide by the one-and-a-half-amp draw, and you get three-point-two hours. So a little over three hours of operation. The marks come from showing the steps — apply the usable-capacity factor, then amp-hours divided by amps — not just guessing a number. If you wrote three-point-two hours with working, that's full marks.

NARRATOR: The dot-point also says material requirements, so don't forget them. Beyond power, a build needs physical materials chosen for the job: a chassis or enclosure that's strong but not too heavy, wires of the right gauge and the right connectors, and materials that suit the environment — waterproof if it's outdoors, heat-resistant near a motor, able to take vibration. Naming a material requirement and tying it to the operating environment is an easy mark people forget.

NARRATOR: Now the wiring diagram — the blueprint that documents how everything connects. This is a design and modelling skill, a cousin of the structure charts you drew in Programming Fundamentals, and it's exactly what outcome S-E-eleven-oh-six means by applying tools to design a system. Listing 2 draws our thermostat-fan. The key idea to take away is that there are two networks layered on the one machine: power lines feed, and data lines talk, and they all share one common ground. The power lines carry the supply — the positive voltage and the ground return — and the heavy current; by convention red is positive, black is ground. The data, or signal, lines are thin and carry only tiny currents — the temperature sensor's reading going into the controller, and the controller's drive signal going out. Power lines feed; data lines talk; one common ground.

QUESTION: Here's the rule examiners reach for. Why can't you just wire the fan motor straight to one of the controller's output pins and switch it on from there?

NARRATOR: Because a controller's output pin can only supply a tiny current — a few milliamps — and a motor needs amps, hundreds of times more. Wire the motor to the pin and you'll either do nothing or destroy the pin. So you never run an actuator straight off a controller pin. Instead, the pin's small signal switches a driver — a transistor or a motor driver — and that driver feeds the motor from the battery's own power line. The controller decides; the driver delivers the power. That's the single most common power mistake in mechatronics, and naming it — pin can't supply motor current, use a driver fed from the main supply — is a reliable mark. It's also why, in the diagram, the motor sits on its own twelve-volt line, switched by the driver, not on the controller's five-volt logic.

NARRATOR: And the safe-wiring checklist, which Listing 3 collects: colour-code your wires; put a fuse near the battery's positive terminal so a short blows the fuse instead of starting a fire; choose a wire gauge thick enough for the current it carries; add strain relief so a tug doesn't rip a connection off; keep high-voltage and low-voltage parts isolated; tie everything to one common ground; and on anything that moves, an emergency-stop on the main power. Each of those maps to a specific hazard — fuse against shorts, gauge against overheating wires, strain relief against broken joints.

QUESTION: Question one. A system has three components drawing, in total, twenty-four watts, supplied by a twelve-volt battery. Calculate the current the battery must supply, and state the runtime from a four amp-hour battery at eighty percent usable capacity. Pause, work it out, then play.

NARRATOR: Current is power divided by voltage — twenty-four watts divided by twelve volts is two amps. Runtime is usable capacity over draw — four amp-hours times zero-point-eight is three-point-two amp-hours, divided by two amps gives one-point-six hours. So two amps of draw and about one-point-six hours of runtime. The marks are for the two formulas — power over voltage for current, and usable amp-hours over amps for runtime — with working shown. Ignoring the eighty-percent factor is the common slip.

QUESTION: Question two. In a wiring diagram, distinguish between a power line and a data line.

NARRATOR: A power line supplies electrical energy to components — it carries the supply voltage and the return ground, and the larger currents the components draw. A data, or signal, line carries information between components — for example a sensor reading going to the controller or a control signal going out — at very low current. The distinction for the mark: power lines deliver energy to run the parts; data lines carry the small signals that coordinate them. Mentioning that both share a common ground earns the extra point.

QUESTION: Question three. Explain why a motor should not be connected directly to a microcontroller's output pin, and describe what should be done instead.

NARRATOR: A microcontroller's output pin can supply only a very small current, a few milliamps, while a motor requires far more — amps — so connecting the motor directly would fail to drive it and could damage the pin or the microcontroller. Instead, the pin's signal should switch a driver, such as a transistor or motor-driver circuit, which delivers power to the motor from the main battery supply rather than from the pin. The mark-earning points: the pin cannot supply enough current, and a driver powered from the main supply is used, controlled by the pin's signal.

QUESTION: Question four. Identify two safe-wiring practices for a mechatronic system and justify each.

NARRATOR: First, fit a fuse rated to the circuit on the battery's positive line — justified because if a short circuit occurs, the fuse blows and cuts the current before wires overheat or a fire starts. Second, select a wire gauge rated for the expected current — justified because an undersized wire carrying too much current overheats, wasting energy and risking damage or fire. Other valid pairs include colour-coding to prevent wiring errors, strain relief to stop connections breaking, and an emergency stop to cut power instantly. The mark is naming a specific practice and tying it to the concrete hazard it prevents.
