NARRATOR: Start with abstraction, because it's the foundation everything else sits on. Abstraction means focusing on what something does rather than how it does it — hiding unnecessary detail so you can think clearly. The classic example is driving a car. As the driver you work at one level: steering wheel, pedals, gear shift. You do not think about fuel injection, spark timing, or the metallurgy of the brake discs. Those details exist, but they're hidden behind a simple interface — the controls. That's abstraction: layers, each hiding the complexity of the one below. In software it's the same — at the top you see major components like a records store, a grade calculator, a report generator, and each hides its own internal machinery from the others. The exam phrasing to bank: abstraction is "hiding complexity behind a simple interface so you can reason at the right level." You'll meet this word again and again — it's one of the four big ideas of object-oriented programming next module, where it sits right next to encapsulation.

NARRATOR: Now the two directions you can design in. Top-down design starts with the big picture and breaks it down: define the whole problem, split it into major sub-problems, split each of those, and keep going until every piece is simple enough to solve directly. Bottom-up design goes the other way: start with small, proven pieces you already have — library functions, existing modules — and combine them upward into larger capabilities until you've built the whole system. The memory hook: top-down splits; bottom-up builds. Top-down is the natural way humans plan and it guarantees you cover all the requirements, but you can miss low-level realities early and design something that won't actually build. Bottom-up reuses proven components and stays realistic about constraints, but you can lose sight of the big picture. So in practice most projects are hybrid — start top-down to understand the shape, switch to bottom-up where you have proven tools, and iterate.

NARRATOR: As you refine, you're producing a hierarchy of modules — and the tool that draws that hierarchy is the structure chart. A structure chart shows how a program is organised into modules and how those modules relate: who calls whom, and what data passes between them. This is the picture of the decomposition you just did by refinement — and it's exactly the picture of the subprograms you called last episode. At the top sits the main controller; beneath it, the major modules it calls; beneath those, their helpers. A tree of responsibility.

NARRATOR: And here's what separates a structure chart from the flowchart we drew last episode — a classic distinguish question. A flowchart shows control flow: the order steps execute in, with decisions and loops, over time. A structure chart shows the static call hierarchy: which module calls which, and the data that flows between them — not the moment-by-moment execution. The one-liner: a flowchart shows the order things happen; a structure chart shows who calls whom and what data they pass. The symbols matter too — a rectangle is a module, an arrow means "calls", a small circle on the line carries a data item being passed, a diamond marks a conditional call, and a curved arrow marks a repeated call. Those, plus the good-versus-poor module guide, are in Listing 3.

NARRATOR: That raises the real question: what makes a module good? Two terms, and they're the most mark-worthy vocabulary in this whole episode. Cohesion: how related the things inside one module are — you want high cohesion, every part of the module working toward one single job. Coupling: how dependent modules are on each other — you want low coupling, modules as independent as possible so a change in one doesn't ripple through the rest. High cohesion, low coupling — say it as a pair, because that pairing is the answer to "what is a well-designed module." A good module does one clear thing — calculate the average, validate an email, format a currency. The trap, and the marker is hunting for it, is the do-everything module: one giant routine that reads and validates and stores and calculates and displays, all at once. That's low cohesion and usually high coupling — impossible to test, reuse, or change safely. If you can't name a module in a short verb phrase, it's doing too much.

QUESTION: Before I move on — here's one to actually attempt. Pause the player. In your own words, what does "high cohesion, low coupling" mean, and why is a do-everything module the opposite? Work it out, then play.

NARRATOR: High cohesion means everything inside a module belongs to one single, well-defined job. Low coupling means modules depend on each other as little as possible, interacting through clear, narrow interfaces. A do-everything module is the opposite on both counts: it has low cohesion because it bundles unrelated jobs — input, validation, storage, calculation, display — into one box; and it tends toward high coupling because everything reaches into it, so touching it risks breaking many things at once. The mark-earning sentence: "A well-designed module has high cohesion — one responsibility — and low coupling — minimal dependence on others — which makes it easy to test, reuse, and maintain." Notice that's the modularity payoff from the very first episode, made precise.

NARRATOR: Now the second dot-point: two named algorithmic design strategies the syllabus wants you to apply by name. The first is divide and conquer. The idea: if a problem is too big, split it into smaller independent sub-problems of the same kind, solve each one — usually by splitting again, recursively — then combine the results. Split, solve, combine. The textbook example is merge sort: to sort a list, split it in half, sort each half by the same method, then merge the two sorted halves back together. Each split makes the problem half the size; the combining does the real work. Binary search is the same spirit — to find a value in a sorted list, look at the middle, and discard the half it can't be in, halving the search space each step. Merge sort, written and runnable, is in Listing 4. The exam phrasing: "divide and conquer breaks a problem into smaller sub-problems of the same type, solves them independently, and combines their solutions." Split, solve, combine — and notice this is top-down design applied to an algorithm rather than to a whole system.

NARRATOR: The second strategy is backtracking, and it's a different shape entirely. Backtracking is for problems where you build a solution step by step by making choices, and some choices lead to dead ends. The strategy: try a choice; if it leads to a dead end, undo it — back up — and try a different choice. Try, fail, undo, retry. The mental image is a maze: you walk down a corridor; if it dead-ends, you walk back to the last junction and take a different turn; you keep a record of where you've been so you don't loop. The same strategy solves a sudoku — place a digit, and if it makes the puzzle unsolvable later, erase it and try the next — and the eight-queens puzzle, and many scheduling problems. A worked maze solver using backtracking, runnable, is in Listing 5. The exam phrasing: "backtracking incrementally builds a solution and abandons a partial choice — backtracks — as soon as it cannot lead to a valid complete solution, then tries an alternative."

QUESTION: So how do I keep divide-and-conquer and backtracking straight? They both break problems up.

NARRATOR: They feel similar but the distinction is sharp, and it's a likely exam pairing. Divide and conquer splits a problem into independent sub-problems, solves all of them, and combines the answers — the splitting is the whole plan, and you use every piece. Backtracking explores choices one at a time down a single path, and throws work away when a choice fails — it's trial-and-error with an undo button. Divide and conquer divides up front and conquers all the pieces; backtracking gropes forward and retreats when stuck. Split-solve-combine versus try-fail-undo-retry. If the sub-problems are independent and you'll use them all, it's divide and conquer; if you're searching among choices where some are dead ends, it's backtracking.

NARRATOR: Now the exam-style questions. Pen down, and when I say pause, attempt it before the model answer.

QUESTION: Question one, recall. What do the terms cohesion and coupling mean, and what does a well-designed module aim for?

NARRATOR: Cohesion is how strongly the elements within a single module belong together — how focused it is on one job. Coupling is the degree of interdependence between separate modules. A well-designed module aims for high cohesion — a single, clear responsibility — and low coupling — minimal dependence on other modules. The extra mark is the consequence: this makes modules easier to test, reuse, and maintain independently.

QUESTION: Question two. Distinguish between top-down and bottom-up design. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Top-down design begins with the complete problem and decomposes it into progressively smaller sub-problems until each is directly solvable; it gives a clear overview and ensures requirements are covered, but may overlook implementation detail early. Bottom-up design begins with small, existing, proven components and combines them into larger units up to the full system; it maximises reuse and stays realistic about constraints, but risks losing the big picture. Most real projects use a hybrid. Full marks: both directions defined, a strength or trade-off for each, and no claim that one is universally better.

QUESTION: Question three. Explain divide and conquer as an algorithmic design strategy, using an example.

NARRATOR: Divide and conquer solves a problem by dividing it into smaller sub-problems of the same type, solving each independently — often by dividing again recursively — and then combining their solutions into the answer to the original. For example, merge sort sorts a list by splitting it into two halves, sorting each half by the same method, and merging the two sorted halves back into one sorted list. The marks: name the three phases — divide, solve, combine — and give a correct example that actually fits the pattern. A weak answer just says "it breaks the problem into smaller parts," which doesn't distinguish it from ordinary decomposition; the strong answer stresses the same-type sub-problems and the combine step.

QUESTION: Question four. A team writes a single module that reads input, validates it, calculates results, and displays them. Identify the design weakness and explain how to improve it.

NARRATOR: The weakness is low cohesion — the module has more than one responsibility, bundling input, validation, calculation, and display into one unit — and it likely creates high coupling, since everything depends on this one module. The improvement is to decompose it, by stepwise refinement, into separate single-responsibility modules — one to read input, one to validate, one to calculate, one to display — each with high cohesion, coordinated by a controller, and connected through clear interfaces with low coupling. The mark-earning terms are cohesion, coupling, single responsibility, and decomposition.

QUESTION: Question five, distinguish. Distinguish between divide and conquer and backtracking.

NARRATOR: Divide and conquer breaks a problem into independent sub-problems of the same type, solves all of them, and combines their results — for example merge sort. Backtracking builds a solution incrementally by trying choices, and when a partial choice cannot lead to a valid complete solution, it undoes that choice and tries an alternative — for example solving a maze or a sudoku. The key contrast: divide and conquer partitions the problem up front and uses every sub-solution; backtracking explores choices sequentially and discards the ones that dead-end. Split-solve-combine versus try-fail-undo-retry. Naming both phases and giving one fitting example each earns full marks.
