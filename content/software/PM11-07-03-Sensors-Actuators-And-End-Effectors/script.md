QUESTION: Quick framing question. If a robot arm has a motor that swings it and a gripper that grabs a part — is the gripper just another actuator?

NARRATOR: No — and this is the single most examinable distinction in the lesson, so hold it tight. An actuator is the thing that creates the motion or force — the muscle. The motor that swings the arm is the actuator. An end-effector is the tool that the motion drives — the hand at the end that performs the task. The gripper is the end-effector. The motor moves; the gripper does the job. Same idea on an excavator: the hydraulic pistons are the actuators, the bucket is the end-effector. Confuse those two and you drop the mark; keep "actuator moves, end-effector does the job" and you keep it.

NARRATOR: Start with sensors — the Sense. A sensor converts something physical into an electrical signal the controller can read; sensors are the eyes and ears of the system. The syllabus names two families you must know. First, motion sensors. The passive-infrared sensor — the kind in a security light — detects movement by sensing changes in infrared heat; it's cheap and great for "is someone there?", but slow, a second or two, and poor at pinpointing exactly where. An accelerometer measures acceleration and tilt, reacts in milliseconds, and is what senses orientation or a phone being turned. And an encoder counts pulses to track rotation or position extremely precisely — it's how a motor knows exactly how far it's turned, essential for robotics. Second family, light-level sensors. A photocell, also called a light-dependent resistor, changes its resistance with light — cheap, fine for day-versus-night switching, but rough. A photodiode produces a current proportional to light, and is fast and precise enough for optical communication.

NARRATOR: One detail that connects straight back to last episode. Sensors come in two signal flavours. A digital sensor sends a clean zero-or-one the microcontroller can read directly off a pin. But an analog sensor produces a continuously varying voltage — and the chip can't read that directly. It has to pass through an analog-to-digital converter first, which turns the smooth voltage into a number. Remember the input-output pins from last episode? This is what hangs off them, and the analog ones need that converter in between. We'll pick this up properly next episode when we talk about sensor data.

NARRATOR: Now actuators — the moving part of the Act. An actuator converts an electrical signal into motion or force. The syllabus singles out hydraulic actuators, so know them: they use pressurised fluid — usually oil — pushed against a piston, and the force they produce equals the pressure times the piston's area. That gives enormous force — thousands of newtons — which is why hydraulics drive excavators, industrial presses, and aircraft control surfaces. The trade-off: they need a pump and a lot of power, and they're heavy. For lighter, cleaner, precise jobs you'd reach for an electric motor or servo instead; for fast, light motion, pneumatics using air. But when the answer is "huge force," it's hydraulics.

NARRATOR: And end-effectors — the tool that finishes the job. The most common is the robotic gripper, and there are several types worth naming. A parallel-jaw gripper has two fingers that close straight together — good for cylinders and consistent shapes. An angular gripper's fingers rotate closed — good for varied or delicate items, very precise. A vacuum gripper uses suction — good for flat, light things like a sheet or a box. And a magnetic gripper holds ferrous metal with a magnet — simple on-or-off, but only for iron and steel. Beyond grippers, an end-effector could be a drill, a welder, a spray head — whatever actually performs the task. Listing 1 lays the whole trio out on one machine — a pick-and-place arm — so you can see the sensor, the actuator, and the end-effector each doing its part of Sense-Think-Act.

NARRATOR: So how do you choose? Every component is a balance, and here's a hook for the four pulls — P-C-R-I: Performance — accuracy, speed, range; Cost; Reliability — durability and tolerance of the environment; and Integration — does it physically fit and connect. You weigh those four, and then you decide on the task's dominant requirement, exactly like the "name the dominant requirement" move from episode one. Is this task really about precision? Force? Speed? Cost? That dominant requirement breaks the tie.

NARRATOR: Listing 2 makes that concrete and runnable. We've got four grippers and a task: place delicate circuit boards — a light grip, tight positioning, two seconds a cycle. The model checks each gripper three ways: strong enough, precise enough, and fast enough. All four happen to clear the numeric thresholds. So we fall back on the dominant requirement — precision, because the boards are delicate — and the model picks the angular gripper, the most precise of the suitable options. That's the exam move in code: screen on the hard requirements, then choose on what dominates.

QUESTION: Question one. Distinguish between an actuator and an end-effector, and give an example of each. Three marks.

NARRATOR: An actuator is a component that converts an electrical signal into motion or force — for example an electric motor or a hydraulic piston that moves a robotic arm. An end-effector is the tool at the end of the system that performs the actual task, driven by that motion — for example a gripper that grasps an object, or a drill. The distinction for the mark: the actuator produces the movement; the end-effector is what that movement is used to do the job with. For three marks, define both and give a correct, distinct example of each — don't let your two examples be the same kind of thing.

QUESTION: Question two. A robotic system on a packing line must detect a cardboard box arriving and lift it onto a pallet. Identify a suitable sensor, actuator, and end-effector, and justify each choice. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: A suitable sensor is a photocell or motion sensor to detect the box breaking a light beam as it arrives — it signals when to act. A suitable actuator is an electric or pneumatic actuator to lift and move the arm — enough force for a light box, and clean and fast. A suitable end-effector is a vacuum gripper, because a cardboard box has a flat top surface that suction holds well without crushing it. The mark-earning move is matching each device to a specific feature of the task — a flat box suits a vacuum gripper, an arriving box needs a position or beam sensor — and naming one device in each of the three S-A-E roles.

QUESTION: Question three. Describe two different types of motion sensor and state a suitable application for each.

NARRATOR: A passive-infrared sensor detects movement by sensing changes in infrared heat; it's well suited to a security light or occupancy detector, where you only need to know that something moved, not precisely where. An encoder counts pulses to measure rotation or position very precisely; it's well suited to motor and robot control, where the system must know exactly how far a joint has turned. The marks come from describing how each sensor works and pairing it with an application that actually needs that sensor's strength — rough presence detection for the passive-infrared, precise position for the encoder.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain why an analog sensor cannot be connected directly to a microcontroller's input, and what is required.

NARRATOR: An analog sensor produces a continuously varying voltage, but a microcontroller works in discrete digital numbers and cannot interpret a raw analog voltage directly. An analog-to-digital converter is required: it samples the varying voltage and converts it into a digital number the processor can read and use. The mark is identifying the analog-versus-digital mismatch and naming the analog-to-digital converter as the bridge — not just saying "you plug it in."
