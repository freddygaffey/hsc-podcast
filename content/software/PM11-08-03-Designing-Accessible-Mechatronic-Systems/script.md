NARRATOR: Objectives. By the end you'll be able to: define a specialist requirement and the golden rule of accessible design; list the specialist requirements that shape an assistive system; explain universal design and why it benefits everyone; and name the safety and interface features these systems need.

NARRATOR: Let's define the key term first. A specialist requirement is a requirement that comes from the user's specific disability and shapes the design — not a generic "make it easy to use," but something concrete the user's needs demand. And before any list, the single most important principle, the one that earns marks and prevents disasters: design with users, not for them. You consult the actual people who'll use the system, throughout — because you cannot guess someone's needs from the outside. The disability community puts it as "nothing about us without us." If your exam answer says the team designed a device for disabled users without involving any, that's the flaw to call out.

QUESTION: So what are these specialist requirements, concretely? If I'm building a powered wheelchair, what do I actually have to get right?

NARRATOR: Here's a checklist hook — S-A-I-D, which is handy because it's literally what the user said they need. S, Safety: a person with a disability may not be able to react quickly to danger, so safety has to be robust — multiple low-force emergency stops, force limits, timeouts, fail-safe behaviour. A, Adaptability: the system must adjust to the individual, because no two users are the same and needs change over time — adjustable control sensitivity, interface height, choice of input method. I, Independence: it must preserve the user's dignity and autonomy — they do it themselves, with privacy, not relying on a carer for every action. And D, Diverse inputs and outputs: offer alternative ways in and out, because not everyone can press a button. S-A-I-D — Safety, Adaptability, Independence, Diverse inputs and outputs. And two more to keep in your pocket: reliability, because people depend on these daily, and affordability, because a brilliant device nobody can afford helps nobody. Listing 1 lays out that checklist with worked examples — a wheelchair, a prosthetic hand, an eye-gaze aid — each tagged with its dominant requirements.

NARRATOR: On that D — diverse inputs and outputs — get specific, because it's very examinable. Traditional controls like buttons and joysticks don't work for everyone, so accessible systems offer alternative inputs: a switch, activated by a head movement, a sip-and-puff, or a blink; voice control, which has to cope with varied speech, including speech affected by conditions like cerebral palsy; and eye-gaze, where you select by looking and dwelling. And feedback should be multi-modal — here's a hook, V-A-H: Visual, Audio, and Haptic, that last one meaning vibration. Offer more than one channel, so a user who can't see the screen hears or feels the response. Visual, Audio, Haptic.

NARRATOR: Let me make it concrete with the safety-and-force idea, because that's where mechatronics meets accessibility most sharply. Listing 2 is a runnable accessible control. The clever bit is that it sizes itself to the individual: instead of a fixed button force, it activates at a gentle fraction — about thirty percent — of the maximum force that particular user can comfortably apply, so someone with limited strength isn't shut out. At the same time it enforces a hard safety limit: if a force comes in above what's safe, the control refuses it outright — vital when a user may not feel or notice a dangerous force. And it honours an emergency stop: once stopped, nothing moves until it's reset. A light accidental brush does nothing; a deliberate gentle press works; a dangerous force is blocked; and the emergency stop overrides everything. That's the S and the A of S-A-I-D in a few lines — Safety and Adaptability made real.

NARRATOR: Now zoom out to the design philosophy: universal design, also called inclusive design. The idea is to design from the very start for the widest possible range of people, rather than building for an imagined "average" user and bolting on accessibility later. And here's the beautiful part, the thing to remember — the curb-cut effect. Curb cuts, those little ramps in a kerb, were put in for wheelchairs, but they help everyone: people with prams, delivery trolleys, travellers with suitcases. Build for the margins and everyone benefits. Captions, voice control, big clear buttons — all started as accessibility features and became things we all use. So accessible design isn't charity bolted on; it makes the system more robust and usable for everybody. Listing 3 condenses the seven formal principles of universal design into a reference card.

QUESTION: Pause the player and try this. You're designing an eye-gaze-controlled communication aid for someone who can move only their eyes. Name two specialist requirements that would shape your design, and justify each. Work it out, then play.

NARRATOR: Here's a strong answer. One: an eye-gaze input method with a dwell-to-select mechanism, justified because the user can't use hands or voice, so gaze is their only reliable channel — and a deliberate dwell time prevents every glance from triggering a selection. Two: fatigue-aware timing and adjustable speed, justified because sustained eye control is tiring, so the system must adapt its pace to the individual and not exhaust them. You could equally name clear multi-modal feedback so they know a selection registered, or dignity — that it speaks in a voice they'd choose, restoring their own communication. The marks come from naming a requirement that genuinely flows from the disability and tying it to why — not a generic "make it user-friendly."

NARRATOR: Now exam-style questions. Pen down, and when I say pause, attempt before the model answer.

QUESTION: Question one. Describe two specialist requirements that influence the design of a mechatronic system designed for a person with a disability, and justify each. Four marks.

NARRATOR: One: adjustable, low-force controls sized to the individual — justified because users vary widely in strength and dexterity, so a control activating at a gentle fraction of the user's own comfortable force lets people with limited strength operate it while avoiding accidental activation. Two: robust, redundant safety systems such as multiple low-force emergency stops and force limits — justified because a user with a disability may not be able to react quickly to a hazard, so the system must fail safe and be stoppable with minimal effort. For four marks, give two distinct requirements, each tied to a specific user need — not two versions of "easy to use."

QUESTION: Question two. Explain what is meant by universal design, and give one way it benefits users beyond those with disabilities. Pause, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Universal, or inclusive, design means designing a system from the outset to be usable by the widest possible range of people, including those with disabilities, rather than designing for an average user and adding accessibility afterwards. A benefit beyond the target users is the curb-cut effect: features built for accessibility help everyone — for example, voice control or large clear controls, originally for users with limited mobility or vision, are convenient for all users. The marks are for defining design-from-the-start-for-all and giving a concrete everyone-benefits example.

QUESTION: Question three. Explain why safety systems are especially important in mechatronic systems designed for people with disability.

NARRATOR: Because users with disabilities may have a reduced ability to perceive a hazard or to react quickly to stop a dangerous action — for example reduced sensation, slower movement, or a visual impairment — so the system cannot rely on the user to protect themselves. Therefore it must provide robust, redundant safety: multiple emergency stops operable with minimal force, force limiting so the actuator can't apply a harmful force, and automatic timeouts or shutdown. The mark-earning point is linking the user's reduced capacity to respond to the need for stronger, automatic, low-effort safety mechanisms.

QUESTION: Question four. A design team builds an assistive device for people with disability but does not involve any such users in the process. Explain one risk of this approach and state a better one.

NARRATOR: The risk is that the team designs around assumptions about the users' needs that are wrong or incomplete, producing a device that doesn't actually fit how people live and use it — wasting effort and potentially excluding the very users it was meant to help. The better approach is inclusive, participatory design: involve real users throughout, from gathering requirements to testing, so the design reflects genuine needs — "design with users, not for them." The mark is identifying the wrong-assumptions risk and naming consultation or participatory design as the fix.
