NARRATOR: On the first of October, twenty thirteen, the United States government launched what was meant to be the centrepiece of a landmark law — a website called Healthcare dot gov, where millions of Americans would shop for and sign up to health insurance. The political stakes could not have been higher; this was the public face of the entire Affordable Care Act. Millions of people came to the site on day one. And on that first day, by most accounts, the number of people who managed to get all the way through and actually enrol was about six. Not six thousand. Six. The flagship website of the world's most powerful government, years in the making, collapsed almost the instant it met the public. This is the story of the Healthcare dot gov launch — and it is the case study that ties together your entire project module, because nearly every classic project-management mistake is in here, stacked one on top of another.

NARRATOR: Let me be clear up front about what kind of failure this was, because that's the lesson. This was not one bad programmer or one disastrous line of code. The eventual rescue proved the underlying technology could be made to work. This was a failure of how the project was scoped, planned, managed, and tested — a software-engineering-project failure, exactly the discipline your module is about. Listing one lays out the timeline. So let me walk through how a government with effectively unlimited resources built a website that could serve six people.

NARRATOR: Start with the ambition and the complexity, because they were real. Healthcare dot gov was not a simple shopping site. It had to pull together dozens of different systems — verifying people's identity and income against multiple government agencies, checking eligibility, talking to many private insurance companies, all in real time, for the entire country. It was a genuinely huge integration problem. And it was built by many separate contractors, coordinated by a government agency that was acting as the integrator but was, frankly, overwhelmed by the role. So immediately you have a classic risk: many pieces, many teams, and no single person clearly accountable for whether the whole thing actually worked end to end.

NARRATOR: Now add the deadline. The launch date — the first of October, twenty thirteen — was fixed, and it was political. It could not move. It was written into law and into the national calendar; slipping it was simply not an option in the way a normal project might quietly push its date back. Hold that thought, because a deadline that genuinely cannot move is one of the most dangerous things in all of project management — not because deadlines are bad, but because of what teams do to hit them.

QUESTION: So think about it as a project manager. The system is enormously complex, the requirements are still being finalised late and keep changing, the work is running behind — and the deadline absolutely cannot move. Something has to give. What gets sacrificed?

NARRATOR: Testing. Almost always, testing. And this is where the choice of methodology turns a hard situation into a catastrophe. The project ran in a broadly waterfall style — the sequential approach where you do each phase once, in order: gather all the requirements, then design, then build, and then, near the very end, test. Remember the shorthand: waterfall falls once. And waterfall has a built-in trap under a fixed deadline. Because testing sits at the end, when everything earlier runs late but the deadline can't move, testing is the phase that gets crushed — squeezed from months into days. There was no fully working, integrated system to actually try until right before launch. End-to-end testing and serious load testing happened only days before going live, which meant the problems they revealed were discovered far too late to fix. An iterative or Agile approach — building and testing a working slice early, then growing it — would have surfaced these failures months earlier, when there was still time. Instead, the first real test of the whole system at scale was the public, on launch day.

NARRATOR: And the most damning specific failure was load. The system had been checked against a modest number of simultaneous users — and then a tidal wave arrived. Listing two shows the trap in runnable form. Imagine a system built to handle around a thousand simultaneous users. Test it against the thousand you planned for, and it passes — zero failures, everyone's happy, ship it. But on launch day, hundreds of thousands of people arrive at once. Run that real load against the same system and it serves under one percent of them; everyone else gets errors and spinning wheels. The point is brutal and simple: "it passed our test" is meaningless if the test load wasn't realistic. They had tested against the load they hoped for, not the load they would actually get. Load testing only tells you anything if you test at the real expected scale — and early enough to do something about the answer.

NARRATOR: Now, here's the more hopeful half of the story, because it's just as instructive. The site was not abandoned. The government brought in a rescue team — a "tech surge" of experienced engineers, including people from the private web industry who had built large-scale sites before. And over about two months, they turned it around. How? Not with magic. They did the things the project should have done all along: they measured the system's actual behaviour under real load, found where it was actually breaking — as opposed to where people assumed it was breaking — prioritised the highest-impact fixes, added capacity, and tested against realistic traffic. By December, the site largely worked, and millions of people enrolled. The recovery is a case study in honest evaluation: measure against real criteria, gather real evidence, reflect on what it tells you, and act on the biggest problems first.

NARRATOR: So let me draw the lessons together, and Listing three maps them. The first: a fixed, immovable deadline colliding with late, unstable requirements is a red flag you must take seriously. The honest move is to reconcile the deadline with feasibility up front — your TECO check, Technical, Economic, Cost-and-schedule, and Operational feasibility — and if the full scope genuinely cannot be delivered safely by an unmovable date, then you reduce the scope, you launch to a limited group first, you do something — rather than promising the impossible and discovering it in public.

NARRATOR: The second lesson is about methodology: waterfall under a tight deadline crushes testing, because testing lives at the end. When requirements are uncertain and the timeline is at risk, an iterative approach that produces a working, testable system early is far safer, because it front-loads the discovery of problems to when you can still fix them. Waterfall falls once; if the fall lands badly, there's no second attempt before the deadline.

NARRATOR: The third lesson is the load-testing one, and it's the most concrete: test at realistic scale, and test early. Performance and load testing are not optional polish for a system that millions will hit at once — they are the whole ballgame, and a pass against an unrealistic load is worse than no test, because it gives false confidence. And the fourth: big integrated projects need clear, single accountability and continuous integration testing, so that "do all these pieces actually work together?" is answered constantly, not discovered on launch day.

QUESTION: And here's a reflective one to close on, because the recovery holds the deepest lesson. The same people, the same broad technology, the same enormous complexity — yet in October it served six people and by December it served millions. What actually changed?

NARRATOR: Not the talent, and not the difficulty of the problem. What changed was the engineering discipline applied to it: measuring reality instead of trusting assumptions, testing against real load instead of hoped-for load, fixing the highest-impact problems first instead of everything at once, and being honest about what was actually broken. That is the entire project module in a sentence — that success or failure in software at scale is decided less by raw cleverness than by feasibility, planning, testing, and honest evaluation. The technology was always capable of working. It only started working when it was managed and tested properly. So when you build something that real people will depend on, remember the launch that served six: ask whether the deadline and the scope can both be true, build something you can test early, test it at the load it will really face, and never let "it passed our test" mean anything less than a test of the real thing. The difference between six users and millions was not genius. It was discipline.
