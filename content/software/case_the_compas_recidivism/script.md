NARRATOR: Imagine you are a judge. In front of you is a person who has just been arrested, and you have to make a decision that will change their life — whether to grant them bail, how to sentence them, whether to release them on parole. And to help you, you are handed a number. A piece of software has read this person's file, run it through a model, and produced a score from one to ten — a prediction of how likely they are to commit another crime. A low number, and you might lean toward leniency. A high number, and you might lean toward keeping them locked up. The number looks objective. It looks scientific. It looks fairer than a tired human judge going on a hunch. And in two thousand and sixteen, a group of journalists set out to check whether it actually was — and what they found launched one of the most important debates in the history of artificial intelligence, a debate about a word we all use as if it were simple: fair.

NARRATOR: This is the story of COMPAS — a criminal risk-assessment tool used in courtrooms across the United States — and of the argument it started about what fairness even means. It is the perfect Year-Twelve case study for the ethics and bias topic, because, unlike a simple "the model was wrong" story, this one has no easy villain and no easy answer. In fact, its deepest lesson is mathematical: that there are several different, perfectly reasonable definitions of "fair," and that some of them cannot all be true at the same time. I want to handle this carefully and accurately, because it touches real people's liberty and a genuinely sensitive subject, so I'm going to stick closely to the facts and the mathematics. Listing one lays out the timeline.

NARRATOR: COMPAS, made by a company then called Northpointe, worked by taking a defendant's answers to a long questionnaire and producing that recidivism risk score — the likelihood of reoffending. Courts used it as one input into bail, sentencing and parole decisions. And here is the first thing to notice, before we even get to bias: the model was a black box. Its inner workings were proprietary — a commercial secret. So a defendant could be handed a high-risk score that influenced years of their life, and neither they nor, often, the judge could see exactly how that number had been calculated. Hold that thought, because transparency comes back at the end.

NARRATOR: In May two thousand and sixteen, the investigative newsroom ProPublica published an analysis of COMPAS scores for around seven thousand people who had been arrested in one county in Florida. They compared the scores the tool had given to what actually happened next — who really did reoffend, and who did not. And they found a stark racial disparity. Among the people who did not go on to reoffend, Black defendants were about twice as likely as white defendants to have been wrongly labelled high-risk by the software. And in the other direction, white defendants who did go on to reoffend were more likely to have been wrongly labelled low-risk. In plain terms: when the tool made a mistake, its mistakes tended to fall against Black defendants. That is a serious, specific finding about what are called false-positive rates — being flagged as a risk when you are not.

NARRATOR: Northpointe pushed back, and here is where it gets genuinely subtle, because their defence was also true. They argued that their tool was calibrated — and calibration is a real, respectable definition of fairness. It means this: take everyone the tool gave a particular score, say a seven out of ten. Among those people, the proportion who actually went on to reoffend was about the same regardless of race. A seven meant the same thing, in terms of real-world risk, whether you were Black or white. By that measure — which has a name, predictive parity — the tool was treating the races even-handedly. So you had two camps, each holding up a number, each saying "this proves it is fair" and "this proves it is not" — and, astonishingly, both of them were right.

QUESTION: So pause on that, because it sounds impossible. How can a single tool be fair by one reasonable measure and unfair by another, at the very same time, with both sides doing their maths correctly?

NARRATOR: Because "fair" is not one thing. There are multiple, competing definitions of fairness, and they are measuring different things. One definition says: the score should mean the same risk for everyone — calibration. Another definition says: the tool should make wrong accusations at equal rates for everyone — equal false-positive rates. And in two thousand and sixteen, researchers proved something that turned this from an argument into a theorem: when the underlying rate of reoffending differs between two groups — when the base rates are different, as they were here — it is mathematically impossible to satisfy both of those fairness definitions at once. Not difficult. Impossible. You can have equal calibration, or equal error rates, but not both. Listing two shows this with a tiny worked example: two groups, the same model, deliberately built so that the tool is equally calibrated for both — the same precision — and yet its false-positive rate is much higher for one group than the other. Equal by one definition, unequal by the other, in the same numbers, at the same time. That is not a bug you can fix. It is a fundamental trade-off baked into the mathematics.

NARRATOR: So let me draw out the lessons, because this is the case that should permanently change how you hear the word "fair." Listing three maps them. The first lesson: there is no single definition of fairness — there are several, and they can be mutually exclusive. When someone tells you their algorithm is "fair," the only sensible response is: fair according to which definition? Calibration? Equal error rates? Equal acceptance rates? They are different questions with different answers, and a model can pass one while failing another. In your bias topic, fairness metrics are plural for exactly this reason.

NARRATOR: The second lesson follows directly, and it's the deep one: because you cannot satisfy every fairness definition at once, you have to choose which one matters most for your situation — and that choice is not a technical decision. It is a value judgement, an ethical and political one, about which kind of mistake is worse. Is it worse to wrongly hold someone who would not have reoffended, or to wrongly release someone who would? Different people, and different societies, answer that differently — and no amount of clever programming can make that choice for you. The engineer's job is not to pretend the maths has a single right answer; it is to make the trade-off explicit, so that the humans responsible can decide it openly, rather than having it hidden inside a black box.

NARRATOR: Which brings the third lesson, the human stakes. This was not a model recommending films or pricing umbrellas. Its outputs touched people's freedom — bail, sentencing, parole. When automation moves into a space like criminal justice, every error has a face: a person held who should have been free, or freed who should have been held. The impact on individuals and on society is exactly what your course asks you to weigh, and it could hardly be higher than it is here. A statistic that looks small on a dashboard is, on the ground, someone's months or years.

NARRATOR: And the fourth lesson returns to that black box. A score used to help decide a person's liberty was generated by a system the person could not inspect or meaningfully challenge. High-stakes automated decisions demand transparency — the ability to see and contest how a decision was reached — and accountability, meaning a human being who is answerable for it. "The algorithm said so" is not a justification you would accept if it were your freedom on the line. A risk score should inform a human judgement, with a human responsible for it — never quietly replace it.

QUESTION: So here's the question I want to leave you with, because it has no clean answer and that is precisely the point. If you were the engineer asked to build a tool like this, and you now know you cannot make it fair by every definition at once — what would you do?

NARRATOR: There is no single correct answer, and anyone who gives you one too quickly hasn't understood the problem. But there is a correct way to approach it. You would refuse to pretend the choice doesn't exist. You would surface the trade-off — show, in plain terms, which definition of fairness the tool optimises and which it sacrifices, and what that means for real people. You would insist the system be transparent and challengeable, and that a human stays accountable for every decision. You would measure the outcomes across groups, openly, rather than hiding behind a single comforting number. And you would be honest that you are making a value choice, not just a technical one, and make sure the people with the authority and the responsibility — not the algorithm — are the ones who make it. COMPAS teaches us that the hardest problems in artificial intelligence are not the engineering problems. They are the human ones underneath, the ones the maths forces into the open and then refuses to answer for us. "Fair" was never a single number. And the moment a machine starts making decisions about people, that stops being a philosophy-class abstraction and becomes the most important question in the room.
