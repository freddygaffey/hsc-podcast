NARRATOR: In November twenty twenty-one, one of the biggest names in American real estate, the company Zillow, did something extraordinary: it announced it was shutting down an entire business unit, taking a loss of hundreds of millions of dollars, and laying off about a quarter of its whole workforce — roughly two thousand people. It was left holding thousands of houses it had bought for more than they were now worth. And the reason was not fraud, or a market crash that ruined everyone, or bad management in the ordinary sense. The reason was a machine-learning model — a price-prediction algorithm — that had been confidently, expensively wrong. Zillow had let an algorithm buy houses, by the thousand, on the strength of its forecasts. And the forecasts didn't come true.

NARRATOR: This is the story of Zillow Offers, and it's the case study that teaches you, in the bluntest possible way, the difference between a prediction and a fact. Everything you study about machine learning for forecasting — that a model predicts a number, that the number comes with uncertainty, that a model fitted to the past can fail when the future stops resembling it — all of it is here, with a price tag in the hundreds of millions attached. So let me tell you how an algorithm bought too many houses. Listing one lays out the timeline.

NARRATOR: You probably know Zillow as the site where you look up what houses are worth — its famous "Zestimate," an estimated value for almost any home, produced by a machine-learning model. That model was Zillow's crown jewel, and it gave the company an idea for a new and much more ambitious business, called iBuying. The idea was this: instead of just estimating what a house is worth, use the model to make an actual cash offer, buy the house directly from the owner, do a little work on it, and resell it for a small profit. Quick and convenient for the seller, and a tidy margin for Zillow — repeated across thousands and thousands of homes. And the entire business rested on one thing: the model being accurate about what a house would sell for in the near future. If the model was right, Zillow made a small profit on each house. If it was wrong, Zillow had overpaid — and owned the mistake, literally, as a house on its books.

NARRATOR: Notice the shape of what they'd done, because it's the heart of the lesson. Forecasting future prices is a regression problem — predicting a number — and it's the F in the applications set you've learned, F-A-I: Forecasting, Assistants, Image recognition. And a regression model does not hand you a fact. It hands you a best guess, with uncertainty around it. On the site, where the Zestimate is just a helpful figure for a curious browser, that uncertainty is harmless. But Zillow had taken that same kind of prediction and wired it directly to a chequebook, at enormous scale. They had turned a best-guess number into thousands of binding, real-money purchases.

QUESTION: So pause and think about the risk they'd built. A model that predicts prices works beautifully while the market keeps behaving the way it did in the model's training data. What happens to that model the moment the market does something new?

NARRATOR: It keeps predicting as if nothing has changed — confidently, and wrongly. And in twenty twenty-one, the housing market became volatile and shifted in ways the recent past hadn't shown. The model had effectively learned "prices have been rising, so prices will keep rising," and it kept extrapolating that trend even as the ground moved under it. Listing two shows this in miniature: a simple forecaster that has seen prices climb steadily and so confidently predicts the climb continues — and then the actual price turns and falls. The model had already made its offer at the higher number. It bought high, into a market that was no longer going its way. This has a name in machine learning: distribution shift — when the live data the model meets stops matching the data it was trained on. The model wasn't broken. The world had simply changed, and the model had no way to know, because all it had ever seen was the old world.

NARRATOR: And here is what turned a forecasting error into a corporate disaster: scale. A single house bought twenty-five thousand dollars too high is a painful but survivable mistake. But Zillow wasn't buying one house on this model — it was buying thousands, automatically, fast. Listing two makes the point with a toy figure: that same per-house overpayment, multiplied across a thousand houses, is already tens of millions of dollars; across Zillow's real operation it ran into the hundreds of millions. This is the brutal arithmetic of automation: when you wire a model directly to action at scale, you multiply not just its successes but its errors. The very thing that made iBuying exciting — that the algorithm could act thousands of times without a human in the loop — is exactly what made it catastrophic when the algorithm was wrong.

NARRATOR: So let me pull out the lessons, because they map straight onto your forecasting and regression topics, and Listing three lays them out. The first: a model's output is a prediction, not a guarantee. A forecast is a number wrapped in uncertainty, and the moment you treat it as a certainty — as a price you'll definitely get — you've thrown away the safety margin that the uncertainty was warning you to keep. Zillow's model was probably not even especially bad at forecasting; the failure was in treating its forecasts as solid enough to bet the business on, with thin margins and little room for error.

NARRATOR: The second lesson is the one about overfitting and distribution shift, and it's the deepest. A model learns the patterns in its training data — and if you trust it too much, you are betting that the future will keep resembling the past. When the past is a smooth rising trend, a model can look like a genius right up until the trend breaks, and then it's confidently wrong in exactly the moment it matters most. This is why, when you train a model, you hold back a separate test set and you stay humble about how it will behave on data it has never seen — because the real world is not a fixed dataset. It moves. And a model that was never tested against a changing world will meet that change for the first time in production, with your money on the line.

NARRATOR: The third lesson is error times scale. Automating a decision multiplies both its upside and its downside. Before you let a model act automatically, thousands of times, with real consequences, you have to ask: what is the cost if it's wrong — not on one case, but on all of them at once? And you build guardrails for that case: caps on how much you'll expose, brakes that trip when the model's predictions start drifting from reality, and a human watching the aggregate, not just the individual decisions. Zillow had wired the model to act boldly and fast, and the guardrails for "what if it's systematically wrong?" weren't strong enough to stop it in time.

QUESTION: And there's a quieter, more admirable lesson in how it ended. When Zillow's leadership realised the model could not reliably predict prices in this market, and that the iBuying business was therefore unsafe — what did they do?

NARRATOR: They shut it down. They took the loss, closed the unit, and stopped — rather than doubling down and hoping the model would come good. It was a painful, public, expensive retreat, and it was the right call, in the same spirit as the companies that scrapped a biased hiring model rather than ship it. Knowing when to switch a system off — when to admit that a model is not good enough to be trusted with real stakes — is a genuine engineering virtue, even when it costs a fortune and a lot of pride. The failure was betting so much on the forecast in the first place; the recovery was being willing to stop.

NARRATOR: So what do you carry away from an algorithm that bought too many houses? That a prediction is not a promise — a forecasting model gives you a number with uncertainty, and the uncertainty is the part you ignore at your peril. That a model trained on the past is implicitly betting the future will look the same, and the world does not cooperate — overfitting and distribution shift are not abstractions, they're how a clever model becomes an expensive one. That automating a decision at scale multiplies its errors as faithfully as its wins, so you must guard against the systematic failure, not just the individual one. And that sometimes the bravest, most professional thing an engineer or a company can do is to look at a model holding the wheel and decide to take it back. A model can tell you what a house might be worth. It cannot promise you that the world will still agree tomorrow. Build as if it might not.
