NARRATOR: On the night of the thirty-first of December, nineteen ninety-nine, a large part of the human race held its breath. In control rooms at power stations, in the back offices of banks, in airports and hospitals and government departments, engineers sat watching clocks, waiting for midnight — not to celebrate, but because nobody was completely sure what would happen when the year rolled over from nineteen ninety-nine to two thousand. There were genuine fears that planes might fall out of the sky, that bank balances might vanish, that power grids might go dark. People stockpiled food and water. Governments spent fortunes. And the thing they were all afraid of was not a war, or a storm, or a terrorist. It was a tiny shortcut, made by programmers decades earlier, to save a couple of digits.

NARRATOR: This is the story of the Y2K bug — the Year Two Thousand problem — and it is one of the most useful stories you will ever hear as a programmer, because it is, at its very heart, a story about something that sounds boring and turns out to be life-and-death: how you choose to represent data. Everything you are learning about data types and storage in this module — how a number is stored, how many digits it takes, what range it can hold — Y2K is the moment all of that stopped being an abstract classroom topic and became a global emergency. So let me tell you how a sensible little decision, made with the best of intentions, quietly grew into one of the largest engineering efforts in human history.

NARRATOR: Go back to the nineteen sixties and seventies. Computers then were almost unimaginably limited compared to the phone in your pocket. Memory was scarce. Storage was scarce. Every single character a program stored cost real, precious space and real money. And programmers, being practical people solving real problems, looked for sensible savings everywhere. One of those savings looked completely obvious. When you store a date, you have to store the year — nineteen seventy-three, say. But why store all four digits? Everyone knows it's the nineteen-hundreds. Just store the last two — seven-three. You have halved the space the year takes up, across millions and millions of records, and you have lost nothing that matters. It was not a mistake. In nineteen seventy, it was good engineering. The savings were real, and the assumption — that the first two digits would always be "one" and "nine" — was, for the moment, perfectly true.

NARRATOR: And here is where the story turns, and where it becomes a lesson for you specifically. That shortcut did not stay in nineteen seventy. The code that contained it kept running. And running. Through the seventies, the eighties, the nineties. The original programmers retired, or moved on, or passed away. The systems they built became the invisible plumbing of the modern world — the payroll systems, the banking systems, the insurance and government systems — quietly doing their jobs, decade after decade, with that two-digit assumption baked deep inside them, mostly forgotten by everyone. The shortcut had become what we call technical debt: a saving taken early, with the bill quietly accumulating, waiting to come due. QUESTION: So pause and work out the problem for yourself. If a program stores the year as just two digits, and it calculates someone's age by subtracting their birth year from the current year — what happens at the stroke of midnight, when nineteen ninety-nine becomes the year two thousand?

NARRATOR: The current year, stored as two digits, ticks over from nine-nine to zero-zero. And the program, which has no idea that zero-zero means two thousand, reads it as nineteen hundred. So now imagine it calculates the age of someone born in nineteen eighty-five. In nineteen ninety-nine, it did the sum ninety-nine minus eighty-five, and got fourteen. Correct. But in the year two thousand, it does zero-zero minus eighty-five — zero minus eighty-five — and gets minus eighty-five. A negative age. Minus eighty-five years old. Listing two shows exactly this in runnable code — the two-digit version producing a negative age the moment the year crosses two thousand. And that is the gentle version. Now multiply that one broken subtraction across every system that calculates interest on a loan, the expiry date on a pension, how long until a bridge needs inspecting, when a prisoner is released, how much medication a patient is due. Date arithmetic is everywhere, hidden inside everything, and all of it was about to start producing nonsense at the same instant.

NARRATOR: When this fully dawned on the world, in the mid nineteen-nineties, it set off one of the largest coordinated engineering efforts there has ever been. Because the problem was not in one program. It was in millions of them, scattered across every industry, much of it written in older languages like COBOL that fewer and fewer people still understood, and a lot of it with no surviving documentation at all. Companies and governments spent enormous sums — credible estimates run into the hundreds of billions of dollars worldwide — to hunt down the problem, line by line, system by system. Retired COBOL programmers were lured back to work for spectacular fees, because they were the only ones who could still read the ancient code. For several years, a substantial slice of the entire software profession was doing one thing: finding every place a year was stored, and fixing it before the deadline that — unlike every other deadline in software history — absolutely could not be moved. The date was coming whether they were ready or not.

NARRATOR: And there were really two ways to fix it, both of which are worth understanding because they teach you something about data representation. The proper fix is called expansion: you go in and change the storage so the year holds all four digits — two thousand, in full — and then the arithmetic simply works, forever, across any century boundary. Listing two shows that version too, calculating ages correctly straight through the year two thousand. Expansion is the real solution. But it is also expensive and invasive — you have to change the data, the files, the databases, every program that touches them. So many teams, short on time, used the cheaper stopgap, called windowing. With windowing you keep the two digits, but you teach the program to interpret them with a cut-off: a small year, below some pivot, means the two-thousands; a large year means the nineteen-hundreds. So zero-five means two thousand and five, but eighty-five means nineteen eighty-five. It works, it is quick, and — notice the catch — it does not actually solve the problem. It just moves it to a future pivot year, leaving a smaller version of the same trap for some later programmer to find. Listing three lays out both approaches and the lessons.

NARRATOR: So midnight came. The thirty-first of December, nineteen ninety-nine, rolled into the first of January, two thousand. And... almost nothing happened. A few minor glitches — a website showing the date as the nineteenth of one-hundred, a handful of small systems hiccupping — but no planes fell, no grids failed, no banks collapsed. The catastrophe everyone had braced for simply did not arrive.

QUESTION: And now here is the strange, almost philosophical twist that I really want you to sit with. When nothing went wrong — what do you think a lot of people concluded?

NARRATOR: A lot of people concluded that it had all been a hoax. A scam. An overblown panic that wasted hundreds of billions of dollars on a threat that was never real. After all — nothing happened, did it? And this is the deep, uncomfortable lesson at the centre of the Y2K story, and it is one every engineer needs to understand. Nothing happened because of the work, not instead of it. The reason the lights stayed on and the planes kept flying is that an army of programmers spent years quietly finding and fixing the problem before it could bite. The non-event was the success. It is the clearest example I know of a hard truth about our profession: when prevention works perfectly, it is invisible. Nobody throws a party for the disaster that didn't happen. The better you do safety-critical engineering, the more it looks, from the outside, like there was never anything to worry about. And you have to be able to live with that, and keep doing the careful, unglamorous work anyway — because the alternative, the world where they shrugged and didn't bother, is a genuinely frightening place.

NARRATOR: So let me draw out the lessons, because every one of them connects to what you are studying right now. The first, and biggest: choosing how to represent data is a real engineering decision with real consequences. Two digits or four digits is not a triviality — it is a choice about storage, about range, about the future, and it can outlive you by decades. This is exactly why, in this module, we are so careful about data types: why you store a date in a proper date type rather than a loose number, why you never store money in a floating-point number, why you think about the range a value needs before you choose how to hold it. Y2K is what happens when a representation choice that was fine for its moment meets a future its designers never imagined.

NARRATOR: The second lesson: a shortcut is a debt, and the bill compounds. Saving two digits per record was nothing, once. Multiplied by billions of records over forty years, it became a problem that cost the world a fortune to repair. When you take a shortcut in how you store or handle data, you are not avoiding the cost — you are lending it to your future self, or to whoever maintains your code after you, with interest. The third lesson: boundaries are where things break. The failure happened precisely at the rollover, the ninety-nine to zero-zero edge — which is exactly why, when you design test data, you test the boundaries and the rollovers, not just the comfortable middle. The whole disaster lived at one edge case that should have been tested decades earlier.

NARRATOR: The Y2K bug has no victims to mourn, and that is precisely what makes it special among the cautionary tales — it is the one where the engineers saw the iceberg coming and turned the ship in time. It cost a fortune, it was deeply unglamorous, and its reward was that the world barely noticed. But hidden inside that anticlimax is everything this module is trying to teach you: that how you represent a humble piece of data — a year, a price, a name — is a decision that matters, that shortcuts come due, that the edges are where things fail, and that some of the most important engineering you will ever do is the kind nobody ever sees, because you did it well enough that nothing happened. Choose your data types as if someone will still be running your code in the year two thousand. Because once, somebody's code was — and they hadn't.
