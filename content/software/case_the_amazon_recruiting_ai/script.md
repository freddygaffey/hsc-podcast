NARRATOR: Somewhere inside Amazon, around twenty fifteen, an experimental piece of software was quietly reading job applicants' resumes and giving each one a score, one to five stars, like a product. It had been built to do something every large company dreams of: take the slow, expensive, human work of sifting through a mountain of applications, and automate it. Let the machine find the best people. And then someone noticed something deeply wrong. The system was marking down resumes that contained the word "women's" — as in "women's chess club captain," or "captain of the women's debating team." It had decided, all on its own, that being a woman was a negative. Nobody had told it to do that. Nobody had written a rule that said "penalise women." It had taught itself, from the data it was given. And that — a machine teaching itself to discriminate, from data that looked perfectly innocent — is one of the most important cautionary tales in all of artificial intelligence.

NARRATOR: This is the story of Amazon's recruiting AI, and it is the case study that makes the bias topic in this module real. Everything you study about machine learning — that a supervised model learns from labelled examples, that a model is only as good as its data, that bias creeps in through specific, identifiable doors — all of it is sitting inside this one story. And the most unsettling part is that nobody involved was a villain. There was no prejudiced programmer typing discrimination into the code. The bias came in through the data, quietly, and the machine amplified it. Let me show you exactly how, because the mechanism is the whole lesson. Listing one lays out the timeline.

NARRATOR: Amazon's tool was a machine-learning model, and specifically it used supervised learning — the first of the four training styles in your S-U-S-R set: Supervised, Unsupervised, Semi-supervised, Reinforcement. Supervised learning works by showing the model a huge pile of labelled examples and letting it find the patterns that connect the input to the label. So how do you build a model that picks good job candidates? You show it lots of resumes, each labelled with the answer — was this person hired, or not — and you let it learn what a "hire" looks like. It is a completely reasonable idea. The model's job is to look at the people Amazon hired in the past, find the pattern, and find more people like them.

QUESTION: And there is the trap, hiding in plain sight in that last sentence. The model learns to find more people like the ones hired in the past. So pause and ask: what happens if the past hiring was itself biased?

NARRATOR: Then the model learns the bias, faithfully and precisely, and treats it as the very definition of a good candidate. Amazon trained this system on about ten years of resumes submitted to the company, and on who got hired. But the tech industry over that decade was overwhelmingly male, and the people hired were overwhelmingly male. So the model, doing exactly what it was built to do, looked at that history and drew the logical conclusion from the data: successful candidates tend to be men. It wasn't malfunctioning. It was working perfectly — on biased data. It learned that resumes that looked like the men who'd been hired should score highly, and resumes that looked different should score lower. And one of the signals it picked up on was the word "women's." This is the single most important idea in machine-learning ethics, and it has a blunt name: bias in, bias out. A supervised model is a mirror held up to its training data. If the data reflects a biased world, the model reflects it straight back — and then applies it to every future decision, at scale, with the cold authority of a computer.

NARRATOR: Listing two shows the mechanism in miniature, runnably. It has a tiny set of past hiring decisions where two groups of applicants are equally skilled, but the past favoured one group over the other — bias baked into the labels. Train a simple model on that history and it learns to prefer the favoured group outright, scoring the other group at zero, never recommending them — not because of skill, which it ignores, but purely because of who was hired before. The model didn't create the bias. It inherited it from the labels and made it a rule.

NARRATOR: Now, your instinct for a fix might be: just don't tell the model anyone's gender. Remove that feature. And Amazon did try to neutralise the specific terms it had latched onto. But here is the second, deeper lesson, and Listing two shows this too: removing the obvious feature is not enough, because the bias leaks through proxies. A proxy is some other piece of information that happens to correlate with the thing you removed. The word "women's" is a proxy for gender. The name of an all-women's college is a proxy. Even patterns in word choice can be proxies. So you delete "gender," and the model quietly reconstructs it from the proxies that remain, and carries on discriminating. In the listing, take away the explicit group label and a correlated word on the resume still leaks the same bias straight back. You cannot fix bias just by blindfolding the model to the obvious feature, because the world is full of correlated clues.

NARRATOR: So let me draw out the lessons, and they map onto the bias topic in this module precisely. Listing three lays them out. The first you already have: supervised learning is only as good as its labels. Bias in, bias out. When you train a model to imitate past human decisions, you are betting that those past decisions were fair — and if they weren't, you have built a machine that automates the unfairness and scales it up.

NARRATOR: The second lesson is to know where bias comes from, because "be less biased" is useless advice — you need to find the specific doors it enters through. Your course gives you those doors as S-H-L-M: Sampling bias, Historical bias, Labeler bias, and Measurement bias. Amazon's disaster was mostly historical bias — the past it learned from was genuinely skewed — riding on top of a sampling problem, because the applicant pool it drew from was itself overwhelmingly male. Naming the source is the start of fixing it: this was history and sampling, not a mislabelling or a measurement error, and that tells you where to intervene.

NARRATOR: The third lesson is the proxy problem we just saw: you must test the model's actual outcomes for fairness, not just remove the sensitive feature and assume you're done. Does the model recommend men and women — or any protected groups — at fair rates, for equal qualifications? You only know if you measure the outputs. And the fourth lesson is mitigation, which your course gives you as D-R-F: Diverse, representative data; Reweighting or rebalancing the data so the minority isn't drowned out; and Fairness metrics applied to the results. Plus the human layer around all of it — clear accountability, documentation of what a model was trained on, and a person responsible for the decision, not just "the algorithm said so."

QUESTION: And here's a question worth sitting with, because Amazon's ending is more honourable than people remember. When they realised they could not trust this model to be fair — when they couldn't be sure they'd closed off every proxy — what was the right thing to do?

NARRATOR: They scrapped it. By around twenty seventeen, the project was abandoned. They did not deploy it as the sole basis for hiring and hope for the best; when they could not guarantee it was fair, they stopped using it. And that is genuinely the right call, and a lesson in itself. Sometimes the responsible engineering decision is not to ship — to look at a system you've poured effort into, recognise that you cannot make it trustworthy, and walk away. In a field racing to automate everything, the willingness to say "this one isn't good enough, and we won't inflict it on people" is a mark of maturity, not failure. The danger was never that the model was badly built. The danger was that it was well built — on a biased world.

NARRATOR: So what do you take from a machine that taught itself to be sexist? That a model learns the world you show it, including the world's injustices, and then applies them tirelessly and at scale. That bias enters through nameable doors — sampling, historical, labeler, measurement — and that you fight it with diverse data, reweighting, fairness metrics measured on the actual outcomes, and human accountability over the whole thing. That removing the obvious feature doesn't work, because proxies remain. And that the most powerful thing you can do, sometimes, is refuse to deploy. When you build something that makes decisions about people — who gets a job, a loan, a place — you are not just writing code. You are deciding whose future gets shaped by the patterns of the past. Hold that data up to the light before you teach a machine to believe it.
