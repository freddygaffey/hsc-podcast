NARRATOR: In the early nineteen nineties, the city of Denver built a brand-new airport — a gleaming, enormous, state-of-the-art airport, ready to open. The terminals were finished. The runways were laid. The control tower was staffed. And it sat there, complete, unable to open its doors to a single passenger, for about sixteen months. Every day it stayed closed cost something like a million dollars. And the reason this entire airport — runways, terminals, the lot — could not open, was one subsystem that didn't work: the machine that was supposed to move the luggage. This is the story of the Denver airport automated baggage system, and it is one of the most famous project failures in the history of software engineering — not because of a clever bug, but because of how a project was scoped, planned, and managed. It is, in other words, the case study that brings your entire Software Engineering Project module to life.

NARRATOR: Let me set the scene, because the ambition is genuinely impressive, and that's part of the lesson. Denver didn't want ordinary baggage handling — conveyor belts and people. They wanted the most advanced baggage system in the world: a vast automated network, around thirty kilometres of track, with thousands of little self-guiding carts — telecars — that would zip each individual bag automatically across the whole airport, from check-in to plane, with almost no human hands involved. On paper, it was the future. In reality, nobody had ever built anything like it at that scale, and it turned out to be fiendishly, brutally complex. Listing one lays out the timeline.

NARRATOR: And when they tested it, it was a catastrophe — and a vivid one. The telecars jammed and crashed into each other. They piled up. Bags were misrouted to the wrong ends of the airport, flung off the carts, chewed up by the machinery; there's famous footage of clothes strewn across the tracks where suitcases had burst open. The system simply could not be made to work reliably in the time available. And here is the part that turns a struggling subsystem into a sixteen-month, multi-hundred-million-dollar disaster: the entire airport's opening had been planned around this baggage system being finished. The airport could not open without a way to move luggage, and they had bet everything on this one unproven, automated way, with no backup. So when the baggage system slipped, the whole airport slipped with it.

QUESTION: So pause and think about that as a project manager. You've got terminals, runways, a tower — all ready — and one risky, never-been-done subsystem running late. Why did that one piece have the power to delay everything?

NARRATOR: Because it was on the critical path — and there was no fallback. The critical path of a project is the chain of tasks that determines the finish date: the project cannot finish until its last necessary piece is done, so whichever required task runs longest drags the whole timeline out to meet it. The airport needed a baggage system to open; the only baggage system they were building was the automated one; so the airport's opening date was chained directly to the most complex, riskiest, least proven part of the whole endeavour. Listing two shows this in miniature: model the airport as a set of subsystems, each with a ready date, and the opening is simply the latest of the required ones. With the automated system as the only option, the opening lands sixteen months late, dragged out by that one long pole. But add a humble manual fallback — ordinary baggage handling — as an alternative, and the airport can open on time, with the fancy automated system finished and added later, once it actually works. That single change — a fallback so the risky subsystem isn't on the critical path alone — is the difference between opening on schedule and a sixteen-month, headline-making delay. And in the end, that is exactly how Denver eventually opened: by building the conventional manual system they should have had as a backstop all along.

NARRATOR: So let me pull out the lessons, because every one of them is a core idea in the project module, and Listing three lays them out. The first is feasibility — and your course gives you a checklist for it, TECO: Technical, Economic, Cost-and-schedule, and Operational feasibility. The single most important question at the start of any project is "can this actually be done — at this scale, for this money, in this time?" And for Denver's baggage system, an honest technical-and-schedule feasibility assessment should have raised the alarm loudly: nobody had built this before, at this size, and certainly not in the time the airport's schedule allowed. The ambition was magnificent and the feasibility was not there. Dreaming big is good; betting an airport on an infeasible dream is not.

NARRATOR: The second lesson is the project-management triangle — Scope, Time, and Cost. The idea is that these three are in tension, and you cannot freely fix all of them: if you lock the scope and lock the time, then cost and quality are what give way. Denver locked both: the scope was this gigantic automated system, and the time was fixed because the entire airport was being built around it on a schedule. So when reality bit, the only things left to absorb the pressure were cost — which ballooned into the hundreds of millions — and quality, which collapsed into crashing telecars. When you find yourself with a fixed, ambitious scope and a fixed, immovable deadline, that is not a plan; it is a warning.

NARRATOR: The third lesson is the one Listing two made concrete: protect the critical path, and keep a fallback. Do not put your riskiest, most novel component on the critical path of the whole project with nothing behind it. If something has never been done before, assume it might not be ready on time, and make sure the project can succeed anyway — with a simpler backup, a manual process, a staged introduction. A fallback is not an admission of defeat; it's what lets you attempt something ambitious without betting everything on it.

NARRATOR: And the fourth lesson is about how you switch a new system on — the implementation method, which your course gives you as DiP-PP: Direct, Phased, Parallel, and Pilot. Denver effectively chose Direct — the big-bang approach, cutting straight over to the brand-new automated system, airport-wide, all at once, with no old system running alongside. Direct is the cheapest and fastest method, and by far the riskiest, because if it doesn't work, you have nothing to fall back on. For something this novel and this critical, almost any other choice would have been safer: a Pilot, proving it on a single concourse first; or Parallel, running manual handling alongside the automated system until the automated one earned trust. They chose the one method that offered no safety net, for the one project that most needed one.

QUESTION: There's a final thread worth naming, because it's a quiet killer of projects everywhere, not just airports. The requirements for this system kept changing — airlines came in late with new demands about how their bags should be handled. What does a stream of late requirement changes do to a project already straining against an impossible deadline?

NARRATOR: It makes an impossible situation worse. Every late change to the requirements means rework — redesigning and rebuilding things you thought were finished — and rework devours the one resource the project least had: time. This is why your module stresses getting requirements clear and stable early, defining the boundaries of the system up front, and managing changes deliberately rather than absorbing a constant trickle of new demands. A project that is already infeasible on time cannot also absorb a moving target. Denver had both: a deadline it could not meet, and requirements that would not hold still.

NARRATOR: So what do you carry away from an entire airport held hostage by its luggage? That the first question of any project is feasibility — TECO — and that "can we actually do this, at this scale, in this time?" is a question you must answer honestly before you build, not discover painfully afterward. That Scope, Time, and Cost cannot all be fixed at once, and pretending otherwise just pushes the failure into cost and quality. That you must protect the critical path and keep a fallback, so your boldest component can't sink the whole ship. That you choose your implementation method — Direct, Phased, Parallel, or Pilot — to match the risk, and a brand-new, mission-critical system is the last place for a no-safety-net big-bang. And that requirements must be pinned down and managed, because a moving target plus an immovable deadline is a project already lost. No one was hurt in Denver — it cost money, and time, and a great deal of embarrassment. But the airport that couldn't open is a permanent monument to a simple truth: most software projects don't fail because the code was bad. They fail because the project was impossible from the start, and nobody was willing to say so.
