NARRATOR: Objectives. By the end you'll be able to: name the types of data a mechatronic system handles; distinguish diagnostic data from optimisation data — the examinable pair; explain how data is obtained and processed, from raw signal to usable number; and state safe practices for storing it.

NARRATOR: Start with the types of data, because the syllabus wants you to use "different types." Here's a hook — M-C-D-O, the four streams. Measurement data: the raw sensor readings of the world — temperature, distance, position. Control data: the commands going out — motor speed, a valve opening, a setpoint. Diagnostic data: the system's own health — fault logs, error codes, overheat events, how long it's run. And Optimisation data: the performance history you use to tune — response times, energy use, cycle counts. Measurement, Control, Diagnostic, Optimisation. The dot-point calls out the last two specifically, so they're where the marks live.

QUESTION: So what's the actual difference between diagnostic data and optimisation data — aren't they both just logs of numbers?

NARRATOR: They can even be the same numbers — what separates them is the purpose. Here's the clean way to hold it. Diagnostic data answers "is it healthy, and what went wrong?" — it's for detecting and fixing faults, for maintenance. A log line saying the motor stalled at two-oh-three, an error code, a count of out-of-range readings — that's diagnostic. Optimisation data answers "can it do the job better?" — it's for tuning performance. A record that the average cycle time fell from two-point-one seconds to one-point-eight after you raised the fan's response — that's optimisation. Diagnostic: is it healthy? Optimisation: can it do better? The same temperature log is diagnostic when you're hunting a fault and optimisation when you're tuning the setpoint — the purpose decides. Listing 1 lays that distinction out with examples; in the exam, define both by their purpose and give a concrete example of each.

NARRATOR: Now, how is the data obtained? Two ways, and there's a neat way to remember them: polling asks, interrupts tell. With polling, the controller goes around and asks each sensor for its value on a schedule — simple, predictable. With an interrupt, the sensor signals the controller the moment something happens — efficient, and right for urgent events you can't afford to miss between polls. And either way there's a sampling rate — how often you read. Sample too slowly and you miss things; sample too fast and you waste processor time and storage. Remember from last episode: if the sensor's analog, its signal goes through that analog-to-digital converter on the way in, turning a smooth voltage into a number.

NARRATOR: Then the data has to be processed — raw readings are rarely clean enough to act on directly. Three steps. Calibrate: correct a known offset so the reading is true — if your sensor always reads half a degree low, you add it back. Filter: smooth out noise, because real sensors jitter, and one wild reading shouldn't swing your whole control system — a moving average is the classic tool, averaging each reading with its recent neighbours. And validate: range-check every reading and reject the impossible — which is exactly the data-dictionary constraint idea from Programming Fundamentals, now guarding a live sensor instead of a form field. Remember "never store money in a float" — the same instinct, that raw values aren't to be trusted, applies here.

NARRATOR: Let me make it concrete — Listing 2 is a runnable pipeline, and it's the whole lesson in one example. We've got a stream of noisy temperature readings, and buried in them is a glitch — a wild ninety-nine degrees that the sensor never really saw. First the diagnostic step catches it: a range check flags that ninety-nine as out of range before it can corrupt anything — that's diagnostic data doing its job. Then we process: drop the glitch, calibrate by the known offset, and smooth the rest with a moving average so the controller sees a steady value, not jitter. And finally the optimisation step uses that clean, smoothed temperature to decide how hard to run the fan — the further above the setpoint, the harder it runs. One pipeline: catch the fault, clean the signal, tune the action. That's measurement, diagnostic, and optimisation data all in play in a few lines.

QUESTION: Pause the player and try this. A distance sensor on a robot occasionally returns a reading of zero when its signal briefly drops out, even though there's a wall half a metre away. Name two processing steps that would stop that bad reading from making the robot crash, and say what each does. Work it out, then play.

NARRATOR: Two strong answers. First, validation — a range check: if zero is below the sensor's believable minimum, or you know nothing's ever truly at zero distance, flag it as faulty and discard it rather than acting on it. Second, filtering — a moving average: smoothing the reading with its recent neighbours means a single dropout to zero gets swamped by the good readings around it, so the controller never sees a sudden phantom zero. Validation rejects the impossible value; filtering stops one bad value from dominating. If you named those two and said what each does, that's the marks. A bonus point: log that dropout as diagnostic data, so someone can later find out the sensor's flaky.

NARRATOR: Last piece — storing the data safely, which is what outcome S-E-eleven-oh-four is really about. Every log entry should wear three things — here's the hook, T-I-U: a Timestamp, an Identity saying which sensor it came from, and Units, all in a consistent format. A number with no time, no source, and no unit is almost useless later — and "no unit" is exactly the kind of slip that crashes real systems. Beyond the entry itself: keep data local for fast real-time control but push it to the cloud for analysis; control who can read operational data versus system configuration; back up your calibration and configuration data, because losing it can mean re-tuning the whole machine; and set a retention policy for how long to keep history. Safe, secure, deliberate — collect, use, and store.

QUESTION: Question one. Distinguish between diagnostic data and optimisation data in a mechatronic system, and give an example of each. Three marks.

NARRATOR: Diagnostic data is information about the system's health and faults, used to detect and fix problems — for example an error log recording that a motor overheated and stopped. Optimisation data is performance information used to tune and improve the system — for example a record of cycle times showing a process got faster after a setting was changed. The distinction for the mark: diagnostic data is about identifying faults and maintaining the system; optimisation data is about improving performance. For three marks, define both by purpose and give a correct, distinct example of each — not two examples of the same kind of log.

QUESTION: Question two. Explain how a raw analog signal from a sensor is turned into data a microcontroller can use. Pause the player, write your answer, then play.

NARRATOR: A sensor's analog output is a continuously varying voltage, which a microcontroller cannot read directly, so it first passes through an analog-to-digital converter that samples the voltage and converts it into a digital number. That raw number is then processed: calibrated to correct any known offset, filtered to smooth out noise, and validated against a sensible range to reject faulty readings — leaving a clean value the control program can act on. The marks come from naming the analog-to-digital conversion and at least one processing step, ideally describing the path from voltage to validated number.

QUESTION: Question three. A control system acts on every raw sensor reading immediately, with no filtering. Explain one risk of this and how filtering addresses it.

NARRATOR: The risk is that a single noisy or glitched reading — a momentary spike or dropout that doesn't reflect reality — causes the system to react wrongly, for example slamming an actuator or, on a robot, swerving for a phantom obstacle. Filtering, such as a moving average that combines each reading with its recent neighbours, addresses this by smoothing out individual anomalies, so one bad value is outweighed by the surrounding good ones and the system responds to the real trend rather than the noise. The mark is identifying the bad-reading risk and explaining how a specific filtering technique reduces its effect.

QUESTION: Question four. Describe two safe and secure practices for storing device data, and justify each.

NARRATOR: First, record every reading with a timestamp, a sensor identifier, and its units in a consistent format — justified because data without time, source, or units cannot be reliably interpreted or analysed later, and unit confusion causes real failures. Second, apply access control and regular backups — restricting who can read or change operational versus configuration data protects against tampering and mistakes, and backing up calibration and configuration data means a failure doesn't force re-tuning the entire system. The mark-earning move is naming a specific practice and tying it to the concrete risk it prevents, not just saying "keep it safe."

NARRATOR: That's the data layer of a mechatronic system. Four streams — Measurement, Control, Diagnostic, Optimisation; obtained by polling or interrupts, processed by calibrating, filtering, and validating, and stored safely with timestamps, identities, and units. Diagnostic asks if it's healthy; optimisation asks if it can do better.
