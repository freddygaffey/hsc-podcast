NARRATOR: Start with the root idea, because if you get this one sentence, everything else in the lesson derives from it. The single deepest difference between the two models is this: Waterfall does the whole project in one straight pass; Agile does it in repeated loops. That's the hook I've been planting for three episodes and now you cash it in: Waterfall falls once; Agile loops. Hold that image, water falling down a series of ledges, once, top to bottom, never back up, versus a spiral that goes round and round, getting bigger each time. Almost every difference you need to compare is just a consequence of "one pass" versus "many loops". Let me build both out, then compare them properly.

NARRATOR: Waterfall first. The Waterfall model runs the software lifecycle as a strict linear sequence of phases: requirements, then design, then implementation, then testing, then deployment, then maintenance. Each phase must be fully completed before the next begins, and, crucially, you don't go back up. You gather all the requirements at the start, completely, and document them. Then you design the whole system on paper. Then you build all of it. Then you test all of it. Then you ship. Think of it as the linear reading of those same eight lifecycle steps from episode one, marched through exactly once. The defining features: sequential phases, heavy documentation at every stage, the client is involved mostly at the very beginning and the very end, and going back to change an earlier phase is difficult and expensive. I've written the Waterfall flow as exam pseudocode in Listing one, and notice its shape: it's a pure sequence, one statement after another, no loop. That's "falls once" made literal.

NARRATOR: Now Agile. Agile runs the project as a series of short iterations called sprints, each typically one to four weeks long. And here's the key: each sprint is a tiny complete lifecycle of its own. In a single sprint you plan a small set of features, design them, code them, test them, and review working software with stakeholders, then you start the next sprint, re-prioritising based on the feedback you just got. So instead of one giant pass, you deliver a small slice of working software every couple of weeks and adapt as you go. The defining features: short iterations delivering working software frequently, continuous collaboration with users, requirements and priorities allowed to change between sprints, and an emphasis on working code over heavy documentation. Listing two is the Agile flow in exam pseudocode, and its shape tells the whole story: it's a "repeat until" loop wrapped around a sprint. That's "Agile loops" made literal, a sequence for Waterfall, a loop for Agile.

QUESTION: You keep saying each sprint is a "complete lifecycle". Doesn't that contradict episode one, where the lifecycle was the whole project?

NARRATOR: Great catch, and the answer ties the two lessons together. In episode one we learned the eight development steps, and I told you they run iteratively, with feedback looping you back. Agile is that iteration taken to its logical conclusion: instead of running the eight steps once for the whole project, you run a miniature version of them every sprint, for a small slice of the product. Waterfall is the other extreme, running them once for everything. So they're not different lifecycles; they're two different schedules for executing the same lifecycle, one big loop versus many small ones. That's exactly the "iterative, not linear" idea from episode one, now given two names.

NARRATOR: Now let's do the thing the dot-point actually asks: compare them. And to compare well, you need dimensions, the axes you hold both models up against, side by side. Here's the trap students fall into first, so let me name it now: a "compare" answer is not two separate descriptions sitting next to each other. If you write a paragraph on Waterfall and then a paragraph on Agile and never put them head to head on the same axis, you have not compared, you've described twice, and you'll lose marks. To compare, you pick a dimension and contrast both models on it. Let me give you four dimensions to compare on, and a hook to remember them: R-F-D-C. Requirements, Feedback, Documentation, Change.

NARRATOR: Dimension one, Requirements. Waterfall needs them stable and known up front, you gather everything at the start. Agile assumes they'll evolve, and welcomes that. Dimension two, Feedback. In Waterfall the client sees working software late, at the end, so feedback comes late. In Agile the client sees working software every sprint, so feedback is continuous and early. Dimension three, Documentation. Waterfall produces extensive documentation at every phase. Agile keeps documentation lightweight and prioritises working code. Dimension four, Change. In Waterfall, change is costly and resisted, going backwards breaks the sequence. In Agile, change is expected and absorbed in the next sprint's planning. R-F-D-C: Requirements, Feedback, Documentation, Change. And notice, every one of those four is just a consequence of the root difference. Stable requirements and late feedback and heavy docs and resisting change all follow from doing it in one pass; evolving requirements and early feedback and light docs and welcoming change all follow from doing it in loops. You don't really memorise four facts; you memorise one idea and derive four.

NARRATOR: Each model has a profile of strengths, and the strengths are mirror images. Waterfall's advantages: a clear, predictable structure, easy timeline estimation, comprehensive documentation, and it works beautifully when requirements are genuinely fixed. Its disadvantages: it's inflexible, feedback comes dangerously late, and if the requirements were misunderstood you don't find out until the end, by which point fixing it is enormously expensive. Agile's advantages are exactly Waterfall's disadvantages flipped: it's flexible, delivers value early, surfaces problems quickly, and keeps users continuously involved. And Agile's disadvantages are Waterfall's advantages flipped: it's less predictable in timeline and final scope, it can suffer documentation gaps, and it's vulnerable to scope creep, the endless adding of features so the core never quite ships. See the symmetry? You're not learning two unrelated lists. You're learning one axis with two ends.

NARRATOR: Now the single most important point in this whole episode, the one that separates a band-four answer from a band-six. Neither model is universally better. This is the trap, and it's the marquee one: students decide Agile is "modern" and therefore "best", or that Waterfall is "old" and therefore "wrong", and they write an answer crowning a winner. Markers penalise that, hard. The mark-earning position is that the right model depends on the project, specifically on how stable the requirements are. If the requirements are well-defined and won't change, Waterfall's structure and documentation are a genuine asset. If the requirements will evolve and you need fast feedback, Agile's adaptability wins. The methodology is a tool you fit to the situation, not a rule. Whenever you see "compare Waterfall and Agile", your conclusion sentence is always some version of "it depends on requirement stability", never "Agile is better".

NARRATOR: Let me make that concrete with the worked examples, because the right example proves you understand the principle. Consider banking software, the software running an automated teller machine, a cash machine. The requirements are strict, defined by regulation, and security-critical; they're not going to casually change, and changes are expensive and dangerous. That's a Waterfall project: stable, regulated, documentation-heavy, plan it thoroughly up front. Now consider a mobile game or a social media app for teenagers. The market is fiercely competitive, user tastes shift constantly, and you genuinely don't know which features will land until real users try them. That's an Agile project: evolving requirements, you need to ship, watch, and adapt fast. Same skill, software development, completely different management model, because the requirement stability is completely different. NASA building a spacecraft is Waterfall, for the same reason as the bank. A streaming service constantly tweaking features is Agile, for the same reason as the game.

QUESTION: What about our running example, the Grade Calculator? Which one is it?

NARRATOR: Honestly, for something that simple, Waterfall's formal structure is overkill, you could plan and build it in an afternoon. But it's a useful thought experiment. Under Waterfall, you'd write a full requirements document, then a design document, then implement everything, then run one big testing phase, then hand it over. Under Agile, sprint one might be just input validation and the average; sprint two adds error handling; sprint three adds weighted grades after a teacher says they actually need that. And that last bit reveals the real decider: if you suspect the requirements will grow, say the school later wants weighted grades or different grade scales, Agile's ability to absorb that change is exactly what you want. If the marking policy is fixed and won't move, Waterfall's one clean pass is fine. The Grade Calculator can be run either way; which is wiser depends entirely on whether you expect the requirements to change. There's your principle again.

NARRATOR: One more layer, because real projects do it and it's worth a mark: many teams use a hybrid. They use Waterfall-style planning up front to set the overall scope and milestones, then run Agile sprints inside those milestones for the actual building, with regular feedback. It's not a betrayal of either model; it's fitting the tool to the job, using Waterfall's structure where requirements are firm and Agile's adaptability where they're not. Mentioning the hybrid in an extended answer signals you understand both deeply enough to combine them.

QUESTION: Question two. "Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of the Agile model." Four marks. Pause, answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer: an advantage of Agile is early and frequent delivery of working software, because the project is built in short sprints, users see and give feedback on functioning software every one to four weeks, which surfaces problems early and reduces the risk of building the wrong thing. QUESTION: Question three. "Compare the execution of the Waterfall and Agile models as applied to software development." Six marks. This is the dot-point itself. Pause, plan a full compare answer, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer: Waterfall executes the lifecycle as one sequential pass, all requirements gathered and documented up front, each phase completed before the next, with client feedback only at the end. Comparing their execution directly: on requirements, Waterfall needs them stable while Agile expects change; on feedback, Waterfall is late while Agile is continuous; on documentation, Waterfall is heavy while Agile is lightweight; and on change, Waterfall resists it at high cost while Agile welcomes it each sprint. QUESTION: Question four. The applied one. "A hospital needs new software to manage patient records, with strict legal requirements for data handling that are fixed by regulation. Recommend a project management model and justify your choice, referring to the project's characteristics." Five marks. Pause, plan it, then play.

NARRATOR: Model answer: I would recommend the Waterfall model. The project's requirements are fixed by regulation and therefore stable and well-defined up front, which is exactly the condition Waterfall is suited to, its sequential, plan-everything-first execution is an asset when requirements won't change. The strict legal requirements for data handling demand comprehensive documentation for compliance and auditing, which Waterfall produces at every phase. Because the requirements are unlikely to evolve, Agile's main advantage, absorbing changing requirements, offers little benefit here, while its lighter documentation would be a liability against the compliance need. Therefore Waterfall's structure, predictability and documentation fit this project's characteristics best. A top answer explicitly ties the recommendation to requirement stability and the documentation requirement, and notes that a different recommendation would follow if the requirements were expected to evolve, showing you're applying the principle, not just naming a model.
