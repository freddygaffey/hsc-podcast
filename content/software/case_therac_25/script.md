NARRATOR: On a spring morning in nineteen eighty-six, a man named Ray Cox walked into a clinic in Tyler, Texas, for what should have been a completely routine appointment. He had a tumour. He had already been through eight treatments on the same machine, in the same room, with no trouble at all. This was his ninth. He lay down on the table, the technician left the room and pulled the heavy door closed behind her, and a few feet away a large machine the size of a small car got ready to do something genuinely remarkable. It was going to fire a beam of radiation into his back, precisely shaped, precisely aimed, calibrated to destroy the tumour and spare the healthy tissue around it. This is one of the quiet miracles of modern medicine. We can deliver a controlled dose of one of the most dangerous forms of energy in the universe, into a human body, to save a life.

NARRATOR: And then, in a fraction of a second, something went wrong inside the machine that nobody in that room could see, and nobody in that room could have stopped. There was no explosion. No spark. No alarm that meant anything. From the outside it looked, for an instant, like nothing had happened at all. Ray Cox felt a sensation he later described as an enormous electric shock, a burning, searing heat in his back, as if someone had poured boiling water on him or pressed a hot iron against his spine. He cried out. He tried to get up. And on the screen in the next room, the operator saw a short, cryptic message — a malfunction code, the kind she had seen many times before — and a status that, as far as she could tell, meant the treatment had not actually been delivered. So, following the machine's own apparent instruction, she did the most natural thing in the world. She told it to go again.

NARRATOR: This is the story of the Therac twenty-five. It is, I think, the single most important case study in the whole of software engineering, and it is taught in serious computer science courses around the world to this day, because it is the moment our field grew up. It is the moment the cost of a software bug stopped being a crashed program or a corrupted file, and became a human life. Between nineteen eighty-five and nineteen eighty-seven, this machine massively overdosed at least six patients with radiation. Several of them died. The others were grievously, permanently injured. And the reasons it happened are not exotic, or unlucky, or unforeseeable. They are mistakes that you, as a programmer, could make on any ordinary Tuesday. That is exactly why it matters. The people who died here were not killed by something rare. They were killed by ordinary engineering decisions that, stacked on top of one another, became lethal.

NARRATOR: I am going to tell you what happened, carefully, and accurately, with respect for the people involved, because real people suffered and died, and they deserve to have their story told honestly rather than sensationally. And then, at the end, once you understand the machine, I am going to draw out the engineering lessons, because every single one of them connects directly to ideas in your course — to encapsulation and protecting the state of an object, to quality assurance and independent testing, to the strange and dangerous world of concurrency bugs. The Therac twenty-five is not just a horror story. It is, in the most literal sense, why we are taught to write software the way we are taught to write it. So let us start by understanding the machine, and the choice at the very heart of it that doomed everything else.

NARRATOR: The Therac twenty-five was a radiation therapy machine, built by a Canadian organisation called A-E-C-L — Atomic Energy of Canada Limited — a respected, government-owned body with deep expertise in all things nuclear. It was what is called a medical linear accelerator. And to understand the accident, you have to understand a clever, dangerous duality at the core of how it worked. This one machine could actually deliver two completely different kinds of treatment, depending on what the patient needed.

NARRATOR: In the first mode, it fired a beam of electrons directly into the patient. Electrons do not travel far into tissue, so this mode was for shallow targets — tumours at or near the surface of the skin. The beam used here was relatively gentle, a low current of electrons. In the second mode, it produced X-rays, which penetrate much deeper, for tumours buried inside the body. But here is the crucial, almost unbelievable physical fact at the centre of this entire story. To make those X-rays, the machine did not use a gentle beam. It took the electron beam and cranked its power up enormously — by a factor of roughly a hundred — into a ferociously powerful, high-energy electron beam. And then it smashed that beam into a metal target placed in its path. The collision of the powerful electrons with the metal is what produces the X-rays. The metal target is what stands between that brutal beam and the patient. It absorbs the raw electron beam, spreads it out, and converts it into the gentle, therapeutic X-rays the patient is actually meant to receive.

NARRATOR: Sit with that for a moment, because it is the whole tragedy in one sentence. The very same machine, in one of its modes, generated a beam roughly a hundred times more powerful than anything that should ever touch a human being. And the only thing protecting the patient from that beam was a piece of metal — the target — being correctly in place at the moment the beam fired. If that powerful X-ray-mode beam were ever to fire while the target was not in position, the full, undiluted, hundred-times-too-strong electron beam would go straight into the patient. That is precisely what must never, ever be allowed to happen. The entire safety of the machine reduces to a single rule. The high-power beam and the missing target must never coincide.

QUESTION: So how do you guarantee that? If a single wrong combination of settings is lethal, how do you make absolutely certain it can never occur?

NARRATOR: And the answer to that question is where the story turns, because the company already knew the answer. They had known it for years. The Therac twenty-five had two older siblings, the Therac six and the Therac twenty. And those older machines solved this exact problem in the most robust way there is. They solved it with hardware. On the older machines, there were physical interlocks — real, mechanical, electrical safety devices wired into the machine itself. If the settings were wrong, if the powerful beam was selected but the metal target was not properly in place, then physical fuses would blow, physical switches would refuse to close, and the machine simply could not energise the beam. It was not a matter of asking the machine nicely, or trusting it to behave. The unsafe state was made physically impossible by the wiring. The electrons literally had no path to the patient unless the protective hardware confirmed everything was correct first. A mechanical interlock does not have bugs. It does not get confused. It cannot be talked out of doing its job by a clever sequence of keystrokes. It is a wall.

NARRATOR: Now here is the decision that sits at the dead centre of this disaster. When A-E-C-L designed the new machine, the Therac twenty-five, they made it more modern, more capable, more computer-controlled than its predecessors. And as part of that modernisation, they took those hardware interlocks — the physical safety devices that had been quietly, reliably preventing catastrophe on the older machines — and they removed them. They took out the hardware that made the unsafe state physically impossible. And in its place, they put software. From now on, the job of making sure the powerful beam never fired without the target in place would be done not by a fuse or a switch or a wall of physics, but by a computer program, checking the settings, and deciding whether it was safe to fire.

NARRATOR: I want to be fair here, because this was not an insane decision in nineteen eighty-two. Software was cheaper. Software was flexible. Software could do more, more elegantly, with fewer moving parts. And the company genuinely believed their software was reliable, partly because they were reusing code from the older Therac machines — code that had run for years, on the six and the twenty, apparently without incident. So the reasoning, unspoken but real, went something like this. The software is reused from machines that never hurt anyone. Therefore the software is safe. Therefore we do not need the hardware backstop any more. We can let the program alone stand guard over the most dangerous thing this machine can do.

NARRATOR: That chain of reasoning had a hole in it big enough to kill people. And it did. Because the old hardware interlocks had not just been redundant decoration. On the older machines, the hardware had been silently catching the software's mistakes the entire time. The software on the Therac six and twenty almost certainly had the very same bugs lurking inside it. But on those machines, when the software made its mistake, the hardware was standing right behind it, and the fuse blew, and nobody was hurt, and nobody ever found out the software was broken, because the hardware kept cleaning up after it. The bugs were invisible precisely because the safety net was working. Remove the net, trust the buggy software alone, and now the bug — which had been there all along — finally reaches the patient. The "proven, reliable" reused code had never been reliable. It had only ever been protected.

NARRATOR: So now we have a machine where a single piece of software, alone, is responsible for making sure a hundred-times-too-powerful beam never fires into a person. And that software has a bug in it. Let us go and find the bug.

NARRATOR: To use the machine, an operator sat at a terminal — a keyboard and a text screen — in a separate room, shielded from the radiation. They would type in the prescription for the patient. Which mode, electron or X-ray. How much energy. How big a dose. The machine would then physically arrange itself to match — and this is important, because arranging itself was a slow, mechanical process. In X-ray mode, motors had to physically swing that metal target into the beam's path, rotate a heavy turntable into the right position, set everything up. That took several seconds of real, physical movement. Meanwhile, inside the computer, hidden from everyone, the program was keeping track of what mode it was in and what it was about to do, in its own internal memory. The machine had, in effect, a hidden internal state — a set of variables describing what it currently believed it was set up to do. And the operator could not see that state. They could only see the text on the screen.

NARRATOR: Now, the operators were not amateurs. They ran this machine dozens of times a day, treatment after treatment, and they got fast. Genuinely, impressively fast at typing in prescriptions. And every so often, an operator would make a small typing mistake — pick the wrong mode, say X-ray when they meant electron — notice it instantly, and correct it, quickly, by moving the cursor back up the screen, changing the entry, and carrying on. This is completely normal human behaviour. Anybody who types fast makes a quick mistake and fixes it without breaking stride. The operators did it all the time. It was nothing.

NARRATOR: But the machine had a bug that lived exactly in that gap — the gap between the operator typing fast, and the slow mechanical hardware physically catching up. Here is what could happen, and it is worth following carefully, because it is one of the most important kinds of bug there is. The operator selects X-ray mode by mistake. The instant they do, the program starts setting up for the powerful X-ray beam, and it starts physically moving that heavy metal target into place — a process that takes several seconds. But the operator immediately notices the mistake and, within those same few seconds, quickly edits the prescription back to electron mode. Now, if they did this fast enough — and the magic number, found later, was roughly eight seconds — a flaw in the way the program tracked its own state meant the two halves of the change came apart. The part of the program that controlled the beam's power got updated. But the part that tracked where the metal target was did not get properly re-checked. The internal state ended up scrambled, inconsistent with itself. And the machine came to believe something that was physically false and physically lethal. It believed it was safe to fire — while it was set up, in the worst possible combination, to deliver the powerful beam with the protective target not in place.

QUESTION: So this only happened if the operator was fast? If they were slow and careful, it never triggered?

NARRATOR: Exactly, and that is the cruel, almost backwards heart of this particular bug. The faster and more competent the operator, the more likely they were to kill someone. A nervous, slow, hunt-and-peck operator who took fifteen seconds to fix a typo would never have hit the bug at all, because the hardware would have finished moving before they finished editing, and the state would have stayed consistent. It was the expert operators — the fast, fluent, experienced ones, the ones a hospital most wants running its equipment — who triggered the disaster. The bug rewarded incompetence and punished skill. And that is one of the reasons it was so monstrously hard to find, because the people most likely to cause it were the people least likely to make any obvious mistake while doing it. There was no clumsy operator to blame. There was a skilled professional doing her job well, and a hidden flaw turning her speed against her.

NARRATOR: What this bug is, in the proper technical language, is a race condition. A race condition is when the correctness of your program depends on the precise timing of two things that are happening at the same time, racing each other — and the program quietly assumes one of them will always finish first, when in fact it might not. Here, two things were racing. The operator's editing, happening at human speed on the keyboard, and the machine's internal setup, happening at mechanical speed in the hardware and the software's own bookkeeping. The programmer had silently, invisibly assumed the editing would always finish before the setup committed. Most of the time, it did. But when a fast operator beat the machine to the line, the assumption broke, the state tore in half, and the result was a lethal beam. The programmer never wrote a line of code that said "fire the dangerous beam." The danger emerged from timing — from two correct-looking pieces of code interacting in an order nobody had imagined.

NARRATOR: And there was a second, completely separate bug, which is almost unbelievable in its own right, because it shows the same machine could kill in more than one way. This second one involved a counter — a single number the program kept and added to, over and over, as it ran its safety checks. And that counter was stored in a tiny amount of space, just one byte, which means it could only count up to two hundred and fifty-five before it ran out of room. When you keep adding one to a number that has run out of room, it does not keep growing. It wraps around back to zero, like a car odometer rolling over, or the seconds on a clock ticking from fifty-nine back to zero. This is called an overflow. And in the Therac twenty-five, the safety check the program performed only happened when that counter was sitting at zero. So picture the deadly coincidence. If an operator pressed the button to fire at the exact instant that the counter had just wrapped back around to zero — which happened roughly once every few minutes of continuous use — then a crucial safety check that should have verified the machine was correctly configured was simply skipped. Not because anyone disabled it. Because a one-byte number had quietly rolled over to zero at the wrong moment, and the code only looked when the number was non-zero. Two utterly different bugs — a race condition and a counter overflow — both able to produce the same catastrophe, in the same machine, at the same time. That is what an unguarded, unprotected internal state will do to you.

NARRATOR: And now we come to the part of the story that, for me, is the hardest to hear, because it is about the human beings in the room, and how the machine lied to them.

NARRATOR: When the bug struck and the beam misfired, the patient received an enormous overdose — by some estimates, more than a hundred times the intended dose, a flood of radiation delivered in a heartbeat. But the operator, sitting in the next room behind a shielded wall, could not see the patient clearly, could not hear well through the heavy door, and was relying almost entirely on the machine's screen to tell her what had happened. And what did the screen say? It displayed a short, cryptic message. The most infamous of them was the phrase "Malfunction fifty-four." That was it. Not "danger." Not "massive overdose delivered." Not "stop, check the patient immediately." Just "Malfunction fifty-four" — a code so obscure that it did not even appear, with any useful explanation, in the machine's own manuals. The operators had no idea what it meant. And worse, the screen reported that the machine had delivered little or no dose at all — that the treatment had, in effect, not happened.

NARRATOR: Now put yourself in that operator's chair. These malfunction messages were common. The machine threw cryptic codes constantly, dozens of times a day, for trivial reasons, and the operators had been quietly trained by experience to treat them as routine annoyances — clear it, and carry on. The culture around the machine had normalised its complaints into background noise. So the operator sees "Malfunction fifty-four," sees a reading that says no real dose was given, and reasonably concludes that the machine hiccupped and the patient needs the treatment they came for. So she does exactly what the machine appears to be telling her to do. She presses the button to proceed. And the beam fires again. A second massive overdose, on top of the first. In at least one case, a patient was overdosed not once but multiple times in a single session, because each time, the machine calmly reported that nothing had happened, and invited the operator to try again. The interface did not just fail to warn anyone. It actively, repeatedly invited them to repeat the very thing that was killing the patient.

NARRATOR: This is the part I want you never to forget. The operators were not negligent. They were not careless. They were skilled professionals, trusting the information their instrument gave them, which is exactly what we expect skilled professionals to do. The machine told them everything was fine. They believed it, because they had every reason to, and the machine was the only window they had into what was actually happening. The betrayal here was not human. It was the design — a system that hid a catastrophe behind a cryptic code and a reassuring "nothing happened," and put the people in the room in the impossible position of trusting a machine that was lying to them.

NARRATOR: And then there is the final, awful layer, which is how long it took anyone to believe what was happening at all. The first accidents were isolated, in different hospitals, in different states and provinces, scattered across nineteen eighty-five and into nineteen eighty-six. A woman in Georgia. A man in Texas. Patients in Washington state, in Ontario. Each hospital saw one terrible, inexplicable injury. If you want the dates laid out in order — the older machines, the removal of the interlocks, each accident in turn, and the final recall — I have put the full timeline in Listing one. The patients reported searing pain during treatment — but radiation burns do not appear instantly. The real damage emerged over days and weeks, as the overdosed tissue died, and by then the connection to that one treatment session was easy to miss or dismiss. When the hospitals contacted the manufacturer and asked, directly, whether the machine could possibly have overdosed the patient, the company investigated, could not reproduce the fault, and told them, in effect, no. It is not possible. The machine has multiple safety checks. It cannot have done this. And they believed that, because they had been unable to make the bug happen on demand — which is the signature curse of a race condition. It depends on timing so precise that you cannot reproduce it by trying; it only happens in the wild, when a real operator hits the exact rhythm by accident.

NARRATOR: It took a genuinely heroic piece of detective work to break the case. A physicist named Fritz Hager, at the clinic in Tyler, Texas, where two patients had been catastrophically injured weeks apart, refused to let it go. He sat down with the very operator who had been running the machine during one of the accidents, and together, painstakingly, they tried to recreate exactly what she had done — the same prescription, the same quick correction, the same fast editing on the keyboard. And after many attempts, getting the timing right by sheer persistence, they made the machine fault on command. They could finally trigger "Malfunction fifty-four" deliberately, and measure the beam, and prove that the machine was firing a massive overdose while calmly reporting that it had not. For the first time, the bug was no longer a ghost. It was reproducible. And that reproduction — that one operator and one physicist, patiently re-enacting a tragedy until the machine confessed — is what finally forced the truth into the open. The machines were eventually shut down, investigated properly, and the regulators got involved.

NARRATOR: By the time it was over, at least six patients had received massive radiation overdoses across those two years. Three of them, and by some accounts four, died from their injuries. Others lost the use of limbs, suffered chronic pain, were permanently maimed by a dose of radiation that no human body can withstand. These were people who walked into a clinic for healing and were harmed by the very machine meant to help them. They deserve to be remembered not as data points in a software case study, but as the human cost of decisions made far away, by people who never meant them any harm, and who were, themselves, horrified when the truth came out.

NARRATOR: So that is the story. Now let me do the thing I promised at the start, and pull out the engineering lessons, because this is where the Therac twenty-five stops being history and starts being about the code you yourself will write. Every one of these lessons maps directly onto your course, and I want you to carry them with you, because the whole reason we teach this case is so that the next generation of engineers — that is you — never repeats it.

NARRATOR: The first lesson is the deepest, and it is about the internal state of the machine, and it connects straight to encapsulation, which you study properly in the object-oriented lessons — specifically the one on encapsulation and the protecting of an object's state. Think about what really went wrong at the technical heart of this. The machine had an internal state — its mode, its energy level, the position of its target — and that state was allowed to become inconsistent with itself. It got into a combination that was physically impossible and physically lethal: high power selected, protective target absent, and the machine nonetheless believing it was safe to fire. In object-oriented terms, the machine was allowed to enter an invalid state. And the entire point of encapsulation is to make that impossible. When you encapsulate an object properly, you hide its internal data behind methods, and those methods enforce what we call an invariant — a rule that must always be true about the object, no matter what. The classic teaching example is a bank account whose balance must never go negative: you make the balance private, you force every change to go through a withdraw method, and that method refuses any withdrawal that would break the rule. The object guards its own validity. It is structurally impossible to put it into a bad state, because the only doors into the object are the methods, and the methods will not allow it.

NARRATOR: The Therac twenty-five is the lethal version of a non-negative balance. Its invariant — the rule that must always be true — was: if the beam power is high, the target must be in place. That rule was sacred. It should have been impossible to violate, in the same way it should be impossible to make a bank balance go negative. But the machine's state was effectively mutable and unguarded. Different parts of the program could change the power and the target position independently, with no single method standing guard to insist that the combination always made sense. There was no setter that refused to leave the object in an unsafe state. So when the race condition tore the change in half, nothing caught it, because nothing was responsible for catching it. I have put a small illustration of this in Listing two — the unguarded version, where the fields are just set directly and an invalid combination can sit there silently — next to Listing three, the protected version, where every change runs through a single guarded operation that refuses to ever leave the object unsafe. That contrast — unguarded state versus an invariant-protected setter — is the single most important programming idea in this entire episode. An object should never, ever be allowed into an invalid state. The Therac twenty-five is what happens when it is.

NARRATOR: The second lesson is about quality assurance, and it connects directly to your lessons on testing and the management of software quality — the ones on quality assurance and the independence of testing. Because the bugs were bad, yes. But the deeper failure was that the process which was supposed to catch the bugs did not exist in any serious form. The official investigation that followed found that the Therac twenty-five's software had been developed with almost no rigour at all by the standards we would now demand of safety-critical code. There was, essentially, no independent review of the software — the people who wrote it were trusted to vouch for it, with no separate, sceptical set of eyes checking their work. There was no serious, systematic test plan that tried to attack the software the way a hostile world would. And — this is the killer — the reused code from the older Theracs was trusted without being rigorously re-tested for its new role. The assumption was: it worked before, so it works now. But "it worked before" meant "the hardware interlocks were quietly covering for it before." Reusing code without re-testing it in its new context, where the old safety nets are gone, is a recurring killer in our field, and it killed here.

NARRATOR: This is exactly why your syllabus is so insistent on the independence of testing. The reason testing must be independent — done by someone other than the author — is that the author cannot see their own blind spots. The very assumption that caused the bug is the same assumption that stops the author from testing for it. The programmer who silently assumed the operator's editing would always finish first was never going to write a test for the operator finishing second, because in his mind, that simply did not happen. It takes a different person, with a different, more hostile imagination, to ask the rude question: but what if it does happen? What if the operator is faster than you assumed? Independent testing, and independent code review, are not bureaucratic box-ticking. They are the mechanism by which a fresh, sceptical mind finds the assumption the author could not see. On safety-critical software — software where a bug can kill — they are not optional. They are the difference between a near-miss and a funeral. And the cost of skipping them, the thing your syllabus wants you to be able to discuss, is laid bare here in the most extreme possible terms: the cost of skipping proper quality assurance on this machine was measured in human lives.

NARRATOR: The third lesson is about the specific kind of bug at the centre of this, and it connects to your lessons on testing strategies — particularly grey-box testing and the testing of concurrency and timing. The race condition is not an ordinary bug. You cannot find it by reading a single line of code and going "ah, that is wrong," because no single line is wrong. Each piece, in isolation, looks correct. The bug lives in the interaction, in the timing, in the order things happen relative to one another. And ordinary testing — running the machine normally, the way it is meant to be used, the happy path — will never find it, because on the happy path everything finishes in the expected order and the state stays consistent. Just like Heartbleed survived because the honest, well-behaved test always passed, the Therac bug survived because the calm, careful, slow operation always worked. The danger only appeared at the hostile edge: a fast operator, an unlucky counter value, a precise and rare timing.

NARRATOR: Finding bugs like this is exactly what grey-box and risk-based testing are designed for. Grey-box testing means you test with some knowledge of the internal structure — you know there is shared internal state, you know there is timing involved, you know there are two processes that could race — and you deliberately design tests that attack those specific danger points. You ask: what are the riskiest interactions in this system? Where could two things happen at once and corrupt each other? And then you go hunting there on purpose. A grey-box test plan that knew about the shared state and the timing would have asked, deliberately: what happens if the operator edits the prescription faster than the hardware can move? What happens at the exact moment the counter rolls over? Those are precisely the questions that expose a race condition, and precisely the questions that nobody asked. The general principle your syllabus drills, in a slightly different context, still applies here in spirit — test the behaviour the system must guarantee, not just the implementation you happened to write. The behaviour that had to be guaranteed was "never fire the strong beam without the target." Nobody tested for the violation of that behaviour under adversarial timing, and so nobody found the violation, until it found the patients.

NARRATOR: Let me put a frame around all of this, because it is going to help you in an exam and it helps me remember it. Think back to the four qualities we want a system's data and state to have. You can remember them with the word "icefs" — I-C-F-S — Integrity, Consistency, Flexibility, and Security. The Therac twenty-five failed the first two so completely that it is almost a teaching diagram of what those words mean. Integrity — the data being correct and trustworthy — was gone, because the machine's record of its own state did not match physical reality; it believed the target was in place when it was not. And Consistency — the state agreeing with itself and never contradicting itself — was gone, because the race condition tore the state into two halves that disagreed: the power said one thing, the target said another. When you lose integrity and consistency on a system's state, the system's view of the world becomes a lie, and every decision it makes after that is a decision based on a lie. On most software, that is a bug. On this software, it was a body in a morgue. I have written the safe configuration logic out in exam-style pseudocode too, in Listing four, so you can see the shape of an invariant check the way you would write it for marks. And there is a checklist in Listing five — the things a proper quality process would have done, the questions a grey-box plan would have asked — and every item on it is a thing that, had it been done, would have caught this.

NARRATOR: So let me gather the whole thing into the few sentences I want you to walk away with. The Therac twenty-five moved safety out of hardware and into software, and trusted that software alone, with no physical net behind it. The software had a hidden, unguarded internal state that was allowed to become inconsistent and invalid — the exact failure that encapsulation and invariants exist to prevent. A race condition, triggered by a fast operator, let the powerful beam fire with the protective target absent. A separate counter-overflow bug could skip a safety check entirely. A cryptic, reassuring interface hid the catastrophe and invited operators to repeat it. And underneath all of it sat a development process with no independent review, no rigorous testing, and a fatal trust in reused code that had never really been safe — only protected. People died. And the reason we tell this story, decade after decade, is so that the lesson is paid for only once.

NARRATOR: Now let me turn this into exam marks, because this case connects to a startling number of your syllabus dot-points, and an examiner who hands you a scenario about safety-critical software, or system failure, or the social and ethical weight of software, wants to hear you reach for exactly this material. Here are a few questions in the style the H-S-C would actually ask, and I want you to genuinely attempt each one before I give you the model answer.

QUESTION: First one. Explain how the principle of encapsulation, and the use of invariants on an object's state, could have prevented the Therac twenty-five overdoses. Pause the player now, work out your answer in full sentences, then come back and compare.

NARRATOR: Here is the model answer. Encapsulation is the bundling of an object's data together with the methods that operate on it, and the hiding of that data behind those methods so it cannot be changed directly from outside. An invariant is a rule about the object's state that must always remain true. In the Therac twenty-five, the machine's state — its beam power and its target position — was effectively unguarded: different parts of the program could change those values independently, allowing the object to enter an invalid combination, namely high beam power with the protective target absent. Had the machine's state been properly encapsulated, every change would have been forced through a single controlled method that enforced the invariant "if beam power is high, the target must be in place," and that method would have refused to leave the object in an unsafe state. The invalid, lethal combination would have been structurally impossible to reach, in the same way a well-encapsulated bank account makes a negative balance impossible. The mark-earning move there is naming the terms exactly — encapsulation, invariant, invalid state — and tying each one concretely to the beam and the target, not just defining them in the abstract.

QUESTION: Second one, and this one is broader. The Therac twenty-five is often used to discuss the social, ethical, and legal implications of software development. Discuss the responsibilities of software engineers when building safety-critical systems, using the Therac twenty-five as your example. Pause, attempt it, then play on.

NARRATOR: Here is a strong answer. Software engineers building safety-critical systems — systems where failure can cause injury or death — carry an ethical and legal responsibility that goes far beyond ordinary software. The Therac twenty-five demonstrates the consequences of failing that responsibility. Ethically, the developers removed the hardware interlocks that physically prevented harm and replaced them with software they had not rigorously verified, transferring a life-and-death guarantee onto code without the testing such a transfer demanded. They reused old code on the untested assumption it was safe, when its safety had in fact come from hardware that was now gone. The interface withheld critical information from operators, presenting a cryptic code and a falsely reassuring status that led to patients being overdosed repeatedly. The legal and professional consequence was a regulatory and liability reckoning, the machines being recalled and the field being permanently changed. The engineer's responsibilities therefore include: rigorous, independent testing and code review proportionate to the risk; never trusting reused or third-party code without re-verifying it in its new context; designing interfaces that communicate danger clearly and honestly; and maintaining defensive redundancy so that no single failure can reach a human being unchecked. A weak answer here just says "they should have tested it more." A strong answer names the specific responsibilities and ties each one to a specific failure in the case — that is what lifts it into the top band.

QUESTION: Last one, and it is the testing one. With reference to the Therac twenty-five, explain why independent testing and grey-box testing are essential for safety-critical software, and why ordinary testing was insufficient. Pause, have a real go, then play.

NARRATOR: Here is the model answer. Ordinary testing typically exercises the expected, well-behaved use of a system — the happy path — and confirms it produces correct results. This was insufficient for the Therac twenty-five because its fatal bug was a race condition: a failure that appears only when two concurrent processes — the operator's fast editing and the machine's slower internal setup — interact in a specific, rare timing order. Under normal, careful operation that timing never occurred, so the bug stayed hidden and the manufacturer could not even reproduce it on demand. Independent testing — testing performed by someone other than the code's author — is essential because the author shares the very blind spot that caused the bug; the programmer who assumed the editing would always finish first would never test for it finishing second, whereas an independent tester brings a fresh, sceptical imagination that asks the hostile question. Grey-box testing — testing with knowledge of the internal structure, such as the shared state and the concurrent processes — is essential because it lets testers deliberately target the riskiest interactions: edit faster than the hardware can move, fire at the exact moment a counter overflows. These are precisely the conditions that expose timing and concurrency faults, and precisely what the development process failed to do. The phrase to land is that race conditions and timing faults are invisible to happy-path testing and are exactly what independent, grey-box, risk-based testing exists to find.

NARRATOR: And that is the Therac twenty-five. A machine that was, in the end, simply trusted too much — trusted to guard a hundred-times-too-powerful beam with software that had never been properly checked, with no hardware standing behind it, with a state nothing protected and an interface that hid the truth. Every safe system you will ever build rests on the opposite of all that: defence in depth, so no single failure reaches a person; encapsulated state that cannot be made invalid; independent, sceptical testing that goes hunting for the rude, the rare, and the hostile; and a humble refusal to trust any code, however old and familiar, without checking it again in the place you are now asking it to stand guard. The patients of the Therac twenty-five paid for those lessons. The least we can do is learn them.
