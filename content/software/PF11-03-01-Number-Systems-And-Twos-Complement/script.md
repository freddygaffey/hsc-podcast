NARRATOR: First conversion, decimal to binary, by repeated division. The method the exam marks: divide by two, write down the remainder, carry the quotient, repeat until the quotient is zero — then read the remainders bottom to top. Take forty-five. Forty-five divided by two is twenty-two remainder one. Twenty-two divided by two is eleven remainder zero. Eleven divided by two is five remainder one. Five divided by two is two remainder one. Two divided by two is one remainder zero. One divided by two is zero remainder one. Now read the remainders from the bottom up: one, oh, one, one, oh, one — so forty-five is one-oh-one-one-oh-one in binary. The full working is in Listing 1. And here's trap number one, the one that costs marks: you read the remainders bottom to top, not top to bottom. Get that backwards and every bit is reversed.

NARRATOR: Going the other way, binary to decimal, by positional values — just add up the place values where there's a one. NARRATOR: Now hexadecimal, and here's why it exists for programmers: each hex digit is exactly four binary bits. That's the whole trick. Sixteen is two to the fourth, so one hex digit maps to a group of four bits, no arithmetic needed once you know the sixteen patterns. To convert binary to hex, group the bits into fours from the right, and translate each group. Take one-oh-one-one-oh-one. Group into fours from the right: that's oh-oh-one-oh, and one-one-oh-one. The first group, oh-oh-one-oh, is two. The second, one-one-oh-one, is thirteen, which is D in hex. So forty-five is two-D in hexadecimal. The hook: each hex digit is exactly four bits, and you group from the right. Listing 2 has the grouping worked out and the full hex table. NARRATOR: Now the real content of today: two's complement, how computers represent negative integers. And the reason it exists is elegant — it lets the same addition circuit handle both positive and negative numbers, with no special case for subtraction. The method is the one mnemonic you must own: flip the bits, add one. To represent a negative number: start with the positive value in binary, flip every bit — that's called the one's complement — then add one. Flip the bits, add one. Let me do negative eighteen in eight bits. Positive eighteen in eight bits is oh-oh-oh-one-oh-oh-one-oh. Flip every bit: one-one-one-oh-one-one-oh-one. Add one: one-one-one-oh-one-one-one-oh. So negative eighteen, in eight-bit two's complement, is one-one-one-oh-one-one-one-oh. The full working is in Listing 3. And trap three, a favourite: one's complement is just the flip; two's complement is flip AND add one. Stop at the flip and you've given one's complement, which is wrong and a different thing — the "add one" is not optional.

NARRATOR: Two facts about two's complement earn marks every time. First, the sign bit. The leftmost, most significant bit tells you the sign: zero means positive, one means negative. Leftmost bit is the sign — zero up, one down. You can see it in negative eighteen: it starts with a one, so it's negative. Positive eighteen starts with a zero. Second, the range. For an N-bit two's complement number, the range runs from negative two-to-the-N-minus-one, up to positive two-to-the-N-minus-one minus one. For eight bits that's negative one-twenty-eight to positive one-twenty-seven. Notice the asymmetry — one more negative value than positive. Why? Zero takes up one of the "positive" slots, so the positives only reach one-twenty-seven, while the negatives reach all the way to one-twenty-eight. Eight-bit signed runs negative one-twenty-eight to positive one-twenty-seven — one more negative than positive. That asymmetry is trap four: people write the range as plus-or-minus one-twenty-eight, which is wrong at the top end.

QUESTION: So why does any of this matter to me as a working programmer — isn't it the language's problem?

NARRATOR: Until it isn't. When a value exceeds the range its type can hold, it overflows — and in fixed-width integer types it can silently wrap around from the maximum to the minimum, turning a large positive into a negative. That's not a hypothetical: there's a famous rocket, the Ariane Five, whose maiden flight ended in an explosion seconds after launch because a large number was forced into a sixteen-bit integer that couldn't hold it. QUESTION: Question three. State the range of an eight-bit two's complement integer and explain why it is not symmetric.

NARRATOR: The range is negative one hundred and twenty-eight to positive one hundred and twenty-seven. It is not symmetric because zero is represented using one of the non-negative patterns, so there is one fewer positive value than negative — the positives stop at one-twenty-seven while the negatives reach negative one-twenty-eight. The mark is the explanation: zero occupying a positive slot is why the top end is one-twenty-seven, not one-twenty-eight.

QUESTION: Question four. Explain why computers use two's complement to represent negative numbers, rather than just a sign bit and magnitude.

NARRATOR: Computers use two's complement because it allows the same addition circuitry to perform both addition and subtraction correctly, with no special handling for negative numbers — adding a number and its two's complement negative naturally gives zero, and there is a single representation of zero. A simple sign-and-magnitude scheme, by contrast, needs separate logic for signs and produces two representations of zero, a positive and a negative. The mark-earning points: one arithmetic unit for both operations, and a single zero.
