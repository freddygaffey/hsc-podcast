NARRATOR: On the twenty-ninth of October, two thousand and eighteen, a brand-new aeroplane took off from Jakarta, in Indonesia, into a clear morning sky. It was a Boeing seven-thirty-seven MAX — one of the most advanced, most fuel-efficient airliners in the world, barely months out of the factory. On board were one hundred and eighty-nine people: families, commuters, a honeymooning couple, children. Lion Air Flight six-ten. The take-off was normal. The weather was fine. The crew were trained and rated to fly this aircraft. And within about a minute of leaving the ground, the pilots found themselves in a fight — a fight with their own aeroplane.

NARRATOR: Something was pushing the nose of the plane down. Not once, but again and again. The captain would pull the nose back up, level the aircraft, and a few seconds later, an invisible hand would shove it down again. He pulled up; it pushed down. He pulled up; it pushed down. More than twenty times, in the space of a few minutes, the pilots wrestled an aircraft that seemed determined to dive — and they did not know why, because they did not know that the thing fighting them even existed. Thirteen minutes after take-off, Lion Air Flight six-ten hit the Java Sea at enormous speed. There were no survivors.

NARRATOR: This is the story of that aeroplane, and of a piece of software inside it called M-C-A-S. It is a story about sensors, and feedback, and automatic control — every single idea you have studied in this module. And it is a story about what happens when a system that is built to sense, think, and act is fed a lie by one broken sensor, and trusts that lie all the way into the ground. Before I tell it, I want to be clear about something. Three hundred and forty-six people died in this story, across two crashes. They were real people, with names and lives, and they were failed by decisions made far away, in offices and design reviews, by engineers who never wanted anyone to come to harm. I am going to tell this carefully and accurately, with respect for them, because the whole reason we study it is so that you — the engineers who come next — never build the thing that does this again.

NARRATOR: So let us start where the tragedy actually started, which is not in the cockpit, and not even in the software. It started with a commercial decision about engines.

NARRATOR: The Boeing seven-thirty-seven is one of the most successful aeroplanes ever built. It first flew in the nineteen sixties, and for decades airlines bought it, pilots trained on it, and the whole industry grew up around it. By around two thousand and ten, Boeing wanted to sell a new version — more modern, far more fuel-efficient — to compete with a rival. The cheapest, fastest way to do that was not to design a whole new aircraft, which takes a decade and costs a fortune. It was to take the existing seven-thirty-seven and bolt on bigger, more efficient engines. And, crucially, to keep it classified as the same type of aircraft — so that airlines could fly it without putting their thousands of existing pilots through expensive new training in flight simulators. That was the promise that sold the plane: it's the same seven-thirty-seven you already know. Your pilots barely need any new training. That promise is going to matter enormously.

NARRATOR: But there was a physical problem. The new engines were bigger, and they would not fit under the wing the way the old ones did, so the engineers mounted them further forward and higher up. And that changed the way the aeroplane flew. With the engines in their new position, when the plane pointed its nose up steeply — at what is called a high angle of attack — the engines themselves generated extra lift at the front, and tended to push the nose up even further. On its own, that tendency to pitch up is dangerous, because if the nose goes too high, the wing can stop flying — a stall. And worse, it meant the new plane did not handle quite like the old seven-thirty-seven. Which threatened the whole promise. If it flew differently, the regulators might demand new pilot training, and the commercial advantage would evaporate.

QUESTION: So put yourself in the engineers' position. You have an aeroplane that, in one corner of its flight, pitches up when it should not, and that feels different from the model it is meant to imitate. How do you fix the way it flies — without redesigning the aircraft, and without retraining the pilots?

NARRATOR: You fix it with software. That is what M-C-A-S was — the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System. It was a program, running on the flight computer, with one job: when it detected that the plane was at a dangerously high angle of attack, it would automatically trim the nose back down, smoothing out that pitch-up tendency, so the aircraft would behave like the old, familiar seven-thirty-seven. The pilots would not even need to know it was there. It would sit quietly in the background, sensing the angle of the plane, deciding when to intervene, and acting on the controls to push the nose down. Sense, think, act — an automatic closed-loop control system, exactly like the ones in your control chapter. And in principle, that is a perfectly reasonable thing to build. Aircraft are full of automatic systems that quietly help the pilots. The idea was not the crime. The way it was built was.

NARRATOR: Because here is the design decision at the dead centre of this disaster. To know the plane's angle of attack, M-C-A-S needed a sensor — an angle-of-attack vane, a little blade on the side of the fuselage that measures which way the air is flowing over the plane. Now, the aircraft had two of these sensors, one on each side. Two. But M-C-A-S, as it was originally built, listened to only one of them at a time. It took its reading from a single angle-of-attack sensor, and it did not compare that reading against the other one. There was no cross-check. No second opinion. No voting. One sensor, on one side of the plane, was given the power to trigger a system that could push the nose of the aircraft toward the ground — and nothing was checking whether that sensor was telling the truth.

QUESTION: And you already know, from the data and the sensors episodes, the question that should be screaming at you. What happens if that one sensor is wrong?

NARRATOR: That is exactly what happened. On Lion Air Flight six-ten, one of the angle-of-attack sensors was faulty — it had been replaced before the flight and was miscalibrated, reading roughly twenty degrees off from reality. So as the plane climbed away from Jakarta, perfectly safely, at a normal, gentle angle, that one broken sensor was screaming to the flight computer that the nose was dangerously high. And M-C-A-S believed it. It did exactly what it was designed to do: it saw a high angle of attack — a lie, but it had no way to know that — and it trimmed the nose down to correct it. The pilots pulled back up. M-C-A-S, still reading the lie, waited a few seconds, decided the nose was still too high, and trimmed down again. And again. And again. The system was not malfunctioning, in the narrow sense. It was working perfectly — sensing, thinking, acting — on a measurement that was completely false. It was Sense-Think-Act with a broken Sense.

NARRATOR: And the pilots never had a chance to understand it, because of that promise — the same plane, no new training. M-C-A-S had been kept so quiet, in the name of not alarming anyone or triggering a training requirement, that it was barely mentioned in the manuals. Most seven-thirty-seven MAX pilots in the world did not know the system existed. So imagine the cockpit: a brand-new plane, the nose dropping over and over for no reason you can see, no warning light that says "M-C-A-S is active," nothing in your training that explains it, and the ground coming up. They were fighting an opponent they could not name. There was a way to shut it off — a long-standing procedure for runaway trim, flipping two switches — but to use it you would have to first realise what was happening, and they had been given almost nothing to help them realise it in time. Listing one lays out the full timeline, from that commercial decision through both crashes to the grounding.

NARRATOR: After Lion Air, there was an investigation, and a bulletin went out to airlines and pilots, pointing them toward the runaway-trim procedure. But — and this is the part that is hardest to forgive — the planes kept flying. M-C-A-S itself was not yet fixed. The single-sensor design was still there, in every seven-thirty-seven MAX in the sky. The warning was, in effect: if this happens to you, here is a checklist. Rather than: we have removed the thing that did this.

NARRATOR: And so, a little over four months later, on the tenth of March, two thousand and nineteen, it happened again. Ethiopian Airlines Flight three-oh-two took off from Addis Ababa. Again, a seven-thirty-seven MAX. Again, an angle-of-attack sensor fed a false reading seconds after take-off. Again, M-C-A-S began trimming the nose down, over and over. This time the pilots had at least heard of the problem, and they tried the procedure — but the forces on the controls at that speed were enormous, the situation unfolded in minutes, and they could not save it. Six minutes after take-off, Flight three-oh-two crashed near the town of Bishoftu. All one hundred and fifty-seven people on board were killed.

NARRATOR: Two crashes. The same aircraft. The same software. The same single sensor. The same lie, told twice, four months and a continent apart. After the second crash, the world finally stopped. Within days, aviation regulators across the globe grounded the entire seven-thirty-seven MAX fleet — every single one, worldwide. They stayed on the ground for the better part of two years.

NARRATOR: Three hundred and forty-six people died between those two flights. One hundred and eighty-nine on Lion Air six-ten, one hundred and fifty-seven on Ethiopian three-oh-two. They were passengers and crew from dozens of countries — aid workers, students, a newlywed, a family of five, flight attendants at the start of their careers. They boarded ordinary flights, on a brand-new aircraft, trusting that the most heavily-engineered machine most of us ever step inside had been built to keep them safe. They deserve to be remembered as people, not as a line in a software lecture. And the engineers who designed M-C-A-S were not monsters; many were reportedly devastated when the truth came out. That is part of why this matters. Catastrophes like this are almost never the work of bad people. They are the work of good people, under pressure, making a sequence of decisions each of which seemed defensible at the time, until they added up to something lethal.

NARRATOR: So now let me do what I always do, and pull out the engineering lessons — because every one of them is something you have studied in this module, and every one of them is something you will face, in smaller and less deadly forms, in your own work.

NARRATOR: The first lesson is the big one: redundancy, and the danger of a single point of failure. A safety-critical decision must never depend on one sensor. The aeroplane had two angle-of-attack sensors, and M-C-A-S listened to one. The fix, when it finally came, was exactly what you would now design: compare both sensors, and if they disagree by more than a small margin, do not trust the data — stand down. Listing two shows this in runnable code, side by side. The single-sensor version trusts one reading, and when that reading is a jammed, false value, it confidently commands the nose down. The dual-sensor version compares the two, sees that they disagree wildly — one screaming twenty-five degrees, the other calmly reading two — and refuses to act, because a system that cannot trust its data should do nothing dangerous rather than something dangerous. That is the whole difference between the plane that crashed and the plane that flies today, in about ten lines of code.

QUESTION: Pause the player and think about it the way an engineer should. M-C-A-S was a closed-loop control system, correcting the aircraft toward what it measured. In one sentence, why did that make a faulty sensor so catastrophic?

NARRATOR: Because a closed-loop controller acts on its measurement — it does exactly what you learned: measure, compare, correct. Feed it a false measurement, and it will correct with total confidence toward the wrong thing. The better the controller, the more decisively it drives the aircraft toward disaster, because it genuinely believes it is fixing a real problem. A control system is only ever as trustworthy as the sensor feeding it — which is why, in the data episode, validating and sanity-checking a reading was not bureaucratic caution. It was this. The whole tragedy is the gap between what the plane was doing and what the plane believed it was doing, and a single unchecked sensor is what opened that gap.

NARRATOR: The second lesson is about autonomy, and the human. M-C-A-S was an autonomous system — it decided and acted with no human approval, repeatedly, and it was difficult to override. And the people it was acting on were not told it existed. Think back to the autonomy episode: a good autonomous system needs a fail-safe, an easy override, and a human kept meaningfully in the loop. M-C-A-S had a weak version of all three. It had no fail-safe for bad data — it never asked "do my sensors even agree?" It was hard to override in the heat of the moment. And the human was not just left out of the loop; the human was kept in the dark. Autonomy that overrides a person silently, on bad data, with no easy way to wrestle back control, is not a help. It is a hazard. And the disclosure failure connects straight to the accessibility and design episode: you design with the people who use the system, honestly, not around them.

NARRATOR: The third lesson is testing — specifically, testing the failure paths. Somewhere in the development of this system, the question "what happens if one angle-of-attack sensor fails?" needed to be a tested case, run in simulation, long before anyone boarded. You learned this in the unit-testing episode: you test the boundary and the faulty inputs, not just the happy path, and you do it in simulation where failure is free. A single unit test — feed the controller one good sensor and one wildly wrong one, and assert that it refuses to fire — is the test that, conceptually, was missing. That test is essentially Listing two. It is sobering how small the missing safeguard was.

NARRATOR: And the fourth lesson is the one that connects this story to the Therac-twenty-five, the radiation machine from your earlier case study. In both, a commercial or schedule pressure quietly overruled safety engineering. Therac removed hardware interlocks to save cost and trusted software alone. The seven-thirty-seven MAX shaped a safety-critical system around the commercial need to avoid pilot retraining, and let that need keep the system hidden and single-sourced. When "cheaper" or "faster" or "don't alarm the customer" is allowed to quietly outrank "safe," in a system that can kill, the result is not a bad quarter. It is a body count. As the engineer in the room, that pressure will land on you one day, in some smaller form, and your job will be to be the person who says: not on this system, not where lives are at stake.

NARRATOR: The seven-thirty-seven MAX flies again today. M-C-A-S was rebuilt to read both sensors and to stand down when they disagree, to act only once rather than over and over, to have its authority limited, and to be something pilots are actually trained on. In other words, it was rebuilt to respect every principle this module has tried to teach you: redundant sensing, trustworthy data, a fail-safe, an override, an honest relationship with the human operator. It took the grounding of an entire global fleet, billions of dollars, and most of all the deaths of three hundred and forty-six people to force those changes — changes that, on paper, a careful engineering review should have demanded before the first flight. Listing three maps each of these lessons back to the lessons of this module.

NARRATOR: That is why we study this. Not to frighten you, and not to make you cynical about engineering, because engineering is one of the great forces for good in the world. We study it because you are going to build systems that sense the world and act on it. Most of them will be small. Some of them, one day, might not be. And when you wire a system to a sensor and let it act on what that sensor says, I want a quiet alarm to go off in your head — the memory of a brand-new aeroplane over the Java Sea, fighting its own pilots, because one little blade on the side of the fuselage was telling it a lie, and nobody had built it to ask for a second opinion. Sense, think, act — and never, ever trust a single sensor with a life. That is the lesson the three hundred and forty-six paid for. The least we can do is learn it.
