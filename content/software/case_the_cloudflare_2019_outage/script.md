NARRATOR: At about twenty to two in the afternoon, London time, on the second of July, two thousand and nineteen, a very large slice of the internet simply stopped working. Not one website — thousands upon thousands of them, all at once. People trying to read the news, log into services, shop, or work were met with the same blunt grey message: error five-oh-two, bad gateway. It looked, from the outside, like half the web had fallen over simultaneously. There was no hack. No power cut. No flood of attacking traffic. The cause was a single line of pattern-matching text — one regular expression — that a company had pushed out to its network minutes earlier. And it teaches one of the most important and least appreciated lessons in all of programming: that an algorithm can be correct, and pass every test of whether it gives the right answer, and still be a catastrophe — because of how long it takes to run.

NARRATOR: This is the story of the Cloudflare outage of twenty nineteen. It is a perfect Year-Twelve web case study, because it sits right at the junction of three things you study in this module — web architecture and how much of the web funnels through shared infrastructure; performance, and why the speed of code is a feature in its own right; and deployment, how you push a change out to the world without taking the world down. So let me tell you how one regular expression melted a huge slice of the web in under half an hour.

NARRATOR: First, who Cloudflare are, because it matters enormously. Cloudflare is one of those companies most people have never heard of, and yet a startling fraction of all web traffic passes through them. They sit in front of millions of websites as a kind of shared protective layer — speeding sites up by caching their content close to users, and shielding them from attacks. Part of that shield is a Web Application Firewall — a firewall for web requests — which inspects incoming traffic looking for anything malicious, like an attempt to inject hostile code into a site. And the way it looks for malicious patterns is, very often, with regular expressions: those compact little patterns you write to describe and match text. So picture the architecture here: an enormous amount of the world's web traffic flowing through one shared layer, and inside that layer, a set of regular expressions inspecting every single request. Hold that picture, because the architecture is half the story.

NARRATOR: On that July afternoon, Cloudflare deployed a new firewall rule, intended to better catch malicious JavaScript. It was a regular expression. And buried in that pattern was a structure with a property called catastrophic backtracking. Let me explain what that is, because it's the technical heart of this, and it's beautiful in a horrifying way. Listing one lays out the timeline of that afternoon.

NARRATOR: When a regular expression tries to match some text, the engine works through the possibilities. For most patterns, that's quick and proportional to the length of the text. But some patterns — typically ones with nested, overlapping wildcards, a "match any amount of anything" inside another "match any amount of anything" — create an explosion of possible ways to attempt the match. On text that ultimately doesn't match, the engine doesn't give up quickly; it tries every single one of those combinations before admitting defeat. And the number of combinations can roughly double for every extra character of input. That is catastrophic backtracking: a regular expression whose running time grows exponentially with the length of the input.

QUESTION: So pause and think about why that is so dangerous, and so sneaky. If a pattern works fine on short test strings, what happens when it meets real, longer input — and why would testing never have caught it?

NARRATOR: It sails straight through testing, because on the short, well-behaved strings you test with, the explosion hasn't kicked in yet — it's fast, it gives the right answer, it looks perfect. Listing two shows the trap in miniature: a model of a backtracking pattern where the work doubles per character. At five characters it's thirty-two attempts — instant, invisible. But at thirty characters it's over a billion attempts, while a well-behaved engine would do thirty. Same pattern, same kind of input, but as the input grew a little, the cost exploded by a factor of tens of millions. Now remember the architecture: this pattern was being run against real web traffic — long, messy, real requests — on machines handling an enormous share of the web. The moment that real traffic hit the explosive pattern, the processors went to a hundred percent, pegged completely, burning all their time backtracking through a regular expression. And a processor pinned at a hundred percent on a firewall pattern has nothing left to actually serve web pages. So the requests stopped being answered. Five-oh-two, bad gateway, across a huge slice of the web.

NARRATOR: And here is the second half of the disaster, which is just as important as the regex itself: how it was deployed. That rule was pushed out globally — to the entire network, everywhere in the world, all at once. So the instant it was bad, it was bad everywhere, simultaneously. There was no sliver of the network running it first while the rest stayed safe. The blast radius was the whole thing. What ended the outage, about twenty-seven minutes later, was that Cloudflare had a global kill switch — a way to rapidly turn the whole firewall off — and once they identified the firewall as the culprit, they hit it, the processors recovered, and the web came back. Listing three lays out the lessons, and there are four, each landing squarely on this module.

NARRATOR: The first lesson: the time complexity of your code is a real risk, not a footnote. We spend a lot of effort asking "does this code give the right answer?" — and far too little asking "how long does it take in the worst case?" This is the Big-O thinking you've met: an algorithm that is order-n is fine; one that is exponential is a landmine, even if it is perfectly correct. A regular expression is code, and a badly-shaped one can be exponential. Correctness was never the problem here — the pattern matched exactly what it was supposed to. The performance was the problem. When you write or review code, especially anything that runs on every request at scale, you have to think about its worst-case cost, not just its output.

NARRATOR: The second lesson is about deployment and blast radius, and it connects to both web architecture and the engineering project module. Pushing a change to your entire system at once means a single fault becomes a total fault, instantly. The discipline that prevents this is staged rollout — you deploy the change to a small fraction first, a sliver of the traffic, watch what happens, and only widen it once you're confident. Had that rule gone to one percent of the network first, the processors on that one percent would have spiked, alarms would have fired, and the rule would have been pulled having harmed almost no one. Big-bang deployment to everything at once is fast and convenient right up until the moment it's a global catastrophe. Deploy gradually; contain the blast radius.

NARRATOR: The third lesson: budget for performance, and keep a kill switch. New code that runs at scale should be checked against a limit on how much processor time it may consume — and rejected or capped if it blows the budget — both before and during rollout. And you should always have a fast, reliable way to turn a change off. Notice that the outage didn't end when they fixed the regex; it ended when they disabled the firewall with the kill switch. The ability to rapidly undo a deployment is itself a critical feature. The fix comes later; the off-switch is what saves you in the moment.

NARRATOR: And the fourth lesson is the architectural one, and it's a genuine trade-off with no clean answer. Putting a huge fraction of the web behind one shared, fast, secure layer is genuinely good — it makes millions of sites faster and safer than they could ever be alone. But that same concentration means a single fault in that layer has staggering reach. Efficiency and blast radius pull against each other: the more you centralise for performance, the more a single failure can take down. That's the same shape as the story of one DNS provider going down and dragging half the web with it — concentration buys you speed and costs you fragility. There's no villain in that trade-off, just an engineering decision you have to make consciously, with your eyes open to both sides.

NARRATOR: So what do you carry away from twenty-seven minutes of grey error pages? That a program can be perfectly correct and still bring down the internet, because correctness and performance are two different questions and you must ask both. That an algorithm's worst-case running time — its Big-O — is not academic; it is the difference between a firewall and an outage. That how you deploy a change matters as much as the change itself: stage it, watch it, contain its blast radius, and keep a switch to kill it. And that the architectures that make the modern web fast — great shared layers that millions of sites lean on — are exactly the architectures where a single regular expression can melt a huge slice of the web. No one was hurt here, and the web came back in under half an hour. But the next time you write a clever pattern, or push a change to everything at once, remember the second of July, twenty nineteen — and ask not just "is it right?", but "how slow could it get, and what happens if I'm wrong everywhere at once?"
